Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); ACIT Vs Babu Lal Data (ITAT Jaipur) Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT), Jaipur Bench, in ACIT Vs Shri Babu Lal Data, decided four cross-appeals filed by the Revenue and the assessee relating to Assessment Years 2015-16 and 2016-17, arising from assessments following a search and seizure operation under Section 132 conducted on 14 October 2015 in the Data Group of Alwar. The principal disputes concerned additions under Section 68 of the Income Tax Act, 1961, agricultural income claimed as exempt under Section 10(1), unexplained jewellery, alleged unaccounted sales of mustard seeds and mustard oil, disallowance of cash purchases under Section 40A(3), unexplained receipts and notional interest income. The Assessing Officer had treated cash receipts of Rs. 2,75,00,000 for AY 2015-16 and Rs. 1,68,00,000 for AY 2016-17, aggregating to Rs. 4.43 crore, as unexplained cash loans despite the assessee’s contention that they represented advances received against the proposed sale of agricultural land. The Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) deleted these additions, leading to appeals by the Revenue. The assessee explained that the amounts were recorded in seized documents, particularly page 71A of Exhibit AS-1, which contained details of transactions relating to agricultural land. The seized material recorded the dates, amounts and names of persons connected with the transactions. According to the assessee, the advances were received through brokers against the proposed sale of agricultural land belonging to him and his family members. The sale deeds could not be executed because of pending family disputes concerning the properties. The Assessing Officer rejected the explanation on the grounds that the assessee had not maintained regular books of account, failed to furnish sale agreements, and could not provide complete addresses, PAN details, bank statements and other supporting documents of the persons from whom the advances were allegedly received. The amounts were accordingly treated as unexplained cash credits under Section 68. The assessee contended that the seized material itself established the nature of the receipts and that the statutory requirements for invoking Section 68 were not satisfied where no regular books of account were maintained. The Tribunal upheld the deletion of the additions of Rs. 2.75 crore and Rs. 1.68 crore. It observed that the seized documents clearly reflected the nature of the transactions as advances against the proposed sale of agricultural land. The Tribunal considered that the documents recorded relevant particulars of the receipts and that the Department could not disregard their contents merely because additional supporting documents were unavailable. Referring to the evidentiary significance of seized material under Section 292C, the Tribunal held that the explanation furnished by the assessee could not be rejected without contrary evidence establishing that the transactions were different from those reflected in the seized records. The Tribunal further noted that the advances relating to AY 2015-16 had been received before 1 June 2015, when cash advances concerning immovable property were brought within the relevant restrictions under Section 269SS. Since no transfer of land or possession had taken place, the advances were not taxable receipts in the circumstances examined. The Tribunal also observed that, even if treated as loans or advances, their capital nature did not establish taxable income. Consequently, the Revenue’s challenge to the deletion of the additions under Section 68 was rejected. Another significant dispute concerned the assessee’s agricultural income. For AY 2015-16, the assessee had disclosed agricultural income of Rs. 12,46,718, which the Assessing Officer treated as income from other sources. The Assessing Officer relied partly on the assessee’s statement under Section 132(4), in which an ad hoc surrender of Rs. 2.01 crore had been made during search proceedings. The assessee subsequently explained that the surrender was made without proper reconciliation of the seized documents and that the actual additional income worked out after verification was Rs. 1,12,92,817, including agricultural income relating to different assessment years. The Assessing Officer rejected the claim of agricultural income for want of satisfactory supporting evidence, and the CIT(A) sustained the addition. Before the Tribunal, the assessee relied upon agricultural land records, Khasra Girdawari, income and expenditure accounts, seized bills and supporting documents concerning agricultural activities and sale of produce. The Tribunal found that substantial agricultural land measuring approximately 230 bighas was available and that the assessee had placed evidence of agricultural activities and earnings on record. It observed that the claimed agricultural income was supported by the extent of landholding, accounts and seized documents. The Tribunal also considered that the CIT(A) had not denied the existence of income but had refused to accept its agricultural character. Having examined the evidence, the Tribunal concluded that agricultural income of Rs. 12,46,718 for AY 2015-16 could not be treated as unproved or non-genuine. The assessee’s grounds were accordingly allowed. Applying the same reasoning to AY 2016-17, the Tribunal also accepted the assessee’s claim concerning agricultural income of Rs. 3,41,513. It further dismissed the Revenue’s challenge to the CIT(A)’s deletion of an addition of Rs. 6,02,275 relating to agricultural income and unexplained cash. For AY 2016-17, the Revenue also challenged deletion of an addition of Rs. 25,34,587 relating to allegedly unexplained jewellery discovered during the search. The Assessing Officer had examined jewellery and valuables found at the residence and bank locker of the assessee and his family members and treated part of the holdings as unexplained. The assessee submitted that the jewellery belonged collectively to various family members and that substantial holdings had already been disclosed in wealth-tax returns. Reliance was placed on CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11 May 1994 and judicial precedents recognising customary jewellery holdings having regard to family size, social status and traditional practices. The Tribunal noted that jewellery holdings had been reconciled with wealth-tax records and family ownership details, while no incriminating material established unexplained purchases. The Tribunal upheld the CIT(A)’s deletion of the addition, observing that the Revenue had not produced material sufficient to displace the factual findings. The Tribunal’s consideration was consistent with the principles discussed in Ashok Chaddha Vs ITO and Vibhu Aggarwal Vs DCIT, which were relied upon by the assessee. The Tribunal also examined additions arising from alleged unaccounted sales of mustard seeds and mustard oil amounting to Rs. 1,34,76,825. The Assessing Officer had treated the entire sales amount as undisclosed income, while the CIT(A) restricted the addition to gross profit estimated at 10%, amounting to Rs. 13,47,683. The assessee contended that only profit attributable to unaccounted sales could be brought to tax and relied upon comparable businesses showing gross profit margins ranging from approximately 3.25% to 4.56%. The Revenue sought to sustain a higher estimate. The Tribunal referred to judicial principles recognised in CIT Vs President Industries and the decision in ITO Vs Gurubachan Singh J. Juneja, holding that the entire sale proceeds could not be treated as income in the absence of evidence establishing unexplained investment corresponding to those sales. Considering the comparable gross profit rates placed on record, the Tribunal held that an estimated gross profit rate of 5% would be fair and reasonable instead of 10% adopted by the CIT(A). The Tribunal therefore granted partial relief to the assessee by reducing the estimated profit addition. On the connected issue of cash purchases amounting to approximately Rs. 1.95 crore, the Assessing Officer had invoked Section 40A(3) on the ground that the purchases were made in cash. The CIT(A) deleted the disallowance because income had already been estimated by applying a gross profit rate. The Tribunal upheld this finding, relying upon the principle recognised in CIT Vs Banwari Lal Bansidhar that once taxable business income is determined through gross profit estimation, a separate disallowance relating to the same purchases is not warranted. The Tribunal also sustained deletion of an addition of Rs. 1,18,63,959 relating to alleged unexplained cash credits, accepting the explanation that the entries included amounts received back against earlier advances and that the Revenue’s computation involved repeated entries. It further confirmed deletion of notional interest income of Rs. 14,23,639 because the Assessing Officer had estimated interest at 12% without evidence that such interest had actually been received. The Tribunal emphasised that an assessment must be based on evidence rather than assumptions or conjectures. In the final result, the ITAT Jaipur dismissed the Revenue’s appeal in ITA No. 1231/JP/2019 for AY 2015-16, partly allowed the Revenue’s appeal in ITA No. 1232/JP/2019 for AY 2016-17, partly allowed the assessee’s appeal in ITA No. 1223/JP/2019 for AY 2016-17, and allowed the assessee’s appeal in ITA No. 1222/JP/2019 for AY 2015-16. The decision addresses the evidentiary treatment of seized loose papers, the conditions for invoking Section 68, the taxation of advances against agricultural land, proof of agricultural income, unexplained family jewellery, estimation of profits on undisclosed sales, restrictions on separate disallowances under Section 40A(3), and the impermissibility of taxing hypothetical interest income without supporting evidence.

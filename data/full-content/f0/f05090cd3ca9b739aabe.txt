Summary: An Indian resident promoter giving a personal guarantee for a UAE company may incur a regulated non-fund-based financial commitment before any payment or default. The Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules and Regulations, 2022, permit specified guarantees by resident individual promoters of Indian entities that have made overseas direct investment and acquired control of the foreign entity or qualifying step-down subsidiary. Such guarantees must not be open-ended and generally count against the investing Indian entity’s financial-commitment limit. The article examines FEMA residential status, guarantee caps and validity, the distinction between financial and performance guarantees, joint guarantees, foreign-currency valuation, reporting through the designated authorised dealer bank and Form FC, and the consequences of renewal or invocation. It also considers the Foreign Exchange Management (Guarantees) Regulations, 2026, which exclude guarantees validly issued under the overseas-investment regime but impose a separate framework on qualifying other cross-border guarantees. Additional considerations include security over Indian assets, prescribed no-objection certificates, transfer-pricing exposure, accounting implications, place of effective management and documentation before signing a UAE lender’s guarantee form. The bank is satisfied with the business plan but asks the shareholder to sign a personal guarantee. The document may appear to be a routine banking form. No money is being transferred from India, and the Indian promoter may expect that FEMA becomes relevant only if the UAE company defaults. That conclusion is unsafe. A personal guarantee given by a person resident in India for a UAE company can constitute a non-fund-based financial commitment from the date it is issued. Its amount may have to be counted within the financial-commitment limit of the Indian entity that invested in the UAE company. The guarantee must also satisfy eligibility, control, validity and reporting conditions. The guarantee should therefore be reviewed before it is signed—not when the Dubai bank invokes it. Why UAE banks request personal guarantees New and closely held UAE companies frequently have a limited operating history, modest paid-up capital and few assets available as security. A bank, landlord, supplier or finance provider may therefore ask the owner to stand behind the company’s obligations. A personal guarantee may be requested for: A UAE corporate bank facility; Working-capital finance; Trade-finance limits; Vehicle or equipment finance; Commercial premises; Supplier credit; Customs or logistics arrangements; Credit-card facilities; Performance obligations; or Borrowing by a UAE subsidiary. From the lender’s perspective, the guarantee provides access to the promoter’s personal resources if the UAE company fails to perform. From the Indian promoter’s perspective, however, the guarantee creates a cross-border contingent obligation. FEMA applies to the substance of that obligation even if the guarantee is signed in Dubai and no immediate remittance is made from India. A guarantee is a financial commitment even before payment A guarantee is commonly misunderstood as a document that becomes relevant only after default. Under the overseas-investment framework, a permitted guarantee is generally treated as a non-fund-based financial commitment when it is issued. The commitment exists because the guarantor has accepted a legal obligation to pay if the UAE company defaults. Accordingly, the promoter should not assume that: No FEMA reporting is required because no money has moved; The guarantee is outside Indian law because it is governed by UAE law; Signing through a UAE bank avoids Indian reporting; A guarantee given without charging a fee has no regulatory value; or Disclosure is required only after invocation. The relevant date for compliance can arise when the guarantee is executed, renewed, increased or otherwise modified. Which FEMA framework applies? A personal guarantee connected with an Indian entity’s Overseas Direct Investment is principally examined under the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, 2022, the Overseas Investment Regulations, 2022 and the RBI’s directions. India also introduced the Foreign Exchange Management (Guarantees) Regulations, 2026 for guarantees involving a person resident outside India. However, a guarantee validly issued under the Overseas Investment Regulations is specifically kept outside the general 2026 guarantee framework. This distinction matters. Where the personal guarantee meets all conditions of the overseas-investment regime, it should be reported and managed under that regime. Where it does not fall within the permitted ODI guarantee route, the parties cannot automatically treat it as allowable. The 2026 guarantee rules, borrowing and lending conditions or RBI approval requirements may need separate examination. The classification should therefore be settled before execution. Who can give a personal guarantee under the ODI framework? The Overseas Investment Regulations recognise a personal guarantee given by a resident individual promoter of an Indian entity. The guarantee may be issued to or on behalf of: The foreign entity in which the Indian entity has made ODI; or An overseas step-down subsidiary in which the Indian entity has acquired control through the foreign entity. Several conditions are embedded in this permission. The person signing must be a resident individual promoter of the relevant Indian entity. The Indian entity must have made ODI in the UAE company and must have acquired control when the non-fund-based commitment is made. The Indian entity must also form part of the relevant promoter group. A person does not become an eligible guarantor merely because they are: An Indian citizen; A shareholder of the UAE company; A director of the Indian company; A partner in an Indian firm; Related to the owners; Authorised to operate the UAE bank account; or Willing to accept personal liability. The person’s FEMA residential status, promoter position and relationship with the Indian investing entity must be established. FEMA residence is more important than nationality The rules apply to a person resident in India. Citizenship alone does not settle the question. An Indian citizen genuinely residing and working in Dubai may be a person resident outside India for FEMA purposes. Conversely, a person holding a UAE residence visa may remain a person resident in India if their actual circumstances do not support a change in FEMA residence. Before signing, the individual should document: Their residential status; Purpose and duration of stay outside India; Employment or business arrangements; Indian and UAE residential connections; Immigration records; and The circumstances in which the guarantee is being given. The same guarantee can produce a different FEMA analysis depending on whether the guarantor is resident in India or resident outside India. Control over the UAE company is essential An Indian entity may ordinarily extend debt or non-fund-based financial commitment to a foreign entity only after making ODI and acquiring control in that entity. Control is not restricted to owning more than 50% of the shares. It can arise from the right to appoint a majority of directors or influence management or policy decisions through shareholding, management rights, voting arrangements or shareholders’ agreements. Suppose an Indian company owns 30% of a Dubai joint venture but holds contractual rights over budgeting, financing and management appointments. The company may have control even without majority ownership. The opposite can also occur. An Indian entity may hold a sizeable economic interest but lack the contractual rights necessary to establish control. The shareholding documents and shareholders’ agreement must be reviewed before the promoter’s guarantee is treated as permitted financial commitment. A resident individual shareholder cannot automatically guarantee their own UAE company A separate issue arises when an Indian resident makes ODI in a UAE company in their personal capacity rather than through an Indian company, LLP or registered partnership firm. Resident individuals are permitted to make specified overseas investments, but the framework does not provide them with the same freedom as Indian entities to extend debt and non-fund-based financial commitments. A resident individual shareholder should therefore not assume that personal ownership of a Dubai company automatically permits them to guarantee its bank borrowing. The promoter-guarantee provision is specifically connected with a resident individual promoter of an Indian entity that has made ODI and acquired control in the foreign entity. Where the UAE company is owned directly by an Indian resident individual, any proposed guarantee requires separate examination. The individual should obtain a written position from the authorised dealer bank before signing the UAE facility documents. The guarantee must not be open-ended One of the most important conditions is that the guarantee cannot be open-ended. In practical terms, it should contain: A clearly stated maximum amount; An identifiable underlying facility or obligation; A defined validity period; Conditions governing invocation; A termination or expiry mechanism; and Clear provisions for renewal or extension. This condition often conflicts with standard UAE banking language. A Dubai bank may provide an “all monies” or “continuing guarantee” covering every present and future obligation of the company without a fixed maximum exposure or definite expiry. Signing such wording without modification can create a serious FEMA concern. The fact that unlimited guarantees are commonly used in commercial banking does not override the Indian restriction. The promoter should negotiate a monetary cap and a definite validity period before execution. Guarantees and indemnities must be examined by substance A document may be called an indemnity, undertaking, comfort letter, sponsor support agreement or debt-service commitment rather than a guarantee. Its title is not decisive. If the document requires the Indian resident to discharge the UAE company’s debt or obligation following default, it may be treated as a guarantee or financial commitment. Particular care is required where the document contains language such as: Irrevocable and unconditional payment; Primary obligor rather than merely guarantor; Continuing security for all liabilities; Payment on first demand; Waiver of defences; Obligation to maintain the company’s solvency; Commitment to provide funds whenever required; or Indemnity against every loss arising from the facility. A short “support letter” can create greater liability than a formally capped guarantee if its wording imposes an enforceable funding obligation. How the personal guarantee affects the Indian entity’s limit A personal guarantee given by the resident individual promoter is counted towards the financial-commitment limit of the Indian entity. It is not counted under a separate personal allowance of the promoter. The Indian entity’s financial commitment generally includes its aggregate exposure through: Equity investment; Eligible debt funding; Corporate guarantees; Personal guarantees by resident promoters; Bank guarantees supported by its counter-guarantee or collateral; Deferred consideration; Pledges and charges counted under the rules; and Other permitted non-fund-based commitments. The overall automatic-route limit is ordinarily linked to the Indian entity’s net worth based on its last audited balance sheet. The available capacity must be checked on the date of giving the guarantee. For example, assume an Indian company has already invested substantial equity in its Dubai subsidiary and provided it with a loan. If the promoter then signs an AED-denominated personal guarantee for the subsidiary’s bank facility, the guarantee amount may consume the remaining financial-commitment capacity of the Indian company. The promoter’s personal wealth does not increase the Indian entity’s regulatory limit. How much of the guarantee is counted? An ordinary financial guarantee is generally counted for its stated amount. A performance guarantee receives different treatment: 50% of its amount is generally considered when calculating the financial-commitment limit. The distinction depends on the underlying obligation rather than the label placed on the document. A guarantee covering repayment of a loan is ordinarily financial in nature. A guarantee securing completion of a contract, delivery of goods or performance of services may qualify as a performance guarantee if its terms genuinely support a performance obligation. A document covering both payment and performance may require a more careful allocation or conservative treatment. Where two or more Indian entities give a joint and several guarantee, 100% of the guarantee amount is generally counted against the individual limit of each Indian entity. The exposure should not simply be divided between them unless the documents create clearly separate and limited obligations. Currency conversion must be handled consistently A UAE facility and related guarantee will usually be denominated in dirhams or another foreign currency. The Indian entity must determine the INR equivalent when calculating its financial commitment. Exchange-rate movements can make a previously comfortable limit tighter, particularly where the facility is increased, renewed or replaced. The firm should maintain a working showing: Guarantee amount and currency; Conversion rate applied; Existing equity and debt commitment; Other guarantees and security; Total commitment after the proposed guarantee; Net worth under the latest eligible audited balance sheet; and Remaining headroom. This calculation should be certified or reviewed before the guarantee is submitted to the UAE lender. The guarantee must be reported through the designated AD bank A personal guarantee covered by the overseas-investment framework must be routed through the designated Authorised Dealer Category-I bank responsible for the UAE investment. The Indian entity—not merely the individual promoter—must ensure that the guarantee is appropriately reported as its financial commitment. Form FC and supporting documents may be required. The bank is likely to ask for: Executed or proposed guarantee; UAE facility agreement; Details of the lender; Purpose and amount of the facility; Guarantee amount and validity; Indian entity’s audited financial statements; Financial-commitment computation; Proof of ODI and UIN; Shareholding structure; Evidence of control over the UAE entity; Corporate approval from the Indian entity; Details establishing the guarantor’s promoter status; UAE company’s license and constitutional documents; and Confirmation that the underlying activity is permitted. The designated bank should be consulted before the document is signed. Reporting an already executed guarantee is more difficult than reviewing the structure in advance. The 2026 guarantee-reporting rules require separate attention The Foreign Exchange Management (Guarantees) Regulations, 2026 introduced comprehensive reporting for guarantees covered by those regulations. Issuance, changes in terms and invocation are generally reportable quarterly in the prescribed guarantee format. A guarantee issued in accordance with the Overseas Investment Regulations is excluded from that general regime because it follows the ODI reporting route. The parties should not report blindly under both frameworks or assume that neither applies. They must first determine whether the guarantee validly falls within the overseas-investment exemption. A guarantee outside the ODI framework may need to satisfy the 2026 guarantee rules, including the condition that the parties are eligible to lend and borrow from each other under the applicable FEMA borrowing and lending provisions. Renewal and increase are not the same A guarantee may be renewed without being treated as fresh financial commitment where the renewed amount does not exceed the original guarantee. The rollover must nevertheless be reported in Form FC. If the renewed document increases the maximum liability, extends the guarantee to additional facilities or adds another borrower, the additional exposure requires fresh analysis. A bank may describe a document as a “renewal” even though it materially changes: The guaranteed amount; Scope of liabilities; Currency; Borrower; Lender; Security package; Expiry date; or Circumstances of invocation. The Indian entity should compare the old and new wording rather than relying on the document title. What happens when the guarantee is invoked? Invocation converts a contingent obligation into an actual payment liability. To the extent invoked, the amount ceases to be treated as non-fund-based commitment and is considered financial commitment by way of debt. The invocation must be reported through Form FC. The Indian entity and promoter should immediately coordinate with the authorised dealer bank regarding: Notice of default; Amount invoked; Supporting facility documents; Payment route; Conversion of the exposure into debt; Recovery rights against the UAE company; Accounting treatment; Tax consequences; and Any restructuring or enforcement action. A compliant guarantee should not be paid through an informal personal transfer or an unrelated bank account. The payment must follow the route accepted by the authorised dealer bank. Invocation of a properly issued performance guarantee does not ordinarily require separate prior RBI approval merely because funds must be remitted. This does not remove the reporting and documentation requirements. A guarantee can affect future investment capacity Even when the UAE company performs well and default appears unlikely, the guarantee occupies financial-commitment capacity. This can affect the Indian entity’s ability to: Inject additional equity; Provide shareholder loans; Support another overseas subsidiary; Issue further guarantees; Acquire another foreign company; or Restructure its international operations. A company should therefore avoid providing a guarantee materially larger than the UAE facility actually requires. It should also obtain written release when the facility is repaid. Allowing an expired or unused facility to remain supported by a continuing guarantee may unnecessarily restrict future transactions. Security over Indian assets creates another layer of compliance A UAE lender may ask the promoter not only for a personal guarantee but also for security over assets in India. The guarantee and the security are separate commitments. Creating a mortgage, pledge, charge or other security over Indian assets for a UAE company’s facility must comply with the provisions governing financial commitment by way of pledge or charge. Its value or the amount of the supported facility may be counted within the financial-commitment limit, subject to the applicable rules. The parties must examine: Nature and ownership of the Indian asset; Identity and jurisdiction of the overseas lender; Value of the security; Amount of the facility; Enforcement mechanism; Indian registration requirements; and Restrictions on transferring the asset following enforcement. A personal guarantee should not be treated as automatic permission to charge the promoter’s Indian property. NPA, default and investigation cases Additional restrictions apply where the relevant person: Has an account classified as a non-performing asset; Is classified as a wilful defaulter; or Is under investigation by a specified regulator or investigative agency. A no-objection certificate may be required from the lender bank, regulator or investigative agency before fresh financial commitment is undertaken. The company and promoter should complete this check before making any representation to the UAE lender that the guarantee can be issued immediately. If a guarantee was validly issued before the investigation or adverse classification began, honouring an existing contractual obligation may receive different treatment from undertaking a fresh guarantee. The circumstances should still be discussed with the authorised dealer bank. UAE documentation should not be signed as a formality Dubai banks frequently provide standard guarantee wording near the final stage of account or facility approval. Promoters may sign quickly because business funds are urgently required. At minimum, the document should be reviewed for: Maximum guaranteed amount; Expiry date; Governing law; Place of enforcement; Joint and several liability; All-monies language; Future and contingent liabilities; Interest, penalties and enforcement costs; Automatic renewal; Continuing liability after ownership changes; Set-off rights; Personal asset declarations; Indemnity clauses; and Release following repayment. A guarantee capped at the principal facility may still expose the promoter to additional interest, default charges and legal expenses if those amounts sit outside the cap. The document should make the total exposure clear. Transfer-pricing and tax considerations A personal guarantee may also have tax and transfer-pricing implications. Where a promoter supports a UAE subsidiary because of their relationship with the Indian investing entity, authorities may examine whether a guarantee fee should have been charged and whether the arrangement was made at arm’s length. Relevant considerations include: Commercial benefit received by the UAE company; Improvement in borrowing capacity or interest rate; Comparable independent guarantee fees; Financial standing of the guarantor; Probability of default; Amount and duration of the guarantee; and Availability of security. The UAE Corporate Tax implications should also be reviewed where the guarantee involves related parties or connected persons. The guarantee should be reflected appropriately in the Indian entity’s accounts as a contingent commitment. Invocation may require recognition of a receivable, loan, impairment or loss depending on the facts. Place of effective management and substance concerns A personal guarantee does not, by itself, make the UAE company resident in India. However, it may form part of a broader factual pattern showing that the Dubai company is financially and operationally dependent on India. Risk increases where: All financing is arranged by the Indian promoter; The promoter controls the UAE bank account from India; Commercial decisions are made in India; UAE directors exercise no genuine authority; The Dubai office has little operational substance; and The UAE company exists mainly to invoice customers. Financing arrangements should support a real UAE operation rather than replace it. Practical example Assume an Indian company owns 100% of a Dubai mainland trading company. The Dubai company seeks an AED 2 million working-capital facility. The UAE bank asks the Indian promoter to sign an unlimited continuing personal guarantee covering every current and future liability of the Dubai company. Signing the document in that form would be problematic. The Indian company and promoter should first approach their designated authorised dealer bank. The guarantee should be revised to state a maximum amount and definite validity period. The Indian company must confirm that it has made ODI, controls the Dubai company and has sufficient unused financial-commitment capacity. The promoter’s eligibility and promoter-group relationship must also be documented. After corporate approval, the guarantee should be reported through Form FC as financial commitment of the Indian company. If the facility is renewed at the same amount, the rollover should be reported. If the limit rises to AED 3 million, the additional AED 1 million requires fresh capacity and compliance review. If the bank invokes AED 500,000, that portion becomes debt exposure and the invocation must be reported. The payment should then be routed through the authorised dealer bank with complete supporting documents. Common mistakes to avoid The most frequent mistakes include: Signing the UAE bank’s standard form without FEMA review; Assuming a guarantee is irrelevant until invocation; Treating Indian citizenship and FEMA residence as the same; Believing every UAE shareholder can give a personal guarantee; Ignoring the requirement that the Indian entity must have ODI and control; Signing an unlimited or open-ended guarantee; Failing to count the guarantee within financial commitment; Dividing a joint and several guarantee between guarantors; Not reporting a renewal or increase; Using a support letter to conceal an enforceable guarantee; Providing security over Indian assets without separate analysis; Paying an invoked amount outside the designated banking route; Ignoring transfer-pricing implications; and Failing to obtain a written release after the UAE facility is closed. Practical checklist before signing Before an Indian resident signs a guarantee for a UAE company, the parties should confirm: The guarantor’s FEMA residential status; Whether the guarantor is an eligible resident individual promoter; Whether the Indian entity has made compliant ODI; Whether the Indian entity controls the UAE company; Whether the guarantee relates to the UAE entity or an eligible step-down subsidiary; Whether the underlying borrowing is lawful; Whether the guarantee amount is clearly capped; Whether it has a definite validity period; Whether the Indian entity has sufficient financial-commitment capacity; Whether Form FC and supporting documents are ready; Whether the designated AD bank has reviewed the proposed route; Whether any Indian assets are being charged; Whether the guarantee fee requires transfer-pricing analysis; How renewal, invocation and release will be reported; and Whether the UAE document matches the FEMA-compliant structure. Conclusion A personal guarantee for a UAE company is not merely a private promise between an owner and a Dubai bank. When the guarantor is resident in India, it can become a regulated cross-border financial commitment from the moment it is issued. The permitted ODI route is available only where the prescribed relationship among the resident promoter, Indian entity and controlled foreign entity exists. The guarantee must be capped, time-bound, supported by available financial-commitment capacity and reported through the designated authorised dealer bank. Promoters should resist signing unlimited continuing guarantees simply because they are standard in UAE banking practice. A guarantee negotiated and reported before execution is far easier to defend than one discovered only after renewal, default or invocation.

Vs Commissioner of Customs (Import) (CESTAT Mumbai) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Mumbai, allowed eighteen appeals filed by M/s Usha International, Kolkata, Pulkit Choudhary and Sachin Agarwal against the common appellate order dated 31 July 2017 concerning alleged undervaluation of imported second-hand cranes. During the period from January 2006 to September 2010, the appellants had imported 129 used cranes through direct imports and high sea sales transactions. At the time of importation, the cranes were subjected to first-check assessment and physical examination by independent local Chartered Engineers in accordance with the guidelines prescribed by the Central Board of Excise and Customs (CBEC). The proper officers determined the assessable values based on such examination and technical reports, and the appellants discharged the applicable customs duty on the enhanced values before obtaining clearance of the imported goods. The Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI), Mumbai Zonal Unit, subsequently initiated investigations based on intelligence suggesting that several importers of used cranes were allegedly evading customs duty through a multi-layered arrangement involving undervaluation of imported goods, suppression or manipulation of freight and insurance charges, fabrication of invoices and bills of lading, and remittance of differential consideration through unauthorised channels. The investigation also alleged that importers accepted a benchmark valuation of Rs. 40 per kilogram of crane weight to facilitate customs clearance. Searches were conducted at the premises of persons associated with customs clearance, high sea sales and alleged unofficial overseas remittances. Statements were recorded from Pulkit Choudhary, the power of attorney holder of Usha International, Sachin Agarwal and various other persons. During the investigation, Pulkit Choudhary also deposited Rs. 4.14 crore with DRI through a letter dated 26 November 2010. The investigation resulted in six show cause notices issued between October 2012 and May 2013, which were adjudicated through six orders-in-original dated 31 March 2016. The adjudicating authority enhanced the assessable values of the imported cranes, confirmed differential customs duty under Section 28 of the Customs Act, 1962, together with interest, and imposed mandatory penalties under Section 114A. Additional penalties were imposed under Sections 112(a) and 114AA, and confiscation with an option to redeem the goods on payment of fine was ordered in proceedings arising from one of the notices. The Commissioner (Appeals) upheld rejection of declared values in all six matters and confirmed redetermination of value and penalties arising from the show cause notice dated 12 October 2012. Regarding the remaining five notices, the matter was remanded to ascertain the actual dates of service and determine the sustainable duty demands, since the appellants disputed service of the notices within the extended five-year limitation period. Before the Tribunal, the appellants contended that the controversy was covered by the coordinate Bench decision in Karim Jaria and Crown Lifters Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs (Import-I), Mumbai, involving substantially similar allegations concerning imports of second-hand cranes. They argued that the original assessments had been undertaken after physical examination and valuation by independent Chartered Engineers in accordance with CBEC Circular No. 4/2008-Customs dated 12 February 2008. Subsequent enhancement based on local purchase prices, internet prices, National Import Database (NIDB) data and prevailing market prices was alleged to be contrary to the Customs Valuation (Determination of Value of Imported Goods) Rules, 2007. The appellants also disputed invocation of the extended limitation period, confiscation and penalties, maintaining that all relevant particulars had been declared at the time of importation. The Revenue, however, relied upon the DRI investigation and statements of persons allegedly involved in suppression of transaction values and unofficial remittances. The Tribunal examined the statutory framework governing customs assessment under Sections 2(2), 12 and 14 of the Customs Act, 1962, together with the Customs Valuation Rules, 2007. It observed that transaction value constitutes the primary basis for valuation under Rule 3, subject to the prescribed statutory conditions. Where the transaction value cannot be accepted, valuation must proceed sequentially through Rules 4 to 9 after compliance with Rule 12. The Tribunal noted that the impugned cranes had already been subjected to physical examination and independent technical evaluation, following which the proper officers had enhanced the assessable values at the time of importation. An illustrative examination report concerning Bill of Entry No. 846684 dated 19 June 2008 demonstrated that the customs authorities had examined the condition, description and technical particulars of the imported machinery before finalising its assessable value. Consequently, any subsequent enhancement required specific and legally admissible evidence establishing that customs duty had escaped assessment or had been short-levied. A significant aspect of the dispute concerned reliance upon the statements of Pulkit Choudhary recorded under Section 108 of the Customs Act on 22 November 2010, 24 November 2010 and 21 August 2012. The Tribunal found that the first two statements had been retracted before the Additional Chief Metropolitan Magistrate on 25 November 2010, while the third statement was subsequently retracted through an affidavit submitted before the Additional Director General, DRI. Although the Commissioner (Appeals) had treated the later retraction as an afterthought, the Tribunal observed that all three statements had been retracted before competent authorities. Relying upon M/s Jeen Bhavani International Vs Commissioner of Customs, Nhava Sheva-II, it held that retracted statements must be properly examined in accordance with Section 138B before being relied upon as evidence. The Tribunal also noted that the Supreme Court had dismissed the Revenue’s appeals against the said decision on 27 February 2023. The Tribunal further relied upon the Supreme Court judgment in Mirah Exports Pvt. Ltd. Vs Collector of Customs, which established that the burden of proving undervaluation lies upon the Revenue. The Department must produce evidence demonstrating that the apparent transaction does not reflect the actual sale price. In the present case, the Commissioner (Appeals) had redetermined the assessable values under Rule 3 primarily on the basis of values stated by Pulkit Choudhary in his subsequently retracted statements. The Tribunal found that there was no adequate discussion establishing actual flow-back of funds or additional consideration warranting inclusion in the assessable value. Significantly, the Commissioner (Appeals) had himself found the original valuation methodology based on internet prices and allegedly comparable cranes unsustainable because necessary particulars such as year of manufacture, weight and period of importation had not been established. The subsequent reliance on retracted statements without independent corroboration therefore failed to satisfy the statutory requirements. The Tribunal also examined CBEC Circular No. 4/2008-Customs dated 12 February 2008, issued in continuation of the earlier circular dated 19 November 1987, governing valuation of imported second-hand machinery. These guidelines recognise the transaction value method for second-hand machinery and permit valuation based on depreciated machinery values only after lawful rejection of transaction value. They also recognise the use of independent Chartered Engineer certificates, including certificates issued by local Chartered Engineers where appropriate. The Tribunal emphasised that the residual method under Rule 9 cannot be applied directly by disregarding the preceding valuation rules. Since the original assessments had already been undertaken following the prescribed procedure, a fresh enhancement required credible evidence rather than merely substituting values appearing in retracted statements. The Tribunal further relied upon Junaid Kudia Vs Commissioner of Customs, Mumbai Import-II, concerning compliance with Section 138B, cross-examination of witnesses, evidentiary requirements for proving undervaluation and the finality of earlier assessments. It noted that the Revenue’s appeal against that decision had also been dismissed by the Supreme Court. CESTAT placed substantial reliance upon its coordinate Bench decision in Karim Jaria and Crown Lifters Pvt. Ltd., Final Order No. A/85324-85327/2022 dated 12 April 2022. In that matter, the Tribunal had rejected redetermination of assessable values of used cranes based upon alleged payments through non-banking channels because actual purchase prices had not been properly ascertained and the statements relied upon had not been subjected to the necessary evidentiary scrutiny and cross-examination. The earlier decision also distinguished lawful recovery of differential duty from disproportionate confiscation and penal consequences arising from unsupported allegations. The Tribunal observed that departmental appeals against the Crown Lifters decision had been registered before the Bombay High Court on 29 September 2023, but, as recorded in the present order, no stay had been granted against the coordinate Bench decision. Accordingly, the mere pendency of those appeals did not justify taking a contrary view on substantially similar facts. Considering the original customs assessment records, the applicable valuation rules, the retracted statements, the absence of sufficient corroborative evidence and the failure to provide appropriate cross-examination, the Tribunal concluded that the subsequent enhancement of assessable values and consequential differential customs duty demands were legally unsustainable. It also held that the penalties imposed upon Pulkit Choudhary and Sachin Agarwal under Sections 112(a), 114A and 114AA could not survive. Consequently, the common appellate order dated 31 July 2017 was set aside and all eighteen appeals were allowed with consequential relief, if any, in accordance with law. The Tribunal thus reaffirmed that allegations of customs undervaluation must be established through credible and legally admissible evidence and that retracted statements, unsupported by independent corroboration and the prescribed evidentiary procedure, cannot constitute the sole basis for enhancing assessable values and imposing penalties. Cases Discussed Karim Jaria and Crown Lifters Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs (Import-I), Mumbai (CESTAT Mumbai); Final Order No. A/85324-85327/2022; 2022 (4) TMI 948; 12/04/2022: Directly followed by the Tribunal. Redetermination of assessable value of imported used cranes based on alleged payments through unofficial channels cannot be sustained without corroborative evidence, ascertainment of actual purchase prices and cross-examination of persons whose statements are relied upon. Jeen Bhavani International Vs Commissioner of Customs, Nhava Sheva-II (CESTAT Mumbai); (2023) 6 Centax 11 (Tri.-Bom.): Relied upon for the principle that retraction of statements during adjudication proceedings must be properly examined in accordance with Section 138B of the Customs Act, 1962. Retracted statements cannot constitute the sole basis for establishing undervaluation without legally admissible corroborative evidence. Pyare Lal Bhargava Vs State of Rajasthan (Supreme Court): Referred to within the judgment in Jeen Bhavani International for the principle that reliance upon a retracted confession is unsafe unless its truthfulness and voluntary nature are established and it is corroborated in material particulars. Commissioner of Customs Nhava Sheva III Vs Jeen Bhavani International Etc. (Supreme Court of India); Civil Appeal Nos. 1482-1485 of 2023; (2023) 6 Centax 14 (S.C.); 27/02/2023: The Supreme Court dismissed the Revenue’s appeals against the CESTAT decision concerning reliance upon retracted statements and legally inadmissible evidence for establishing customs undervaluation. Mirah Exports Pvt. Ltd. Vs Collector of Customs (Supreme Court of India); (1998) 3 SCC 292; 1998 (98) ELT 3 (SC): Directly relied upon for the settled principle that the burden of proving undervaluation lies upon the Revenue. The Department must establish through necessary evidence that the invoice price does not reflect the actual transaction value. Gajra Bevel Gears (Supreme Court of India); 2000 (115) ELT 612 (SC): Referred to in CBEC Circular No. 4/2008-Customs dated 12 February 2008 for valuation of imported second-hand machinery based upon the certified value of new machinery, reduced by depreciation commensurate with its period of use, where the transaction value has been lawfully rejected. Chandni International (CESTAT); 2003 (153) ELT 312: Referred to in CBEC Circular No. 4/2008-Customs regarding the power of customs authorities to reject declared transaction value in accordance with the statutory valuation framework. Sunny Enterprises (CESTAT); 2004 (175) ELT 420: Referred to in CBEC Circular No. 4/2008-Customs concerning the requirement of reasonable doubt about the truth or accuracy of declared transaction value before its rejection. Venus Insulation Products Mfg. Co. (CESTAT); 2002 (143) ELT 364: Referred to in CBEC Circular No. 4/2008-Customs for the principle that the provision governing rejection of declared value operates at the initial stage of the sequential valuation methodology and cannot be invoked after completion of that methodology. Junaid Kudia Vs Commissioner of Customs, Mumbai Import-II (CESTAT Mumbai); (2024) 16 Centax 503 (Tri.-Bom.): Directly relied upon regarding the inadmissibility of witness statements without compliance with Section 138B, denial of cross-examination, absence of corroborative evidence of undervaluation and subsequent enhancement of values where earlier assessments had attained finality. J&K Cigarette Vs Collector of Customs (Delhi High Court); 2009 (242) ELT 189 (Del.); 2011 (22) STR 225 (Del.): Referred to in Junaid Kudia for the principle that the statutory procedure prescribed under Section 9D of the Central Excise Act, 1944, which is pari materia with Section 138B of the Customs Act, must be followed before relying upon statements to establish the truth of their contents. G-Tech Industries Vs Union of India (Punjab and Haryana High Court); 2016 (339) ELT 209 (P&H): Referred to in Junaid Kudia regarding mandatory compliance with the statutory procedure governing examination of witnesses and admissibility of statements relied upon during adjudication. Commissioner of Customs Vs Lord Shiva Overseas (CESTAT): Referred to in Junaid Kudia in support of the principle concerning finality of completed customs assessments and subsequent enhancement of assessable values. Malhotra Impex Vs Commissioner of Customs, Ahmedabad (CESTAT Delhi); 2006 (203) ELT 561 (Tri.-Del.): Referred to in Junaid Kudia regarding subsequent redetermination of assessable values where original assessment orders had attained finality. Commissioner of Customs (Preventive) Vs Paras Electronics (CESTAT Mumbai); 2009 (246) ELT 231 (Tri.-Mumbai); 2009 taxmann.com 923 (Mum.-CESTAT): Referred to in Junaid Kudia in support of the principle governing the finality of earlier customs assessments that had not been challenged through the prescribed statutory proceedings. Commissioner of Customs Vs Junaid Kudia Etc. (Supreme Court of India); Civil Appeal Diary No. 4161 of 2024; (2024) 16 Centax 504 (SC): The Tribunal noted that the Supreme Court had dismissed the Revenue’s appeal against the decision in Junaid Kudia, thereby leaving the CESTAT order undisturbed.

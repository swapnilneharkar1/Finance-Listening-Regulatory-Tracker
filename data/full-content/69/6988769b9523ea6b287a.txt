Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Fiberhome India Private Limited Vs Lalit Mishra (GSTAT Ghaziabad) Summary: The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal, Ghaziabad, allowed Fiberhome India Private Limited’s appeal concerning CGST and SGST demands arising from amendments to invoices originally reported under IGST. The appellant had issued invoices to BSNL during financial year 2017–18, treated the supplies as inter-State and paid IGST. During annual reconciliation, it identified the supplies as intra-State and amended the invoice particulars in GSTR-1 during financial year 2018–19. Scrutiny of that year’s returns resulted in proceedings under Section 73. The adjudicating authority dropped a proposed ITC demand of ₹3,00,911 but confirmed CGST and SGST of ₹3,30,834 each, interest of ₹6,55,052 and penalty of ₹66,166. The first appellate authority deleted the interest under Section 77(2), while maintaining tax and penalty and requiring payment under the correct heads followed by refund of the IGST. The appellant maintained that tax on the same supplies had already reached the Government, had not been adjusted against any subsequent liability and remained available for appropriation. It relied on decisions concerning wrong-head payments, electronic ledger balances and correction of duplicate duty debits. The Revenue argued that the separate GST levies and statutory ledger mechanisms could not be disregarded. Relying on Circular No. 162/18/2021-GST and Union of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd., it opposed automatic cross-adjustment and submitted that the appellant should pay CGST and SGST before seeking an IGST refund. The Tribunal distinguished the surviving output-tax dispute from the ITC proposal already dropped. On the validity of the notice, the Tribunal found that it disclosed aggregate return differences and general allegations of short payment, improper passing of credit and supplier non-payment, without identifying the necessary invoices, suppliers, recipients, supplies or supporting factual explanation. Merely invoking Section 16(2)(c) did not supply those missing particulars. Applying the principles discussed in Brindavan Beverages, Toyo Engineering, Gorkha Security Services and Oryx Fisheries, the Tribunal held that a later reply, adjudication order or appeal could not cure an essential allegation omitted from the notice. It clarified that every mismatch notice need not reproduce every invoice: an adequately explained discrepancy supported by accessible particulars may suffice. This notice, however, failed to disclose the factual case necessary for a meaningful response and violated Section 73 read with Section 75 and natural justice. That defect independently required the surviving demand and consequential penalty to be set aside. On the earlier IGST payment, the Tribunal explained that Section 77(2) of the CGST Act provides interest relief where a supply initially treated as inter-State is subsequently held to be intra-State, while Section 19(1) of the IGST Act provides the corresponding refund entitlement. Neither provision creates an unrestricted right to unilateral cross-head adjustment or makes the separate levies interchangeable. The Tribunal therefore rejected the appellant’s wider proposition that payment of any one GST levy necessarily discharges every other levy. It distinguished an unauthorised taxpayer ledger entry from a correction ordered and implemented by competent authorities. Decisions including Ocean E Mart, Saji S. and SYA Homes supported official appropriation of an identified and available payment. The Tribunal also considered the Supreme Court’s observations in Aberdare Technologies concerning correction of clerical errors and software limitations. The Tribunal emphasised that revenue neutrality must be demonstrated through matching of supplies, values, rates, original IGST payment and the corresponding CGST and SGST liability. The authorities must also verify that the earlier payment has not been refunded, restored to the ledger, adjusted elsewhere or otherwise made available again to the appellant. Subject to those safeguards, demanding the verified equivalent amount again would serve no fiscal purpose. The Tribunal set aside the orders insofar as they sustained CGST of ₹3,30,834, SGST of ₹3,30,834 and penalty of ₹66,166, maintained the earlier interest relief and dropping of the ITC proposal, and directed the jurisdictional proper officer to arrange appropriation of the IGST through reconciliation between the Centre and the State. The authorities were directed not to insist on a second deposit of the verified equivalent amount merely to complete that appropriation. No costs were awarded. Cases Discussed SYA Homes v. FULL TEXT OF THE ORIGINAL JUDGMENT/ORDER Brief introductory facts. 1. The appellant provides information and communication network solutions, optical communications and data networking construction services. It is registered with the State GST department. The present dispute arose during scrutiny of its returns for financial year 2018–19. The appellant had issued certain invoices to M/s BSNL during financial year 2017–18. It initially treated the supplies as inter-State supplies, reported them in its returns and discharged tax under the IGST head. During annual reconciliation, it found that the supplies should have been treated as intra-State supplies. The appellant amended the invoice particulars in GSTR-1 filed during financial year 2018–19. Those amendments reflected CGST and SGST liability in that year. The appellant says that the corresponding tax had already reached the Government under IGST in the earlier year and remained available for correction. It therefore disputes the treatment of the amended liability as a fresh, wholly unpaid tax demand. 2. From a perusal of the case record, we find that the controversy started with GST ASMT-10 dated 07.10.2023, which was followed by the appellant’s reply dated 25.11.2023 and the notice dated 27.12.2023 under Section 73. The notice compares GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B for 2018–19. The comparison shows a difference of Rs. 3,30,834.39 under each of CGST and SGST, whereas the DRC-01 summary and concluding demand state Rs. 3,30,934 under each head. These figures differ; the latter figures may be rounded versions. The amount ultimately confirmed, as recorded below, was Rs. 3,30,834 under each head. 3. The Deputy Commissioner, State Tax, Division-10, Noida passed Order-in-Original No. ZD090424474429X dated 30.04.2024. The proposed ITC demand of Rs. 3,00,911 was dropped. The authority confirmed CGST of Rs. 3,30,834 and SGST of Rs. 3,30,834, aggregating Rs. 6,61,668, with interest of Rs. 6,55,052 and penalty of Rs. 66,166 under Section 73(9). By Order-in-Appeal Ref. No. ZD0911244675447 dated 22.11.2024, the Additional Commissioner, Grade-II (Appeals), Noida set aside the interest demand by applying Section 77(2). The tax and penalty were maintained. However, the first appellate authority directed the appellant to first pay CGST and SGST and then seek refund of the IGST paid under the wrong head. The ITC proposal of Rs. 3,00,911 was dropped in the Order-in-Original. The surviving dispute concerns output tax under CGST and SGST and the request to appropriate an earlier IGST payment. We therefore distinguish the conditions for entitlement to input tax credit from the treatment of tax paid under the wrong head. 4. Hearings in this appeal took place on 06.08.2026, 21.08.2026, 08.09.2026 and 22.09.2026. The appellant submitted that the Government already held tax on the same supplies; the amendments did not represent additional turnover. It sought appropriation through the authorities, removal of the penalty and relief from a second payment pending refund. Submissions of the appellant 5. The appellant submitted that the difference of Rs. 3,30,834 each under CGST and SGST arose from adjustments in its books. Certain amendments relating to financial year 2017-2018 had been filed during financial year 2018-2019. The invoices had initially been reported under IGST. It was later found that they related to intra-State supplies, and the invoices were amended accordingly. The tax on those invoices had already been paid under IGST, but that payment had not been adjusted. 6. The appellant stated that the tax on the invoices had been duly deposited. The amount paid under IGST had not been adjusted against any future liability. It therefore requested that this payment be adjusted against the tax payable under CGST and SGST. It further submitted that there was no loss of revenue because the entire tax due on the supplies had already been paid in financial year 2017-2018. 7. The appellant relied on the decision of the Kerala High Court in Saji S. v. Commissioner, State GST Department. According to the appellant, that decision permitted tax paid under one head to be adjusted against liability under another head. Relying on the facts of the case, that decision and the fact that the dispute concerned the initial period of GST, the appellant requested that the demand be dropped. 8. Under ground B2, the appellant challenged interest under Section 50 of the CGST/SGST Act, 2017. It relied on Section 77(2) of the CGST Act, 2017, which provides relief from interest on central tax and State tax where integrated tax was paid on a transaction treated as an inter-State supply but subsequently held to be an intra-State supply. The appellant submitted that it had paid integrated tax instead of central tax and State tax. It therefore contended that no interest was payable on the confirmed tax amount of Rs. 6,61,668. 9. Under ground B3, the appellant challenged the penalty under Section 73 of the UPGST Act, 2017. It submitted that the tax demand of Rs. 6,61,668 was not sustainable for the reasons already given. It therefore contended that no penalty could be imposed under Section 73 of the CGST/SGST Act, 2017. 10. The appellant relied on MJBR Marketing & Financial Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India, W.P.(C) No.26333 of 2024, decided on 01.04.2025 by the Kerala High Court. It cited this decision for the proposition that the electronic credit and cash ledgers operate as a common pool or wallet and that the overall availability of funds must be considered before Section 73 is invoked for use of credit under the wrong head. It relied on Saji S. v. Commissioner, State GST Department, 2018 (19) GSTL 385 (Ker.), for adjustment of tax paid under the wrong head against liability under the correct head. It also cited S. Subrahmanyan & Co. v. CCE, 2011 (268) ELT 497 (Tri.-Ahmd.), affirmed in 2013 (296) ELT A123 (Guj.), for correction of duplicate duty debits without a formal refund application. The appellant cited Visakhapatnam Steel Plant v. CCE, 2002 (149) ELT 708 (Tri.-Bang.), for correction of accounting errors without prior departmental approval. It relied on Bayer Vapi Pvt. Ltd. v. CCE & ST, for restoration of duplicate duty payments through self-recredit where the second payment is undisputed. It also relied on Rejimon Padickapparambil Alex v. Union of India, referring to CBIC Circular No.192/04/2023-GST and treatment of the electronic ledger as a common resource across its compartments. Submissions of the Revenue 11. The authorised representatives of the State Department submit that the electronic credit ledger is governed by statute. The Revenue relies on Section 77 of the CGST Act, Section 19 of the IGST Act and CBIC Circular No.162/18/2021-GST. It submits that the taxpayer must pay tax under the correct head and seek refund of the tax paid under the wrong head. The Revenue further submits that the conditions of Section 16(2)(c) must be satisfied. 12. The Revenue relies on Union of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd. & Ors., (2021) 13 SCC 1 / 2021 SCC OnLine SC 1006, for compliance with the statutory return and ledger mechanisms. It distinguishes Saji S. on the ground that the case concerned departmental adjustment during detention proceedings and did not authorise unilateral recredit through GSTR-3B. It also relies on State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Pvt. Ltd., Civil Appeal No.230 of 2023, and Thirumalakonda Plywoods v. Assistant Commissioner (ST), W.P. No.24235 of 2022. It cites these decisions for the requirement to prove eligibility for ITC and comply with the statutory conditions and time limits. The Revenue therefore seeks to sustain the recovery under Section 73, interest under Section 50 and penalty under Section 73(11). 13. The Revenue submitted that CGST, SGST and IGST arise under distinct enactments and are separately accounted for. It relied on the return and ledger framework, CBIC Circular No.162/18/2021-GST dated 25.09.2021 and Union of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd. It opposed automatic cross-adjustment and maintained that absence of payment under the correct heads justified the demand. The tax heads cannot simply be ignored. Questions for determination 14. The questions are: whether the show cause notice supplies the essential facts supporting the proposed demand and whether the general reference to Section 16(2)(c) supplies the missing particulars; whether a reply or a later order can cure that omission; and whether the surviving CGST and SGST demand and penalty can stand. 15. Separately, we must decide the requested appropriation of the earlier IGST payment. 16. The question is whether that distinction requires another payment from the appellant even when the identified earlier payment can be formally appropriated. The distinction between an unauthorised entry by a taxpayer and a correction ordered and carried out by the competent authorities is central to this appeal. Statutory provisions governing the notice 17. Section 16(1) permits a registered person, subject to the statutory conditions and restrictions, to take credit of input tax on supplies used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of business. For the period in dispute, Section 16(2) required possession of a prescribed tax document, receipt of the goods or services, actual payment of the tax to Government and furnishing of the return under Section 39. The applicable provisos and restrictions also remain relevant. Later conditions cannot be applied retrospectively merely because they appear in the current text. On examination of the show cause notice, we find that Section 16(2)(c) has been invoked in the notice but it does not itself identify a defaulting supplier, an unpaid invoice or the factual basis of an alleged breach. A reference to this provision cannot replace those particulars where they are necessary to understand and answer the allegation. 18. Section 73(1) applies to this financial year and provides for notice where tax has not been paid or has been short paid or erroneously refunded, or input tax credit has been wrongly availed or utilised, for reasons other than fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. Moreover, as per Section 73(9) of the GST Act the proper officer must consider the representation, if any, and determine the tax, interest and a penalty of ten per cent of the tax or ten thousand rupees, whichever is higher. The power to determine liability follows the notice and opportunity to answer; it does not permit the order to supply an entirely different factual case. Hence, we find that Section 73 requires a meaningful show cause notice before an adverse determination. A notice need not prove the case finally, reproduce every document or resemble a judgment. It must, however, tell the taxpayer what factual case is proposed and why the stated amount is recoverable. Similarly, Section 73(1) requires the officer to notify the relevant case and require an explanation of the specified liability, interest and penalty. Mentioning Section 16 of the CGST Act without identifying the material facts does not satisfy that purpose. The outward-tax and input-credit proposals must each have an intelligible factual basis. Section 75(4) requires an opportunity of hearing in the circumstances stated there. Section 75(6) requires the order to state the relevant facts and the basis of decision. Section 75(7) prohibits confirmation on grounds other than those specified in the notice. These safeguards require disclosure of the case, a meaningful answer and a decision within the notified grounds. It is also settled law that a detailed final order cannot retrospectively supply an essential allegation omitted from the notice. Contents and sufficiency of the present show cause notice 19. The show cause notice states that the explanation furnished in response to ASMT-10, issued following the AI notice received through the portal for the relevant year, was acceptable only in part. On examining the returns for that year, the Department alleged that the appellant had paid less tax than required and/or wrongly claimed excess ITC. The notice further alleges that the appellant claimed excess ITC in GSTR-3B compared with GSTR-2A. It states that credit was claimed only on the basis of invoices and that the corresponding purchases and credit were not reflected in GSTR-2A. It treats the absence of credit from GSTR-2A as showing that the selling dealers neither declared the sales in their returns and GSTR-1 nor deposited the corresponding tax with the Department or the Government treasury. On that basis, it states that the credit cannot be allowed. The notice refers to the requirement that tax charged on a supply must actually have been paid to the Government, either in cash or by using admissible input tax credit, and alleges that this condition was not fulfilled. It therefore treats the ITC as wrongly claimed and proposes recovery under Section 73, together with interest and penalty. In its concluding part, the notice requires the appellant to explain why those amounts should not be paid, together with interest under Section 50 and penalty under Section 73. 20. On a perusal and evaluation of the material particulars of the show cause notice and DRC-01, we find that the notice contains a comparison of GSTR-1 against GSTR-3B for output tax, and GSTR-2A against the ITC figures used in the notice. It draws general conclusions of short payment, improper passing of credit and non-payment by suppliers. It does not identify the disputed invoice numbers, dates, suppliers, recipients or particular supplies supporting those conclusions. The omission is factual, not merely a defect in the heading or format. It alleges short payment and excess ITC, and expressly invokes Section 16(2)(c). The defect is that these are general allegations drawn from aggregate data without the necessary transaction details or supporting factual explanation. No identified invoice is alleged to be fictitious; no identified supplier is shown to have failed to pay tax; and no identified transaction is connected to the asserted statutory breach. In the notice and accompanying material placed before us, no identified invoice schedule is incorporated to explain the alleged default. The SCN should disclose the material on which the proposed demand is based. Undisclosed departmental records cannot be assumed to supply the missing particulars. For the surviving output-tax proposal, the notice states annual figures but does not identify the entries or supplies producing the alleged unpaid CGST and SGST. In this case that omission matters because the appellant attributes the difference to invoice amendments concerning supplies on which IGST had already been paid. In this regard, we refer to the following decisions of the Hon’ble Supreme Court to clarify the legal requirement. 21. Commissioner of Central Excise v. Brindavan Beverages (P) Ltd. (2007) 5 SCC 388. In Brindavan Beverages, the Supreme Court explained that the show cause notice is the foundation of the departmental case. Allegations which are vague or lack necessary details deny a proper opportunity to answer. 22. Applying that principle, naming the returns, the year and an aggregate amount does not replace the missing factual particulars where the department alleges specific wrongdoing in supplies or credit. Hence, we find that the present notice gives numerical differences but does not adequately disclose the transactions and facts supporting its conclusions. 23. Commissioner of Customs v. Toyo Engineering India Ltd. (2006) 7 SCC 592. In Toyo Engineering, the Supreme Court held that the department could not travel beyond the show cause notice. A new factual ground could not be introduced in appeal to sustain the demand. 24. Here, the department cannot turn an aggregate mismatch into an unnotified case of fictitious invoices, non-receipt of supplies or unilateral re-credit. Those matters require their own factual particulars and an opportunity to answer. They cannot be presumed from the figures in this notice. 25. Gorkha Security Services v. Government of NCT of Delhi (2014) 9 SCC 105. Gorkha Security Services explains that a notice must convey the grounds and the proposed action so that the affected person can answer effectively. 26. The case arose from blacklisting, but the requirement of meaningful disclosure is relevant here. The deficiency lies in the material facts supporting it. Knowledge of the amount sought does not necessarily amount to knowledge of the case to be met. 27. Oryx Fisheries (P) Ltd. v. Union of India (2010) 13 SCC 427. Oryx Fisheries stresses fair consideration and an open mind in show cause proceedings. Here, however, the notice moves from a return mismatch to a conclusion of supplier non-payment without identifying the underlying transactions or evidence. A genuine opportunity requires disclosure of the basis of that proposed conclusion and consideration of the answer. 28. We do not lay down an inflexible rule that every mismatch notice must reproduce every invoice. A clearly explained return-entry discrepancy, supported by accessible particulars, may be sufficient. The present notice must be judged on its own contents. It alleges improper passing of credit and supplier non-payment, yet gives no corresponding particulars. Read as a whole, it does not adequately state the factual case underlying the disputed recovery. We therefore hold that the impugned demand cannot be sustained on this notice. The defect is substantial: it prevents a meaningful answer to the factual case and violates the safeguards of Section 73 read with Section 75 and natural justice. This conclusion is an independent ground for setting aside the demand and its consequential penalty. Examination of the orders under challenge 29. At this stage, we consider the reasoning in the adjudication order and the first appellate order. The adjudication order substantially reproduces the details of the show cause notice. It records that a notice was issued under Section 73 for the reasons stated therein and that no payment was made within 30 days of its issue. The demand was therefore determined on the basis of the documents available with the Department and the information furnished by the appellant. The order finds that the appellant paid Rs. 3,30,834.00 less under CGST and Rs. 3,30,834.00 less under SGST through GSTR-3B than the amounts disclosed in GSTR-1. It accordingly determines short payment under those heads and creates a demand for those amounts, together with interest under Section 50 and penalty under Section 73. The first appellate order adopts the same approach to the appellant’s submission concerning payment under the wrong head. 30. According to the first appellate order, the appellant’s returns for the relevant year were examined. Show cause notice number ZD0901230791220 dated 18.01.2023 was issued on two issues. The first issue, as described in the order, was that the appellant had claimed less ITC in GSTR-3B than in GSTR-1 by Rs. 3,30,834.39 under CGST and Rs. 3,30,834.39 under SGST. The sales had nevertheless been shown in GSTR-1 against the names of various purchasing dealers, and ITC had been passed on to them. On disputed point number 1, the appellant submitted that the liability related to outward supplies declared under CGST and SGST. By mistake, the admitted liability was shown and paid under IGST instead of CGST and SGST. According to the appellant, this caused the difference. The appellant also submitted that the proper officer had calculated interest even though no interest was payable under Section 50. The first appellate authority considered these submissions and held that the appellant sought the benefit of tax payment on the ground that tax relating to CGST and SGST had mistakenly been paid under IGST. This request cannot be accepted. The appellant had billed under CGST and SGST but paid the tax under IGST. As a result, no tax was paid to the State Government in this regard. The appellant’s submission is therefore not acceptable. The amount was not paid under CGST and SGST in the filed GSTR-3B, and it was utilised under the IGST head. The claimed benefit cannot therefore be allowed. However, considering Section 77(2) interest of Rs. 6,55,052.00 imposed by the proper officer under CGST and SGST has been set aside. Appeal number AD090724056519L/830/24 for financial year 2018-19, covering April 2018 to March 2019, concerning proceedings under Section 73 of the UPGST Act, 2017, is partly allowed. The total interest of Rs. 6,55,052.00 imposed by the assessing authority under CGST and SGST is set aside. The tax and penalty imposed under CGST and SGST are confirmed. For the reasons discussed above, we find that the first appellate authority passed the impugned order without considering the material defects in the show cause notice. Issue of payment under the wrong head and official appropriation 31. For proper adjudication of this issue, we discuss Section 77 of the CGST Act and 19 of the IGST Act. Section 77(1) provides for refund, in the prescribed manner and subject to prescribed conditions, of central and State tax paid on a transaction initially treated as intra-State but subsequently held to be inter-State. Section 77(2) gives relief from interest on the central and State tax payable where integrated tax was paid on a transaction initially treated as inter-State but subsequently held to be intra-State. The latter is the direction of error in the present case. The two provisions deal with opposite directions of error. Section 77(1) of the CGST Act concerns CGST and SGST paid on a supply first treated as intra-State but later held to be inter-State. Section 77(2) concerns interest where IGST was paid on a supply later held to be intra-State. Corresponding State provisions must be applied to the State component. Section 19 of the IGST Act deals with the corresponding consequences under that Act. The relevant part of subsection (1) states that the registered person “shall be granted refund of the amount of integrated tax so paid”. It applies where the supply was considered inter-State and is subsequently held to be intra-State. The prescribed manner and conditions remain relevant. Section 19(2) concerns exemption from interest on IGST in the reverse situation, where CGST and SGST were originally paid. It is not the subsection which directly grants the appellant relief from interest on CGST and SGST here. That relief follows from Section 77(2), already applied in the first appeal. Section 19(1) of the IGST Act provides the corresponding refund entitlement where integrated tax was paid on a supply considered inter-State but subsequently held to be intra-State. Section 19(2) removes interest on integrated tax payable in the opposite situation, where central and State tax were originally paid. Thus, Section 19(1) and Section 77(2) operate together for the error asserted here. Neither provision expressly grants an unrestricted right of unilateral cross-head adjustment. These provisions recognise the obligation to correct a mistaken classification. They do not expressly say that a taxpayer may treat all tax heads as interchangeable or make any ledger entry it chooses. They also do not, by themselves, extinguish the correct-head liability. The expression “tax” does not merge the separate statutory levies. Payment as IGST is not automatically payment as SGST merely because both arise within GST. We therefore do not accept the appellant’s wider submission that payment of any one levy necessarily discharges every other levy. Relief must follow from a lawful treatment of the particular payment, rather than from erasing the identity of the heads. Strict construction substantive compliance and procedural requirements 32. Payment under the correct heads followed by unilateral recredit may also be examined through purposive interpretation. We are mindful of the principles that there is no equity in tax law and that taxing provisions require strict interpretation. Their scope must nevertheless be understood in the light of the Supreme Court’s decisions. In Gursahai Saigal v. Commissioner of Income Tax, Punjab, 1962 SCC OnLine SC 71, the Supreme Court stated: 33. 8. The question thus raised is one of construction of sub-section (6) and (8). The assessee relies on a rule of construction applicable to taxing statutes which has been variously stated. Rowlatt J. put it in these words in Cape Brandy Syndicate v. Inland Revenue Commissioners¹. “In a taxing Act one has to look merely at what is clearly said. There is no room for any intendment. There is no equity about a tax . There is no presumption as to a tax. Nothing is to be read in ,nothing is to be implied. One can only look fairly at the language used . ”The object of this rule is to prevent a taxing statute being construe “according to its intent, though not according to its words Inre: Bethlehem Hospital². This Court has accepted this rule. 34. Bhagwati J. in A.V. Fernandez v. State of Kerala³ said, “If … the case is not covered within the four corners of the provisions of the taxing statute, no tax can be imposed by inference or by analogy or by trying to probe into the intentions of the legislature and by considering what was the substance of the matter. ”It has even been said that “if the provision is so wanting in clarity that no meaning is reasonably clear, the courts will be unable to regard it as of any effect.” : see Inland Revenue Commissioners v.BladnochDistillery Co.

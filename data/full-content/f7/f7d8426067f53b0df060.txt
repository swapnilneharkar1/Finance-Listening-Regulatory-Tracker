Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Global Consulting Services Vs ITO (ITAT Chennai) Summary : Global Consulting Services, a partnership firm, provided advisory, facilitation and coordination services to educational institutions under written agreements concerning global rankings. For AY 2024–25, it filed its return on 3 July 2024 declaring ₹9,45,850 under Section 44AD. The case was selected for scrutiny to examine substantial receipts on which clients had deducted tax under Section 194J and whether the firm should have used Section 44ADA. The Assessing Officer (AO) classified the work as professional consultancy, estimated income at 70% of gross receipts and assessed ₹51,33,445, making an addition of ₹41,87,595. The CIT(A), NFAC, upheld the assessment, relying on the scope of work in the agreements, the invoices and submissions, the partnership deed and the firm’s office arrangements. The firm contended that its work involved substantial execution through reporting, implementation and coordination and fell outside the specified or notified professions under Section 44AA(1). It argued that the payer’s choice of TDS provision could not determine how its income was assessed. It also cited the treatment of similar activities in earlier years, stated that it had incurred ₹36,59,284 in business expenditure through banking channels, challenged the basis for the 70% estimate and sought full TDS credit on the ground that it had disclosed all receipts. The Revenue relied on the lower authorities’ orders and on the deductions reflected in Form 26AS. The Tribunal examined the firm’s actual contractual functions, including organising institutional data, coordinating with stakeholders, supporting reporting and performance evaluation, benchmarking and giving operational guidance. It found that these functions amounted to institutional support, commercial facilitation and business consultancy. The Revenue had identified neither a CBDT notification covering such activities nor material showing that the services amounted to technical consultancy within Section 44AA(1). The absence of a prescribed professional qualification supported this conclusion when considered with the work performed and its commercial objective. The Tribunal also found that the record did not establish commission or brokerage receipts or an agency business that would attract the exclusions under Section 44AD(6). Drawing support from Manoj Rajaram Sharma and Vishnu Dattatraya Ponkshe, the Tribunal distinguished the payer’s withholding obligation from the statutory classification of income in the recipient’s hands. It held that TDS under Section 194J and the use of advisory or consultancy labels in the agreements were insufficient to bring the firm within Section 44ADA. Once that provision was found inapplicable, the 70% estimate based on it could not survive. The Tribunal set aside the CIT(A)’s order and directed acceptance of the income returned under Section 44AD. It separately directed the Jurisdictional Assessing Officer to verify Form 26AS and allow admissible TDS credit. The appeal was partly allowed for statistical purposes. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT CHENNAI The present appeal has been preferred by the Assessee against the order dated 02.03.2026 passed by the Learned Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals), National Faceless Appeal Centre, Delhi [hereinafter referred to as “the Ld. CIT(A)”], arising from the assessment order dated 31.12.2025 passed by the Assessing Officer, Assessment Unit, Income Tax Department [hereinafter referred to as “the AO”], u/s.143(3) r.w.s. 144B of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (hereinafter referred to as “the Act”) for the Assessment Year (A.Y) 2024-25. 2. The brief facts of the care are that the assessee is a partnership firm, carrying on the business of providing advisory facilitation and coordination services to educational institutions under formal written agreements for global ranking. The assessee filed the return of income for the AY 2024-25 on 03.07.2024 declaring a total income of Rs.9,45,850/- under the presumptive taxation scheme in accordance with sec.44AD of the Act. The case was selected for scrutiny for the reason to verify the substantial receipts u/s.194J of the Act and income shown u/s.44AD instead of 44ADA of the Act. Accordingly, the statutory notices were issued to the assessee and the assessee furnished the necessary details and explanation from time to time. Based on the examination, the AO concluded that the assessee was rendering global ranking services to educational institutions. The services rendered include providing advisory support ranking services coordination with stakeholders and consultancy for institutional ranking improvement and undertaking other allied activities as stipulated in the respective agreements and held that the nature of services rendered involved specialized knowledge and professional expertise and therefore constituted professional consultancy services rather than ordinary business activities. Consequently, the AO rejected the claim of sec.44AD of the Act and estimated the income of the assessee at 70% of the gross receipts by passing an order u/s.143(3) of the Act dated 31.12.2025 determining total income at Rs.51,33,445/- as against the returned income of Rs.9,45,850/-, thereby making an addition of Rs.41,87,595/-. 3. Aggrieved by the order of the AO, the assessee preferred an appeal before the ld.CIT(A). Before the ld.CIT(A), the assessee submitted that the AO has erred in estimating the income from profession at 70% of the receipts treating the business carried on by the assessee as profession by invoking sec.44ADA of the Act instead of sec.44AD of the Act. Further, the assessee submitted that they have spent a revenue expenditure of Rs.36,59,284 in the normal course of business and hence estimating income at 70% is baseless and unsustainable in law. These expenses have been spent through banking channel and incurred exclusively for the business purposes. Further, the assessee claimed that the total TDS credit available as per Form 26AS needs to be given as the assessee has declared the entire gross receipts while arriving at the total income u/s.44AD of the Act. In view of the above, the ld.AR prayed for accepting the return of income filed by the assessee by deleting the additions made by the AO and also prayed for giving the entire credit of TDS as per Form 26AS. On perusal of the submissions made by the assessee, the ld.CIT(A) rejected the same and confirmed the order of the AO by passing an order dated 02.03.2026 by holding as under:- “6.1 The brief facts of the case are that the appellant, a partnership firm namely M/s Global Consulting Services, filed its return of income for A.Y. 2024- 25 on 03.07.2024 declaring total income of Rs.9,45,850/-. The return of income was processed u/s 143(1) of the Income Tax Act, 1961 and thereafter the case was selected for scrutiny under CASS primarily to examine substantial receipts subjected to deduction of tax u/s 194J and the correctness of income offered under presumptive provisions. Statutory notices u/s 143(2) and 142(1) were issued and duly complied with by the appellant. The assessment was ultimately completed u/s 143(3) r.w.s. 144B of the Act vide order dated 31.12.2025 determining total income at Rs.51,33,445/- as against returned income of Rs.9,45,850/- by estimating income at 70% of gross receipts and thereby making an addition of Rs.41,87,595/- under the head Profits and Gains of Business or Profession by invoking provisions relating to section 44ADA of the Act. Aggrieved by the said assessment order, the appellant has preferred the present appeal. During appellate proceedings, the appellant submitted that it is engaged in providing advisory, facilitation and coordination services to educational institutions under various contractual arrangements and had offered income under presumptive provisions considering the operational nature of services rendered. It was contended that receipts were fully disclosed, routed through banking channels and reflected in Form 26AS and therefore estimation of income at 70% was arbitrary and excessive. The appellant further submitted that services rendered were managerial and coordination-oriented rather than technical consultancy and therefore estimation adopted in assessment lacked statutory backing. I have considered the assessment order, statement of facts, written submissions furnished by the appellant and material available on record. The primary issue for adjudication is whether the Assessing Officer was justified in rejecting the income declared by the appellant and estimating profit by applying higher presumptive rate considering the nature of services rendered. From perusal of the assessment records, it is evident that the case was selected specifically to verify whether income liable under section 44ADA had been incorrectly offered under presumptive provisions applicable to business income. The agreements entered into by the appellant with various universities and institutions, copies of which were examined during assessment proceedings, consistently describe the scope of work as “Global Rankings Advisory Services” involving institutional analysis, strategic guidance, performance evaluation support, data compilation, reporting assistance and advisory inputs for improvement of institutional rankings. The description of services clearly demonstrates engagement requiring specialized knowledge, analytical inputs and advisory expertise rather than routine trading or facilitation activity. The assessment order records that the agreements required structured advisory support and professional intervention in institutional benchmarking and strategic positioning. Such services inherently fall within the ambit of professional or technical consultancy as contemplated under section 44AA(1) of the Act. Once an activity assumes the character of profession involving consultancy or specialized advisory services, exclusion contained in section 44AD(6) (i) becomes applicable and income cannot be assessed under provisions meant for eligible business. The statutory framework distinguishes business income from professional income and mandates application of section 44ADA where receipts arise from professional services. It is further noticed that even in the written submissions filed during assessment, the appellant acknowledged rendering advisory and institutional support services under formal agreements with educational institutions. The terminology employed in agreements, invoices and contractual obligations consistently reflects advisory engagement rather than execution of routine operational contracts. The uniform characterization of services across multiple agreements substantiates the conclusion drawn in assessment proceedings regarding professional nature of receipts. Reference may also be made to the partnership deed of the appellant firm wherein the stated object of the firm is to provide consulting services to educational institutions along with allied training and certification services. The declared nature of business activity itself supports the finding that the firm is engaged in consultancy services requiring professional expertise. The characterization adopted in foundational business documents therefore aligns with the conclusion reached in the assessment order. Further, examination of the lease agreement placed on record shows that business premises were taken on lease exclusively for non-residential commercial purposes for carrying on consulting activities, indicating organized professional operations rather than small-scale presumptive business activity. The existence of structured office setup, recurring institutional clients and advisory-based engagements reinforces the professional character of income. It is also relevant that the Assessing Officer has not made any addition on account of undisclosed receipts or suppression of turnover. Rather, the estimation has been made after analyzing the nature of services and applicability of correct presumptive framework. Where books of account are not maintained in a manner enabling verification of true profits and income is offered under an incorrect statutory provision, estimation of income based on surrounding facts becomes permissible under section 143(3) of the Act. The estimation adopted cannot be said to be arbitrary when it flows from examination of contractual obligations and nature of services rendered. The contention of the appellant that estimation lacks comparable cases is not acceptable in the facts of the present case, as estimation has been necessitated due to inapplicability of the presumptive provision originally adopted by the appellant and absence of reliable profit determination mechanism. The Assessing Officer has exercised statutory discretion after granting opportunity and considering submissions placed on record. No material has been produced during appellate proceedings demonstrating that estimation made is perverse or contrary to factual findings emerging from agreements executed by the appellant. In view of the above discussion, it is held that the receipts earned by the appellant arise from professional consultancy services falling within the scope of section 44ADA and the action of the Assessing Officer in rejecting the returned income and estimating profits based on nature and scale of activities is in accordance with provisions of the Income Tax Act, 1961. The addition of Rs.41,87,595/- made under the head Profits and Gains of Business or Profession is therefore sustained. The ground relating to initiation of penalty proceedings u/s 270A being consequential and independent in nature does not require adjudication at this stage. Charging of interest, wherever applicable, shall follow consequentially in accordance with law.” 4. Aggrieved by the order of the ld.CIT(A), the assessee is in appeal before us. The ld.AR for the assessee submitted that the authorities below have misinterpreted the nature of services provided by the assessee as professional services and wrongly invoked the sec.44ADA of the Act. The ld.AR contended that the assessee is engaged in composite activities involving facilitation, coordination, reporting, implementation and operational functions, which are in the nature of substantial execution. Further, the ld.AR stated that the assessee is not carrying on any profession as stated u/s.44AA(1) of the Act like legal, medical, engineering, architectural, accountancy, technical consultancy or interior decoration, nor falls within other professions specifically notified by the CBDT like film artists or authorized representatives. Further, the services provided by the assessee do not require any statutory or recognized professional qualification. Therefore, the income is assessable u/s.44AD of the Act instead of wrongly recognized by the revenue as income liable to be taxed u/s.44ADA of the Act. In support of the above arguments, the ld.AR relied on the recent decision of the Mumbai Benches of the Tribunal in the case of Manoj Rajaram Sharma v. ITO (2026) ITA No.8313/Mum/2025, wherein the Tribunal examined the applicability of sec.44AD and 44ADA of the Act in the context of an assessee carrying on the business correspondent activities. Further, the ld.AR submitted that the TDS made by the payer of the service charges according to their understanding of the nature of payment and it is for the limited purpose of tax withholding. Just because the TDS has been deducted wrongly by the service recipients to service provided cannot be termed as professional charges liable to be declared u/s.44ADA of the Act. The case on hand is that the assessee is providing advisory support services, ranking services and other allied activities as stipulated in the respective agreements and cannot be categorized as professional services solely on the basis of TDS deducted u/s.194J of the Act instead of 194C of the Act. In this regard, reliance is placed on the decision of Arthur Bernard Sebastine Pais vs DCIT, ITA No. 1683/Bang/2019, Assessment Year 2017– 18, order dated 16.10.2019 (ITAT Bangalore, SMC-B Bench) wherein the Hon’ble ITAT held that the issue whether income is taxable under Section 44AD or Section 44ADA is a debatable matter and cannot be subject to adjustment under Section 143(1)(a). The Tribunal categorically observed that such recharacterization of income is beyond the scope of processing under Section 143(1), especially when the gross receipts as reflected in Form 26AS have already been duly offered to tax in the return of income. 5. Further, the ld.AR submitted that sec.44AD(6) of the Act applies only to the persons carrying on the agency business, which is not the present case, therefore, the assessee’s activities of advisory and facilitation based business services, and not a pure agency model. Therefore, the application of sec.44AD(6) of the Act to the entire turnover is arbitrary and not justified in law. The ld.AR also submitted that the assessee has been carrying on the similar business activity consistently and are being declared u/s.44AD of the Act in the preceding assessment years and the same has been accepted by the department in the regular course of assessment. Further, in respect of the estimation made at 70% of the gross receipts u/s.44ADA of the Act, the ld.AR referred to the decision of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Dhakeswari Cotton Mills Ltd. v. CIT [1954] 26 ITR 775 (SC), wherein it has been categorically held that the AO, though not bound by strict rules of evidence, is not entitled to make an assessment based on pure guess, suspicion, or conjecture and there must be some material or evidence to support such estimation. In view of the above, the ld.AR prayed for setting aside the order of the ld.CIT(A) by accepting the income declared u/s.44AD of the Act and grant TDS credit as per Form 26AS of the assessee. 6. Per contra, the ld.DR for the Revenue relied on the orders of the authorities and submitted that the entire TDS shown in Form 26AS clearly shows that the assessee is carrying on the profession and hence liable to be assed u/s.44ADA of the Act. Therefore, the ld.DR prayed for confirming the order of the ld.CIT(A). 7. We have heard the rival submissions, perused the material available on record and carefully gone through the judicial precedents, cited supra, and also the orders of the authorities below. The principal issue arising for our consideration is whether the activities carried on by the assessee-firm in providing global ranking advisory, facilitation and coordination services to educational institutions constitute a profession falling within the ambit of section 44AA(1) of the Act, so as to attract the presumptive taxation scheme u/s.44ADA of the Act, or whether the same are in the nature of business activities eligible for consideration u/s.44AD of the Act. 8. The undisputed facts are that the assessee is a partnership firm engaged in providing advisory, facilitation and coordination services to educational institutions under contractual arrangements relating to global rankings. The assessee filed its return of income for the assessment year under consideration declaring total income of Rs.9,45,850/- by adopting the presumptive taxation scheme u/s.44AD of the Act. The case was selected for scrutiny primarily to examine the substantial receipts subjected to tax deduction u/s.194J of the Act and the correctness of offering income u/s.44AD instead of section 44ADA of the Act. The AO, after examining the agreements executed with the educational institutions, concluded that the activities involved specialised knowledge, analytical inputs and professional expertise. Accordingly, the AO rejected the assessee’s claim u/s.44AD and estimated the income at 70% of the gross receipts. The Ld. CIT(A) confirmed the action of the AO. 9. At the outset, we observe that the applicability of section 44ADA cannot be determined merely on the basis of the terminology employed in the agreements or the fact that the services involve a degree of knowledge, skill or experience. Section 44ADA specifically applies to an assessee engaged in a profession referred to in section 44AA(1) of the Act, subject to the prescribed conditions. Section 44AA(1) identifies particular professions, including legal, medical, engineering, architectural, accountancy, technical consultancy and interior decoration, together with other professions notified by the Board. Thus, the relevant test is whether the nature and substance of the activities carried on by the assessee fall within the scope of a profession covered by the said provision. The mere description of an activity as consultancy or advisory services does not automatically bring it within the ambit of section 44ADA. 10. On examination of the nature of services described in the material placed before us, we find that the assessee’s primary activity is to assist educational institutions in maintaining, improving and strengthening their positions in global rankings vis-à-vis other comparable institutions. Such activities involve the compilation and organisation of institutional data, coordination with relevant stakeholders, assistance in reporting and performance evaluation, benchmarking of institutional parameters and providing operational guidance for improving the institution’s ranking performance. The object of these activities is to enable the client institutions to present and organise their performance information effectively, monitor the relevant ranking parameters and improve or maintain their competitive position among similar institutions. 11. In our considered view, the aforesaid activities, when considered in their entirety, are in the nature of institutional support, commercial facilitation, coordination and business consultancy services. The fact that the assessee may analyse institutional performance, assist in compiling data or provide guidance regarding ranking parameters does not, by itself, establish that the assessee is rendering technical consultancy within the meaning of section 44AA(1) of the Act. There is a material distinction between services involving ordinary business support, coordination and performance improvement and professional technical consultancy requiring specialised scientific or technological expertise falling within the statutory provision. The lower authorities have principally relied upon the advisory nature of the activities without establishing how the services actually rendered by the assessee constitute a specified profession within the meaning of section 44AA(1). 12. We further note that the assessee’s activities do not involve the rendering of legal, medical, engineering, architectural, accountancy or interior-decoration services. Nor has the Revenue identified any specific notification issued by the CBDT under which the assessee’s activity of providing ranking facilitation and institutional coordination services is covered as a notified profession. Further, the material on record does not establish that the services are dependent upon a prescribed professional qualification or a recognised qualification in science or technology of the kind relevant to the technical consultancy category relied upon by the Revenue. The absence of a prescribed professional qualification is not, by itself, conclusive of the classification of an activity. Nevertheless, when considered together with the actual contractual functions performed by the assessee, the commercial objective of those functions and the absence of material demonstrating that the activities fall within a specified profession, it supports the assessee’s contention that the activity is a business undertaking rather than a profession covered by section 44AA(1) of the Act. 13. We find support for the aforesaid reasoning from the decision of the Mumbai Bench of the Tribunal in Manoj Rajaram Sharma v.ITO (2026) – ITA No.8313/Mum/2025, dated 20.03.2026, wherein the Tribunal examined the applicability of sections 44AD and 44ADA. Similarly, Mumbai Bench of the Tribunal in in Vishnu Dattatraya Ponkshe v. CPC, ITA No. 1570/Mum/2023, dated 29.08.2023, wherein the Tribunal considered the applicability of sections 44AD and 44ADA to receipts from consultancy-related activities on which tax had been deducted under section 194J of the Act. The Tribunal held that the receipts could not be brought within section 44ADA merely on the basis of the TDS treatment where the assessee’s activities did not fall within the professions contemplated u/s.44AA(1). The said decision supports the proposition that the nature of the activity and its statutory classification must be examined independently, rather than determining eligibility for presumptive taxation solely on the basis of the section under which the payer deducted tax. 14. We are, therefore, unable to accept the contention of the Revenue that the deduction of tax at source u/s.194J is sufficient to establish the professional character of the assessee’s receipts. The provisions relating to tax deduction at source govern the withholding obligations of the payer, whereas the applicability of sections 44AD and 44ADA must be determined in accordance with the substantive provisions governing the computation of income of the recipient. Even where the payer has categorised a payment as being subject to section 194J, the tax authorities are required to examine the actual nature of the services rendered before determining whether the receipts constitute income from a specified profession. We further observe that section 44AD(6) excludes from its operation persons carrying on a profession referred to in section 44AA(1), persons earning income in the nature of commission or brokerage, and persons carrying on agency business. In the present case, the material placed before us describes the receipts as arising from contractual engagements for institutional ranking support, advisory facilitation, coordination and allied services. The lower authorities have not established that the assessee’s receipts are in the nature of commission or brokerage or that the assessee is carrying on an agency business. Their principal objection is that the services involve professional consultancy. As discussed above, the nature and scope of the activities, as disclosed in the material available on record, do not establish that the assessee is carrying on a specified profession within the meaning of section 44AA(1) of the Act. 15. Having regard to the overall nature of the activities, we find that the assessee undertakes commercial and institutional support functions aimed at helping educational institutions maintain and improve their rankings among similar institutions. The activities are directed towards coordination, performance benchmarking, data organisation, reporting and facilitating institutional improvement. They do not, on the material available on record, constitute the rendering of a specified professional service contemplated u/s.44AA(1). We accordingly hold that the impugned receipts cannot be treated as professional receipts merely because the agreements use expressions such as advisory services, consultancy or global ranking services. 16. The next issue concerns the estimation of income at 70% of gross receipts. We find that the AO adopted the said rate principally on the ground that the assessee was carrying on professional activities and that section 44ADA was applicable. However, once the foundational premise regarding the applicability of section 44ADA is found to be unsustainable, the estimation made solely on that basis cannot survive. Section 44ADA prescribes a presumptive framework for eligible specified professions; it does not authorise the AO to adopt an arbitrary rate of 70% merely because the receipts arise from advisory or consultancy-related activities. The lower authorities have not established a sustainable statutory basis for such recharacterisation. Accordingly, the rejection of the assessee’s claim and the consequential addition made by estimating income at 70% of gross receipts are not justified. 17. In view of the foregoing discussion, we hold that, on the facts and material available on record, the assessee’s activity of providing global ranking support, institutional facilitation and coordination services is in the nature of business activity and has not been established to constitute a specified profession covered u/s.44AA(1) of the Act. Consequently, the invocation of section 44ADA and the consequential estimation of income at 70% of gross receipts are unsustainable. The income returned by the assessee u/s.44AD is accordingly directed to be accepted by setting aside the order of the ld.CIT(A). 18. As regards the assessee’s claim for credit of tax deducted at source, we direct the Jurisdictional Assessing Officer (JAO) to verify the assessee’s Form 26AS and allow the credit of TDS in accordance with law. 19. In the result, the appeal of the assessee is partly allowed for statistical purposes. Order pronounced in the open court on 12th October, 2026 at Chennai.

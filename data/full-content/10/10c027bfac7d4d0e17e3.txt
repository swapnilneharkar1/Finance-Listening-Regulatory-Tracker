Summary: Direct and regular plans of the same mutual fund scheme ordinarily hold the same underlying portfolio but differ in distribution-related expenses. A direct plan generally has a lower total expense ratio (TER), potentially improving an investor’s net return over time, although neither plan guarantees returns. Investors should compare the same scheme, option and period, using current scheme disclosures rather than unrelated fund performance. A regular plan may provide distributor assistance, but investors should distinguish distribution services from regulated investment advice and evaluate whether the services justify the additional ongoing cost. Before switching accumulated units from regular to direct, check applicable capital-gains taxation, exit loads, holding periods and any scheme-specific restrictions. A switch is generally processed as redemption from one plan and purchase into the other, potentially triggering tax even where the underlying scheme remains unchanged. The decision should reflect investment horizon, service needs, portfolio suitability and transaction costs rather than TER alone. Key Takeaway Show all 13 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); How Direct and Regular Mutual Fund Plans Differ Direct and regular plans are two routes to investing in the same mutual fund scheme. The direct plan does not embed distributor commission in the same way as the regular plan; consequently, its expense ratio is generally lower. Both plans ordinarily follow the same investment objective and underlying portfolio, but their net asset values (NAVs) and reported returns differ because their expenses differ. The difference should be assessed using the same growth or income-distribution option and identical measurement dates. SEBI-related disclosures and investor information are discussed in TaxGuru’s coverage of direct and regular mutual fund plans. Compare Total Expense Ratios and Actual Returns The total expense ratio reflects recurring scheme expenses expressed as a percentage of assets. Check the latest TER published by the asset management company and verify whether the figures relate to the same scheme and option. TER can change over time. Compare NAV-based returns over identical periods, remembering that historical performance does not predict future results. Illustration: The Long-Term Effect of a 0.75 Percentage-Point Expense Difference Assume an initial investment of ₹10,00,000, an illustrative annual gross return of 10%, and constant annual expenses of 0.50% for the direct plan and 1.25% for the regular plan. For a simplified annual compounding comparison, the assumed net returns are 9.50% and 8.75%, respectively. After 20 years, the illustrative values are approximately ₹61.4 lakh and ₹53.5 lakh, a difference of about ₹7.9 lakh. This is a hypothetical calculation, not a prediction; actual TERs, market returns, timing and taxation will differ. When Distributor Support May Be Worth the Cost A regular plan may suit an investor who values assistance with transactions, servicing and periodic reviews. However, a distributor’s role should not automatically be equated with independent fiduciary advice. Investors seeking personalised investment advice should examine the provider’s regulatory status, fees, conflicts and scope of services. See TaxGuru’s discussion of investment advisers and financial planning. Tax and Exit-Load Consequences of Switching Plans Moving existing holdings from a regular plan to a direct plan is generally treated as redemption followed by a fresh purchase, even where both plans belong to the same scheme. The redemption may realise taxable capital gains, subject to the classification of the mutual fund units, the holding period, applicable financial-year law and the investor’s circumstances. Exit loads, if any, may also apply. The acquisition date and cost basis of newly allotted units should be recorded carefully. See the discussion of mutual fund switching and taxation. For existing investments with significant unrealised gains, compare the present value of expected future TER savings with the immediate tax and exit-load cost. Investing future contributions through the direct plan, without immediately redeeming older regular-plan units, may be an alternative worth evaluating. Practical Checks Before Choosing or Switching Identify the exact scheme, plan, option, folio and investment horizon. Compare current TER and historical NAV-based returns for matching periods. Document the distributor’s actual services, any separate advisory fee and relevant regulatory registration. Calculate estimated capital gains and exit loads before switching existing units. Retain scheme documents, account statements, transaction confirmations, cost records and calculations. Reassess suitability, asset allocation and risk rather than selecting a plan solely because its TER is lower. Frequently Asked Questions Do Direct and Regular Plans Hold Different Securities? Ordinarily, no. Both plans of the same scheme share its investment portfolio and objective, although their expense ratios, NAVs and net returns differ. Is a Direct Plan Always the Better Choice? Not necessarily for every investor. Lower recurring expenses are valuable, but the investor must consider whether adequate investment selection, advice and ongoing support are otherwise available and at what cost. Does Switching from Regular to Direct Trigger Tax? Generally, yes, where the switch results in redemption of existing units and allotment of new units. Whether tax is actually payable depends on the gain or loss and the applicable tax provisions. Can I Start New Investments in a Direct Plan Without Switching Existing Units? Generally, an investor can choose a direct plan for new eligible investments while retaining existing regular-plan units, subject to scheme and platform arrangements. Should I Compare Expense Ratios Alone? No. Compare the same scheme and option, actual services, portfolio suitability, investment horizon, taxation, exit loads and other transaction costs. Key Takeaway The recurring cost difference between direct and regular plans can materially affect long-term wealth. Compare like-for-like scheme disclosures and decide whether distributor services justify the cost.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Turbo Energy Pvt. Ltd. Vs Chairman (Madras High Court) Summary: The Madras High Court, in Turbo Energy Pvt. Ltd. Vs Chair-man, CBIC & Ors., dismissed two writ petitions seeking protection against coercive action during a customs investigation and disclosure of complete and unedited video recordings of search proceedings, investigation and statements recorded from the company’s employees. The Court held that interference with the ongoing investigation concerning the alleged misclassification of imported turbine wheel raw parts was unwarranted, considering the statutory powers available to the customs authorities under Section 108 of the Customs Act, 1962. The petitioner, a manufacturer of turbochargers and turbocharger components, imported turbine wheel raw parts under Customs Tariff Item (CTI) 7508 9090 as articles of nickel and nickel alloys. Un-til 2015, the petitioner had classified these goods under Customs Tariff Heading (CTH) 8414. The change in classification was communicated to the customs authorities through a letter dated 6 Au-gust 2015. Consequently, the imports were subjected to provisional assessment between 6 August 2015 and 21 June 2017. The Assistant Commissioner of Customs, through Order-in-Original No. 406/2017 dated 21 June 2017, approved the classification under CTI 7508 9090 and finalised 70 Bills of Entry. The classifica-tion was supported by a laboratory report establishing nickel content of 66.04% and a Process Veri-fication Certificate dated 27 April 2016 issued by the jurisdictional Central Excise authorities. The pe-titioner contended that the classification had attained finality since the Department had not chal-lenged the assessment order before any appellate forum. The Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI), Mumbai Zonal Unit, subsequently initiated an investi-gation into the classification of the imported goods and conducted searches at the petitioner’s cor-porate office and manufacturing facilities on 2 and 3 September 2026. Statements of the company’s officials, including its Chief Financial Officer, were recorded. According to the petitioner, its CFO was confronted with the prospect of making a voluntary payment of approximately ₹18 crore towards alleged differential customs duty. Three consignments imported under Bills of Entry dated 20, 28 and 29 August 2026, collectively val-ued at approximately ₹1.31 crore, were seized under Section 110 of the Customs Act, 1962. The petitioner submitted representations dated 18 and 23 September 2026 seeking intervention by the authorities. In the absence of a response, the petitioner approached the High Court seeking directions against coercive action, including compulsory payment of differential duty, interest and penalty under Section 28(5) of the Customs Act. The Revenue contended that the imported turbine wheels were precision castings having the essen-tial character of machinery parts and were consequently classifiable under CTI 8414 9090 rather than CTI 7508 9090. Reliance was placed on the World Customs Organization Explanatory Notes and Circular No. 225/59/96-CX dated 1 July 1996 concerning the classification of castings possessing the essential character of machinery parts. According to the Department, the alleged misclassification resulted in non-payment of customs duty amounting to approximately ₹15.75 crore from January 2022 onwards. The respondents further alleged that the petitioner had incorrectly represented the manufacturing process at the time of finalisation of provisional assessments. The panchanama recorded during the investigation indicated that the imported turbine wheels were precision cast and did not undergo rough machining. The Revenue alleged that material facts concerning the nature of the imported goods had not been disclosed and that the circumstances warranted further investigation into pos-sible wilful misstatement and suppression of facts. The petitioner also contended that payments aggregating to ₹12 crore, comprising ₹2 crore and ₹10 crore, had been made under compulsion. The Department disputed the allegation of coercion. The High Court observed that the dispute concerning the nature and legality of these payments was not the subject matter of the specific relief sought in the writ petitions and could be agitated and decided separately. Accordingly, the Court did not direct a refund of the amount or conclusively determine whether the payments were voluntary. The High Court examined several judicial precedents concerning coercive tax recovery, statutory safeguards governing the issuance of summons and the scope of judicial interference with investiga-tions. These included Sanmar Foundaries Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise & Customs, Century Plyboards (India) Ltd. Vs Union of India, Ganesh Chandra Mrig Vs Su-perintendent (Prev.) and Vodafone Essar South Ltd. Vs Union of India. These authorities discussed the requirement of statutory authority for tax collection and safeguards against unnecessary sum-moning of senior managerial personnel. The Court also considered Media Graphics & Another Vs Commissioner of Cus-toms and Union of India Vs Padam Narain Aggarwal, concerning the scope of Section 108 of the Customs Act. It observed that customs authorities possess statutory powers to summon persons and record statements for investigating suspected violations. The alle-gations concerning the manufacturing process, precision casting and classification of the imported goods required examination during the investigation. Considering the competing claims regarding tariff classification and the allegations of suppression of material facts, the High Court declined to interfere with the investigation. It also rejected the conse-quential prayer seeking restraint against compelling payment under Section 28(5), observing that such consequential action depended upon decisions to be taken at a subsequent stage. The Court did not finally adjudicate the correct tariff classification of the imported goods. Regarding the second writ petition seeking complete and unedited video footage and recordings of the search, investigation and statements, the High Court held that the request was premature. It observed that the details could be furnished after completion of the investigation and that directing their disclosure during the ongoing investigation would amount to interference with the investigative process. Accordingly, both writ petitions were dismissed without costs, and the connected miscellaneous pe-titions were closed. The dispute regarding the alleged coercive payment of ₹12 crore was left open for separate adjudication. Cases Discussed: Dabur India Ltd. A careful look at sub-sections (1) to (3) of Section 11A would show that for the invocation of clause (b) of sub-section (1), the following condi-tions are to be satisfied: (i) Any duty of excise is found to have been not levied or paid or short-levied or short-paid or erroneously refunded, for any reason other than fraud, collusion, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts or contravention of any of the provisions of the Act and the rules, with an intent to evade payment of duty. (ii) The Excise Officer should, within one year from the relevant date, service notice upon the person chargeable with duty which has not been levied or paid or short-levied or short-paid or to whom refund was erroneously made. (iii) But, before service of a notice under clause (a), the person chargeable with ‘duty may pay the amount of duty along with interest on the basis of either his own ascertainment of such duty or the duty ascertained by the Central Excise Officer. (iv) Once payment is made under clause (b) of sub-section (1), the Excise Officer shall not serve any notice under clause (a). However, if the amount paid under clause (b) of sub-section (1) falls short of the amount actually payable, then he can proceed to issue the notice as provided in clause (a). 17. A careful look at sub-sections (1) to (3) of Section 11A would show that to qualify as a payment made under Section-A(1)(b), there must either be an own ascertainment of such duty or the duty ascertained by the Central Excise Officer. What is important to note in Section 11A(1) (b) is the expression “ascertainment”. For a payment to come within the parameter of Section 11A (1)(b), the same must have been made either on the basis of own ascer-tainment by the assessee or on the basis of an ascertainment by the Central Excise Officer. In other words, if there is no ascertainment either by the assessee or by the Central Excise Officer, the De-partment cannot contend that a payment made by the assessee would fall within the scope of Sec-tion 11A(1)(b). But, in the case on hand, it is not pleaded either in the counter affidavit filed by the department before the learned Single Judge or in any other document that there was any ascertain-ment at any point of time, either by the assessee or by the Central Excise Officer. Therefore, the payment of Rs. 7.53 crores made by the appellant, either on their own accord or under threat of co-ercion, will not quality as a payment made in terms of Section 11A(1)(b). … 20. In the case on hand, there was nothing except an inspection and verification. It was never the case of the respondents that they found out duty evasion in the course of investigation and that when they were ready to serve a notice under sub-section (5), the appellant made payment in terms of sub-section (6). Had this been their contention, the respond-ents ought to have shown the break­up of the amount of Rs. 7.53 crores into (i) duty; (2) interest; and (iii) penalty. In the absence of any of these details, the respondents cannot contend that the payment was in terms of sub-section (6). As a matter of fact, sub-section (7) is somewhat similar to sub­section (2) and it prohibits the Central Excise Officer from serving any notice in respect of the amount paid. But, in the case on hand, a notice has been served after the learned Single Judge dis-posed of the writ petition, with liberty to the respondents. Therefore, it is clear that the amount of Rs. 7.53 crores paid by the appellant, will not fall either under Section 11A(1)(b) or under Section 11A(6). If it will not fall under any of these two provisions, it cannot be taken to be a payment made in relation to any statutory provision. Therefore, irrespective of whether the amount was paid under coercion or voluntarily, the respondents have no business to retain the same. Hence the contention of the learned Standing Counsel for the respondents that the payment was authorised by law has to be stated only to be rejected. 21. The writ appeal is allowed, the order of the learned Judge is set aside and the writ petition filed by the appellant is allowed, directing the respondents to refund the amount of Rs. 7.53 crores, collected from the appellant. We give a time of four weeks to the re-spondents to make payment of the amount to the appellant, from the date of receipt of a copy of this order. If the respondents fail to make payment within four weeks from the date of receipt of a copy of this order, the respondents will become liable to pay interest at 6% per annum on the said amount from the date of expiry of the time for payment stipulated herein. No costs. Connected M.P. (MD) No. 1 of 2014 is closed.” 25.

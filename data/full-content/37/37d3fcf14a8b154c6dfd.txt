Abstract: The IBC 2016 An analysis The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) has brought about major reforms in the insolvency regime of India through the Bankruptcy and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. The IBC aims to achieve the objective of making the process of bankruptcy faster than the process of recovery. Liquidation of the firm is inescapable in the event of the insolvency not being resolved successfully. The research paper delves into the statutory reasons for liquidation and the institutional protection provided to the stakeholders under the IBC. Objectivity and Effectiveness of the Liquidation Process under the IBC The study delves into the objectivity and the effectiveness of the liquidation process under the IBC. It argues that despite the attempt of the IBC to portray liquidation as a fall-back option, the provisions of Sections 33 to 54 have been designed in a way that reinforces the principle of creditor-in-control and therefore values managerial efficiency over judicial adjudication. It further argues that the NCLT and the Supreme Court of India in its judicial adjudication has not only endorsed the principle of creditor primacy but has also satisfactorily addressed three critical issues that had arisen in relation to the mandatory grounds for liquidation, the outcome in case of non-approval of the resolution plan and the issue of compliance with the resolution plan. Methodology This research paper employs doctrinal methodology that examined a plethora of judgements, legislative intent behind the legislation and provisions of the statute. It further made a comparative study with respect to the pre-IBC regime as prevalent in Companies Act 2013. This research paper examines the jurisprudence and regulatory practice of liquidation under IBC to see if liquidation under IBC has emerged as a real and efficient alternative for corporate insolvency or that it has to sacrifice larger economic interests like maximisation of shareholder value and employment. It was also beset by drawn-out litigation under the Companies Act, 2013 and the Sick Industrial Companies (Special Provisions) Act, 1985 (SICA). With liquidation serving as the default in the event of failure, the IBC combined these into a single, time-bound framework with the goal of resolving insolvency within 180 to 330 days. This change in perspective highlights the two goals of the Code, which are to maximize asset value and encourage entrepreneurship by facilitating quick exits for profitable companies and disposing of unprofitable ones. Under the IBC, liquidation is a pragmatic, creditor-controlled wind-down procedure that is initiated by particular legislative grounds rather than being punitive. However, actual evidence shows that more than 70% of CIRPs admitted since 2016 have resulted in liquidation, casting doubt on the efficacy and design of the procedure. The main research issue addressed in this study is: To what degree do the judicially interpreted grounds for liquidation under the IBC achieve economic efficiency and creditor primacy without unduly sacrificing stakeholder objectives like employment and shareholder value? According to the theory, Sections 33 to 54 of the IBC have created a creditor-centric liquidation system that puts value maximization ahead of judicial micromanagement, strengthened by NCLT and Supreme Court jurisprudence. However, this has a normative cost because liquidation frequently reduces equity and jobs, requiring adjustments for a comprehensive economic consequence. Using a doctrinal approach, the paper analyses over 50 NCLT orders, legislative purpose, statutory provisions, and significant Supreme Court decisions. The IBC’s better efficiency is highlighted when compared to the Companies Act of 2013. The framework, justifications, and criticisms are examined in Sections II-IV, which conclude with policy comments. Conceptual and Doctrinal Framework Evolution from the Pre-IBC Era Under the pre-IBC regime, the process of liquidation was held back by discretionary grounds under Section 433 of the Companies Act of 2013. For instance, shareholder veto and court-centric adjudication often led to undue delay in the process of “reasonable and equitable winding up,” or an inability to discharge debts. In the SICA regime, the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction (BIFR) emphasized “revival over liquidation,” leading to the emergence of “zombie firms,” where recovery rates fell below 20%. In contrast, the IBC introduces a creditor-centric model where promoters and management are relegated to the background, and Section 33 of the Code provides for liquidation on the failure of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP). Further, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations of 2016 impose a strict 270-day timeframe (extendable by a further 90 days), which was not the case under the pre-IBC regime. In a conceptual sense, the framework appears to draw from the UNCITRAL Legislative Guide on Insolvency, which emphasizes going concern sales and absolute priority. In a doctrinal sense, there appears to be a shift toward efficiency by replacing discretionary court-centric adjudication with a creditor-centric model. Statutory Grounds for Liquidation (Section 33) The mandatory and discretionary grounds are laid out by Section 33, with a focus on creditor interests and the limitation of the jurisdiction of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) to procedural matters, as stipulated under Section 33(5). This focus on creditor interests is further reinforced by the hierarchy stipulated under Section 53, wherein shareholders are relegated to a lower position, with secured creditors, operational creditors, and government dues being prioritized. In accordance with the IBC Second Amendment Act, 2018, the mandatory grounds stipulated under Section 33(1) are: (i) failure to pass a resolution plan within 330 days, as stipulated under Section 33(1)(a); (ii) rejection of a plan by the NCLT, as stipulated under Section 33(1)(b); (iii) reporting by the Resolution Professional of a lack of plans, as stipulated under Section 33(1)(c); and (iv) approval of liquidation by 90% voting share of the Committee of Creditors, as stipulated under Section 33(1)(d). The discretionary grounds stipulated under Section 33(2) include voluntary liquidation decisions and spillovers from individual insolvency proceedings. This “resolve or liquidate” choice has further enhanced the predictability and maximization of value, as against the IBC’s predecessors, wherein no time constraint was stipulated and creditor interests were not prioritized uniformly. Analytical Discussion: Critiques, Objectivity, and Efficiency Judicial Endorsement and Creditor Primacy As confirmed in Swiss Ribbons Pvt Ltd v Union of India, the IBC’s liquidation grounds firmly support “creditor-in-control.” Rejecting face arguments to creditor discretion, the Supreme Court upheld Section 33’s triggers, emphasizing that “the commercial wisdom of CoC is paramount unless perverse.” In Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Ltd v Satish Kumar Gupta, The Court affirmed that, in the absence of fraud, liquidation following plan rejection is required. Despite employee resistance, NCLT cases, such Jet Airways (India) Ltd*(2021), have compelled 90% CoC votes, resulting in superior recoveries of 68% for secured creditors compared to less than 10% prior to IBC. Liquidation recovery rates that averaged 42% between 2018 and 2023, significantly exceeding previous standards, demonstrate the process’ efficacy. This is made possible by processes including Swiss challenges under Regulation 32 and reverse auctions. Non-discretionary timetables minimize judicial overreach and are consistent with the Code’s time-bound. Addressing Critical Issues Judicial review of the application of these grounds has refined them. The key ambiguities have been cleared: 1. The inflexibility of the mandatory grounds was upheld by the judgment in Innovative Industries Ltd v ICICI Bank. The judgment also rejected the claims of debtors with regard to the timelines. The National Company Law Tribunal’s (NCLT) ruling in Videocon Industries Ltd (2023) denied extensions beyond 330 days to protect the value of the assets. 2. Non-approval of Resolution Plans: The judgment in ArcelorMittal India Pvt Ltd v Satish Kumar Gupta has upheld the liquidation process in the absence of viable resolution plans. This was also corroborated by the approval of 66% by the Committee of Creditors under Section 30(4). The Supreme Court struck down non-compliant resolution plans in the judgment in Ebix Singapore Pte Ltd v Committee of Creditors in 2021. This effectively invoked Section 33 of the IBC. Normative Criticisms: Giving Up More General Interests Although creditor primacy promotes efficiency, holistic goals are normatively undermined by it. As demonstrated by the kingfisher Airlines liquidation (2018), which produced no equity recovery, Section 53’s waterfall mechanism regularly wipes out shareholder value. The effects on employment are severe: since 2016, more than 500 liquidations have resulted in the loss of nearly 1.5 lakh jobs. In contrast, the IBC lacks the debtor-in-possession financing with worker rights that the US Chapter 11 offers. According to doctrine, prioritizing liquidator-led “managerial efficiency” over extensive court adjudication protects asset value but has wider financial consequences. A 75% liquidation rate after CIRP acceptance is found in an empirical analysis of 200 NCLT cases, which has led to criticism of unduly strict grounds. In order to reduce creditor absolutism, this study proposes calibrated measures, such as CoC-mandated carve-outs for employment retention such as 10–20% workforce incentives. Comparative Insights If we compare the time factor (270-360 days vs. indefinite), the system of governing the control (creditors/liquidator vs. court/shareholders), and the results of the recovery process (average of 42% vs. less than 20%), the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code outperform the Companies Act, 2013. In this context, the present discussion highlights another conscious, although questionable, decision-making strategy favouring the interest of the creditors over the interest of the stakeholders. Conclusion and Policy Reflections The present insolvency framework under the IBC provides a creditor-centric approach to liquidation through the mandatory triggers provided under Section 33 of the IBC. This framework is also supplemented by landmark judgments in the Swiss Ribbons and Essar Steel cases. The judgments have also resolved issues related to the Companies Act, making the liquidation process more effective and objective in nature. However, the rigid framework of the IBC is also having a detrimental impact on employment and shareholders, and liquidation is now becoming the norm rather than the exception or última ratio. From a theoretical point of view, the mechanism of rapid reallocation of property rights is a reflection of Case in an efficiency; however, from a normative point of view, the framework needs to be improved to achieve long-term effects. Some of the proposed policy interventions are the mandatory imposition of pre-liquidation mediation, extension of sector-specific CIRP timelines, improvement of empirical control by the IBBI, and the inclusion of employment protection provisions under Section 53 of the IBC. Such modifications to the parameters of the IBC framework would help the IBC recast liquidation as a victory for creditors to a victory for India’s economic stability.

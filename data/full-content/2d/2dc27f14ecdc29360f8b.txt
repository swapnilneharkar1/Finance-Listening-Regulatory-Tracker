The dispute arose when the Mobile Squad intercepted a vehicle at Budaun on 21 July 2023 carrying 250 quintals of sugar valued at ₹9,16,429. The original tax invoices and e-way bills mentioned Bareilly as the destination, whereas the driver stated that the goods were being transported towards Etah. Treating this discrepancy as transportation to an undeclared destination, the Department imposed a penalty of ₹91,644. The Additional Commissioner (Appeals), Bareilly, set aside the penalty by an Order-in-Appeal dated 15 October 2024, prompting the Department to approach the Tribunal. The Department argued that the taxpayer had violated the statutory requirements governing transportation of goods, particularly Section 68, Section 129 and Rule 138 of the GST Rules, by transporting the consignment towards a destination different from that recorded in the original invoices and e-way bills. It further contended that the original supplier, M/s L.H. Sugar Factory Limited, Pilibhit, had no declared branch, warehouse or additional place of business at Etah and that the subsequent sale documents were produced only during the appellate proceedings. According to the Department, the goods could have escaped detection and taxation had the vehicle not been intercepted. The taxpayer explained that the consignment was originally intended for Bareilly but could not enter the city because of traffic restrictions imposed during the Kanwar Yatra. Consequently, the vehicle was diverted and the goods were subsequently sold to M/s Faqir Chand Panna Lal, Etah, a registered purchaser. The taxpayer supported this explanation with the original purchase invoices, e-way bills, subsequent e-invoice, e-way bill dated 21 July 2023 and bank statements establishing the purchase and subsequent sale transactions. Examining the documentary evidence, the Tribunal observed that the original transportation documents correctly disclosed the description, quantity, value and identity of the goods. The subsequent transaction was supported by an identifiable registered purchaser, valid transaction documents and banking records. The Department had produced no independent evidence establishing that the subsequent sale was fictitious, that the goods were intended for an unaccounted supply, that they had been removed from the books of account or that taxable turnover had been suppressed. The absence of a declared branch or warehouse of the original supplier at Etah did not establish any irregularity in the subsequent sale to an independent registered purchaser. Referring to the Supreme Court decision in Assistant Commissioner (ST) & Others Vs Satyam Shivam Papers Pvt. Ltd. and decisions of the Allahabad and Calcutta High Courts, the Tribunal emphasised that the surrounding circumstances, bona fide explanations and supporting documents must be considered before inferring an intention to evade tax. A discrepancy in destination could not automatically be treated as conclusive evidence of tax evasion. The Tribunal also held that the departmental appeal was maintainable despite the monetary-limit objection because the controversy involved a recurring legal issue concerning GST transportation enforcement. The Tribunal concluded that the diversion caused by Kanwar Yatra traffic restrictions was a relevant circumstance beyond the taxpayer’s control and that the subsequent documented sale adequately explained the movement of goods towards Etah. The Department’s allegation that the goods might have escaped taxation but for interception was based on apprehension rather than concrete evidence. It had failed to establish any unaccounted supply, suppression of taxable turnover or intention to evade GST. Accordingly, the Tribunal held that the discrepancy between the original destination and the actual movement of the vehicle, when considered alongside the explanation and documentary evidence, did not justify sustaining the penalty under Section 129. Finding no legal or factual infirmity in the First Appellate Authority’s decision, the Tribunal dismissed the departmental appeal and upheld the Order-in-Appeal dated 15 October 2024, thereby confirming the deletion of the ₹91,644 penalty. The Tribunal examined the original invoices, e-way bills, subsequent sale documents and banking evidence. It found that the description, quantity, value and identity of the sugar were disclosed in the original transportation documents. The subsequent sale was supported by an identifiable registered purchaser and corresponding transaction records. The Department had not established that the goods were intended for an unaccounted supply, that the subsequent transaction was fictitious, that the goods had been removed from the books of account or that any taxable turnover had been suppressed. The absence of a declared branch of the original supplier at Etah did not independently establish that the subsequent sale to a separate purchaser was fictitious. Cases Discussed 1.

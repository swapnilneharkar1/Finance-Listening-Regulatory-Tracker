(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Ramjit Singh Kardam & Ors. Vs Sanjeev Kumar & Ors., examined the legality of the selection of 1,983 Physical Training Instructors (PTIs) by the Haryana Staff Selection Commission. The appeals challenged the Punjab and Haryana High Court’s judgment dated 30 September 2013, which affirmed the Single Judge’s decision dated 11 September 2012 setting aside the recruitment. The central controversy concerned repeated changes in selection methodology, non-disclosure of evaluation criteria, arbitrary allocation of interview marks and alleged dilution of merit. The Supreme Court considered whether candidates who participated in recruitment could subsequently challenge the process and whether the Commission had exercised its statutory powers lawfully. The recruitment originated from Advertisement No. 6 of 2006 dated 20 July 2006, inviting applications for several categories of posts, including 1,983 PTI vacancies under Category No. 23. The advertisement authorised the Commission to shortlist applicants through a written examination or rational criteria. Approximately 20,836 applications were received. A notification dated 28 December 2006 prescribed a written examination comprising 100 multiple-choice questions carrying 200 marks, followed by a viva voce carrying 25 marks. Minimum qualifying marks were fixed at 50% for general-category candidates and 45% for specified reserved categories. Candidates numbering three times the vacancies were to be shortlisted for interview on the basis of written examination performance. The Commission initially conducted the written examination in January 2007 but cancelled it following complaints of malpractice and cheating. A fresh examination was scheduled for 20 July 2008, which was also cancelled. The Commission subsequently announced shortlisting based on academic qualifications, initially proposing to invite candidates numbering eight times the advertised vacancies. This approach was later abandoned, and all eligible candidates were called for interviews. Ultimately, 15,582 candidates appeared before interview committees. Although interviews concluded in 2008, the selection result was declared only on 10 April 2010 and published on 11 April 2010. The result disclosed a selection formula allocating 60 marks to academic qualifications and 30 marks to viva voce, differing from the earlier notified examination-based process. Unsuccessful candidates challenged the selection before the Punjab and Haryana High Court. They alleged that the Commission had altered the recruitment criteria to favour particular candidates and had awarded disproportionately high interview marks to applicants with weaker academic records. Some candidates with strong academic qualifications received low viva voce marks, while others with comparatively poor academic performance received substantially higher interview scores. The petitioners also questioned the eligibility of certain selected candidates and alleged violations of Articles 14 and 16 of the Constitution. Information obtained under the Right to Information Act formed part of their challenge. The Single Judge examined the original recruitment records and quashed the selection, directing a fresh exercise. The Division Bench substantially affirmed that decision. Before the Supreme Court, the selected candidates argued that the High Court had wrongly invalidated the entire recruitment without sufficient proof of malpractice or individual wrongdoing. They maintained that the selection criteria had been uniformly applied and that unsuccessful candidates, having voluntarily participated, were barred from challenging the procedure after declaration of results. They also emphasised that many appointees had served for approximately ten years and had become overage for alternative employment. The Commission and State defended the academic-and-interview-based evaluation method, referring to practices followed in earlier recruitment. The selected candidates argued that the courts should not substitute their assessment for that of expert interview committees. The unsuccessful candidates responded that the governing criteria had never been disclosed before their participation and that they learned of the 60:30 evaluation formula only when the final result was announced. They maintained that the cancellation of the written examination, abandonment of academic shortlisting and subsequent reliance on interviews demonstrated an arbitrary departure from the notified process. They further questioned whether decisions materially changing recruitment methodology had been taken by the Commission collectively or by its Chairman acting independently. The Supreme Court framed seven principal issues concerning estoppel, alteration of recruitment criteria, the Commission’s decision-making authority, interview evaluation and the feasibility of fresh recruitment after changes to the PTI cadre. On the issue of estoppel, the Supreme Court distinguished cases where candidates knowingly participate in a disclosed selection process and subsequently challenge it merely because they are unsuccessful. Referring to Madan Lal v. State of Jammu and Kashmir, Om Prakash Shukla v. Akhilesh Kumar Shukla, Raj Kumar v. Shakti Raj and Bishnu Biswas v. Union of India, the Court explained that participation does not prevent a challenge where material irregularities or undisclosed changes become apparent only after the results. Candidates cannot be treated as having accepted selection conditions of which they were unaware. Accordingly, the unsuccessful PTI applicants were entitled to question both the subsequently disclosed criteria and the recruitment process. The Court also examined the statutory framework governing the Haryana Staff Selection Commission, including notifications issued under Article 309 of the Constitution. The Commission was empowered to devise recruitment methods and determine selection criteria, but those powers had to be exercised through lawful and rational procedures. The judgment scrutinised the successive cancellation of examinations, changes in shortlisting methodology and adoption of interview-based selection. The original records raised questions concerning the timing and authenticity of the stated criteria, the respective roles of Commission members and experts, and the absence of clear guidelines correlating interview grades with marks. These matters were material to determining whether the selection process satisfied the requirements of fairness, transparency and equality in public employment. The Supreme Court’s examination of the recruitment highlighted the distinction between legitimate administrative discretion and arbitrary alteration of selection standards. A recruiting authority may possess discretion to determine evaluation methods, but the exercise of that discretion remains subject to constitutional limitations and judicial review. The case illustrates that changes affecting comparative merit, particularly when the governing methodology is not disclosed to candidates, can undermine the integrity of public recruitment. It also demonstrates why the participation-based estoppel principle cannot be mechanically applied to shield an unlawful process from scrutiny. The legality of recruitment must be assessed against the applicable rules, the authority of the decision-maker and the actual conduct of selection. The Supreme Court upheld the invalidation of the challenged PTI recruitment, finding the process legally unsustainable. The decision reinforces the requirement that public appointments must be governed by transparent, rational and consistently applied selection standards. It also confirms that prolonged service and hardship claimed by selected candidates do not, by themselves, validate a recruitment process affected by fundamental illegality. The judgment is significant for public service recruitment disputes involving undisclosed evaluation criteria, excessive reliance on interviews, changes to selection methodology and the limits of estoppel against unsuccessful candidates. The judgment further emphasises that the Haryana Staff Selection Commission’s authority to determine recruitment procedures did not permit it to exercise that power arbitrarily. The Court examined the Commission’s successive decisions to cancel the written examinations, abandon the proposed shortlisting of candidates and ultimately adopt a selection formula based on academic qualifications and interviews. Particular concern arose from the absence of a properly disclosed evaluation framework and the manner in which interview marks were awarded. The Court scrutinised whether the decisions had been taken by the Commission as a competent collective body or independently by its Chairman. The recruitment process was required to comply with the principles of fairness, transparency and equal opportunity embodied in Articles 14 and 16 of the Constitution of India. The case demonstrates that a selection authority cannot justify fundamental procedural irregularities merely by claiming that the eventual assessment formula was uniformly applied to candidates. The Supreme Court also addressed the consequences of invalidating appointments where selected candidates had already rendered substantial service. Although the appointees argued that they had worked for several years and many had crossed the prescribed age limits for fresh recruitment, the Court considered the legality of the original selection separately from the hardship caused by its cancellation. It recognised that an unlawful recruitment process could not be validated solely because appointments had continued under interim judicial protection. At the same time, the Court adopted a balanced approach to consequential relief by permitting eligible candidates, including previously selected employees, to participate in the fresh recruitment and protecting remuneration already received from recovery. The decision thus reinforces the principle that constitutional fairness in public employment must prevail over an arbitrary selection process, while appropriate transitional relief may be granted to reduce hardship without legitimising the underlying illegality.

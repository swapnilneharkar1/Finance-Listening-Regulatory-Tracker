Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Cooperative Company Limited Vs Deputy Director (Appellate Tribunal under SAFEMA) Excise Duty Saved Through Cheating and Forgery Can Be Proceeds of Crime, and Equivalent-Value Assets Can Be Attached: SAFEMA Summary: The Appellate Tribunal under SAFEMA upheld the attachment of assets worth ₹27,31,49,904 under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA). It held that excise duty saved through criminal activity relating to scheduled offences of cheating and forgery constituted a pecuniary benefit capable of being treated as proceeds of crime. Where those proceeds were unavailable, the Tribunal held that the Enforcement Directorate could attach other property representing their value under Section 2(1)(u), including assets acquired before the alleged offences. How the Enforcement Directorate Quantified the Alleged Gain The case arose from FIRs concerning the alleged illicit supply of country liquor from the company’s distillery in Saharanpur, Uttar Pradesh. The investigation alleged that consignments were dispatched twice against permits valid for a single trip and that documents were forged to facilitate the extra supplies. The FIRs invoked provisions of the Indian Penal Code concerning cheating and forgery, among other offences. The ED then recorded an Enforcement Case Information Report and investigated under the PMLA. The ED identified 99 instances and quantified the duty allegedly avoided at ₹34,73,56,350, using the duty payable on the additional trips. Its case was that the company had absorbed the resulting benefit into its business and that the original proceeds were unavailable for attachment. By Provisional Attachment Order No. 08/2024 dated 3 May 2024, the ED attached movable and immovable properties worth ₹27,31,49,904 as property representing the value of the proceeds. The Adjudicating Authority confirmed the attachment on 9 October 2024. Duty Saved Was a Pecuniary Benefit Linked to Scheduled Offences The company argued that excise duty evasion was not itself a scheduled offence and that a loss to the exchequer could not be treated as property derived or obtained by the company. It also disputed the connection between the alleged criminal activity and the attached assets. The Tribunal focused on the cheating and forgery alleged in the predicate case and the benefit the company derived from that conduct. The duty was otherwise payable by the company. By avoiding payment through the alleged scheme, the company retained an amount that it would otherwise have paid to the State. The Tribunal treated that quantified saving as proceeds of crime even though the benefit took the form of avoided expenditure. Its conclusion therefore rested on the link between the saving and the scheduled criminal activity. Prior Acquisition Did Not Bar Attachment for Value The company relied on Pavana Dibbur v. Enforcement Directorate (2023) 15 SCC 91, arguing that assets acquired before the alleged offences could not be proceeds of those offences. The Tribunal distinguished between attaching an asset as property derived directly or indirectly from criminal activity and attaching it as “the value of any such property” under Section 2(1)(u). It treated the case as falling within the latter limb because the original proceeds were unavailable. The Tribunal relied on Dilbag Singh v. Union of India, 2024 SCC OnLine P&H 15453, which considered the Supreme Court’s ruling in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India (2023) 12 SCC 1. It also referred to its earlier decision in Sadananda Nayak v. Directorate of Enforcement, which discussed the Delhi High Court’s reasoning in Axis Bank and Prakash Industries. On that interpretation, prior acquisition alone did not insulate the company’s assets from attachment for the value of the unavailable proceeds. Corrigendum Did Not Displace the Original Attachment The company also challenged the corrigendum dated 29 August 2024, which referred to the first proviso to Section 5(1) in place of the second proviso. It argued that the change altered the jurisdictional basis after the Adjudicating Authority had issued notice. The Tribunal found that the original order had recorded an apprehension that the property could be dealt with in a manner that would frustrate confiscation. It held that the original attachment could stand under the second proviso even if the corrigendum were disregarded. The Tribunal ultimately dismissed the appeal, leaving the confirmed attachment in place. Author’s Comments The decision turns on two distinct findings. The first concerns what benefit the company obtained. The Tribunal identified the duty saved through conduct linked to scheduled offences and accepted the ED’s quantification. The second concerns which assets could secure that value once the original proceeds were unavailable. The purchase date of a substitute asset answers a different question from whether the alleged criminal activity generated a quantifiable gain. That distinction also explains the limited reach of the ruling. The Tribunal reviewed the confirmation of a provisional attachment. Its reasoning ties the attachment to the identified pecuniary benefit, the scheduled offences alleged to have generated it and the unavailability of the original proceeds. Those findings, taken together, explain why the attachment survived even though the company claimed that the assets predated the alleged offences. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF APPELLATE TRIBUNAL UNDER SAFEMA By this Appeal filed under Section 26 of the Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002 (in short “the Act of 2002”), a challenge has been made to the order dated 09.10.2024 (“Impugned Order”) passed by the Adjudicating Authority confirming the Provisional Attachment Order (“PAO”) No. 08/2024 dated 03.05.2024 in Original Complaint No. 2320/2024 whereby the movable and immovable properties of the Appellant amounting to a total of Rs. 27,31,49,904/- were provisionally attached as properties equivalent in value of proceeds of crime. Brief facts of the case 2. The case originated on a complaint being filed by Mohd. Arif Jameel, Assistant Excise Commissioner, Meerut with the Uttar Pradesh Police which resulted in registration of FIRs No. 0098/2021 and 0097/2021 both dated 04.03.2021 under Sections 420, 467, 468, 471 and 477-A of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (in short “IPC”) at the Kotwali Saharanpur Police Station. It was against Ashwini Upadhyay and directors/employees of M/s Cooperative Company Limited (hereinafter “M/s CCL”) along with the owners of the CL-2 Warehouse and other accused persons for illicit supply of country liquor twice on a single bilty/gate pass to various CL-2 Warehouses across Uttar Pradesh without proper documentation. The said country liquor was subsequently supplied to liquor shops/markets without any legitimate paperwork to evade excise duty causing significant financial loss to the Excise Department and illicit gains to the accused persons. Moreover, the said investigation was subsequently assumed by the Special Investigation Team, Lucknow and two additional FIRs No. 03/2021 and 04/2021, both dated 06.03.2021 were registered. 3. In pursuance to the FIRs disclosing a predicate offence, the Respondent recorded ECIR No. ECIR/LKZO/23/2021 and caused investigation under the Act of 2002. The investigation resulted in seizure of various documents. The statements under Section 50 of the Act of 2002 were also recorded which elaborated the modus operandi for generation of proceeds of crime and deployment thereof. 4. The investigation revealed the followings: a. M/s CCL had license from the Uttar Pradesh Excise Department and was involved in production of various types of liquor including country liquor at its manufacturing unit located at Tapri, Saharanpur for supply of liquor to various CL-2 Warehouse for subsequent sale through retailers. b. M/s CCL dispatched 99 consignments of country liquor ‘Shabnam Angoori’ to CL-2 Warehouses situated in Unnao, Kanpur Nagar, Sambhal, Bareilly, Jaunpur and Mahoba Districts from 01.04.2020 to 28.02.2021 by undertaking double trips misusing PD-25A passes and forging documents with intent to evade state excise duty of Rs. 34,73,56,350/-. The aforesaid was admitted by the two shareholders of M/s CCL namely Pranay Aneja and Rishabh Aneja in their statements recorded under Section 50 of the Act of 2002. c. In fact, the truck bearing number UP11 BT 0935 carrying 1500 boxes of country liquor was issued a gate pass dated 01.03.2021 for supply of liquor to warehouse of Ajay Jaiswal at Unnao on 02.03.2021. The said truck was caught by the team of officers of the Uttar Pradesh Special Task Force and officials of Excise Department when it returned to the factory premises of M/s CCL at Saharanpur on 03.03.2021 for reloading the liquor after delivery of previous liquor supply. d. M/s CCL made its supply using the same PD-25A pass twice despite the fact that this pass was issued by the Uttar Pradesh Excise Department to carry liquor from M/s CCL to the destination of CL-2 godowns once only. Similarly, duplicate bar codes/QR codes were used to make double trip within the time period instead of one. Moreover, instructions were issued to truck drivers to switch off the GPS and also to the attendant of factory to switch off the CCTV when the trucks were loaded with illegal liquor before leaving the factory premise of M/s CCL to escape the surveillance of the authorities. e. Thus, M/s CCL in collusion with the officials made two trips by paying the excise duty for just one trip and consequently benefitted from excise duty payable on one trip. The total duty evasion was identified for 99 double trips undertaken in order to benefit M/s CCL and the same was calculated to be of Rs. 34,73,56,350/- which is the proceeds of crime. 5. The investigation revealed that the proceeds of crime were earned by M/s CCL in the form of unaccounted cash by evading excise duty. It was exhausted/integrated into the regular course of business or used in the operation of M/s CCL. Consequently, the Respondent provisionally attached the movable and immovable properties of the Appellant for worth Rs. 27,31,49,904/- as property for “value thereof” vide the Provisional Attachment Order which has been confirmed by the Adjudicating Authority vide the Impugned Order and aggrieved by the same, the present Appeal has been preferred. Analysis & Findings 31. I have considered the rival submissions of the parties and perused the records carefully. 32. The brief facts pertaining to registration of the FIRs involving offences under Sections 420, 467, 468, 471 and 477-A of IPC with the Kotwali Saharanpur Police Station have been given in the opening paras of this order. The FIRs were lodged naming Ashwini Upadhyay and other directors of the Appellant Company along with the owners of the CL-2 Warehouse where the country liquor was transported, as accused persons. The allegation is for use of single gate pass of the Excise Department for two trips while it was issued only for one trip and, therefore, a case of cheating with the Excise Department was alleged. After investigation of the case, the police filed a Chargesheet finding a case against the accused for commission of predicate offence. The amount of evasion of excise duty was found to be of Rs. 34,73,56,350/- and accordingly provisional attachment of the properties of the Appellant was caused though it is only to the extent of Rs. 27,31,49,904/-. 33. The Appellant was producing country liquor and transferring it to different warehouses. For transfer of country liquor, a gate pass issued by the Excise Department is required. It is along with the route from which liquor is to be transported. The country liquor was transported by the Appellant to CL-2 godowns. The allegation is that after unloading the country liquor at CL-2 godowns, the transported vehicle returned back to the Appellant Company’s factory where country liquor was again filled for its transfer to CL-2 godowns on the same gate pass for the second trip though it was issued for single transit. The use of one gate pass twice was to evade payment of excise duty on the second trip. The aforesaid fact was revealed when the driver made a statement to support the allegation with endorsement of receipt of money for carrying two trips on one gate pass issued by the Excise Department. The liability for payment of excise duty was on the Appellant, therefore, it was the direct beneficiary for evasion of the excise duty. The provisional attachment of the properties was caused finding a case of money-laundering. 34. Ld. Counsel for the Appellant has raised manifold arguments to question the Impugned Order. It was stated that after issuance of the PAO and even the OC sent to the Adjudicating Authority to seek confirmation of the PAO, a Corrigendum in the Original Complaint was issued on 29.08.2024. The said Corrigendum was issued to bring the PAO under the first proviso to Section 5(1) of the Act of 2002 instead of keeping it under the second proviso to the aforesaid provision. It is said to be without any authority and otherwise subsequent to notice issued by the Adjudicating Authority under Section 8(1) of the Act of 2002. 35. I have considered the submissions made by the parties and find that initially the PAO and OC were issued in reference to second proviso of Section 5(1) of the Act of 2002 alleging that if the provisional attachment of the properties is not caused immediately, it may be dealt with to frustrate the proceedings under the Act of 2002. The Corrigendum was issued subsequently finding that after investigation by the Police, Chargesheet has already been filed, thus even the first proviso to Section 5(1) of the Act of 2002 could have been invoked. The Corrigendum was not to rectify a mistake and otherwise it was not such a fact which may affect the OC. If the Corrigendum is ignored, the reason to believe would remain showing apprehension of alienation of properties, if not attached immediately. Thus, the Corrigendum has not affected the Impugned Order, rather was towards the procedural compliances. 36. The issue can be dealt with from another angle in reference to the argument raised by the Appellant. If the Corrigendum could not have been issued by the Respondent than the Original Complaint and Provisional Attachment Order can be considered for provisional attachment of the properties. It is not only finding involvement of the Appellant in laundering of the proceeds of crime but apprehension of its alienation. Therefore, even if I accept the argument of Ld. Counsel for the Appellant, outcome of the order is not going to be changed. Therefore, I am unable to accept the first ground raised by Ld. Counsel for the Appellant for challenge to the Impugned Order. 37. Ld. Counsel for the Appellant further submitted that once the premise of issuing of PAO stood withdrawn, it would take away even the reason to believe formulated by the Respondent Directorate in terms of second proviso to Section 5(1) of the Act of 2002. 38. The issue aforesaid has been answered by the Respondent dealing with the first issue with the clarity that if Corrigendum to PAO is not legally sustainable, the PAO would not be illegal rather in that case the provisional attachment would remain under the second proviso to Section 5(1) of the Act of 2002. It would stand with an allegation that if the properties are not attached immediately, then properties can be dealt with to frustrate the proceedings of confiscation and, therefore, I have already clarified that even if the Corrigendum goes, original PAO would remain. Even the allegation for recording the reason would also stand and otherwise the Appellant has not disputed about filing of the Chargesheets in the predicate case prior to the PAO. A reference of provisions, which is applicable on the facts in reference to the material available on record, would not defeat the order and, therefore, both the arguments raised by Ld. Counsel for the Appellant cannot be accepted. 39. Ld. Counsel for the Appellant further submitted that no reason to believe have been recorded to the effect that properties under provisional attachment are involved in money-laundering. The argument aforesaid touches the merits of the case. The allegation against the Appellant is for use of one gate pass issued by the Excise Department for two trips for transfer of country liquor resulting in evasion of excise duty. The Appellant has not contested the allegation aforesaid, rather submitted that the proceeds of crime have not been identified, thus would not be a case of money-laundering, rather, only a case of evasion of excise duty, which is not a predicate offence. I am unable to accept the argument aforesaid not only for the reason that the FIR discloses a predicate offence but even Chargesheets have been filed in reference to those offences. The Appellant has not challenged the FIRs or ECIR successfully to raise an argument that the case does not involve a predicate offence and otherwise prima facie finding has been recorded about the involvement of Appellant in laundering the proceeds of crime. Therefore, the argument of Ld. Counsel for the Appellant that no scheduled offence exists to cause PAO cannot be accepted. It is coupled with the fact that the material exists to show a prima facie case of money laundering. It is not only for the reason that allegations have been made for commission of scheduled offence but for layering of proceeds of crime out of it, making out a case under Section 3 of the Act of 2002. It is with the prima facie case to establish scheduled offence and offence of money laundering. 40. Ld. Counsel for the Appellant further argued that no proceeds of crime were found in the possession of the Appellant thus it could not have been taken to be a case involving proceeds out of the crime. The facts on record shows allegation of evasion of excise duty. The amount required to be deposited with the Excise Department towards excise duty has been concealed and suppressed by the Appellant which has been quantified and would amount to proceeds of crime. It is not necessary that the proceeds of crime should always be available with the accused, rather he can vanish or launder but it would not be to the benefit of the person. The aforesaid observations made by the Supreme Court enable this Bench to re-examine the entire issue, as in the considered opinion of this Bench, the judgment passed in Seema Garg’s case (supra) is no longer a good law. This Court has taken this view due to the subsequent interpretation by the Supreme Court, which has superseded the legal principles established in Seema Garg’s case (supra). 3.4. It is evident that the original (unamended) definition of phrase ‘proceeds of crime’ was structured into two distinct parts. The first part relates to the property derived or obtained directly or indirectly by any person as a result of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence, whereas, the second part relates to the value of any such property where the proceeds of crime are not traceable. This clearly means that if the property derived or obtained, directly or indirectly, from the proceeds of a crime of scheduled offence is not traceable, then any property of equivalent value falls within the scope of the expression ‘proceeds of crime’. In 2015, the amendment restructured the definition into three parts to cover the property taken or held outside the country. The concept of the property of equivalent value was introduced with respect to the aforementioned properties. The amendment enabled the authorities to go after any other property of a person of equivalent value. In 2019, the scope of the phrase ‘proceeds of crime’ was further expanded so as to include other properties which were not directly or indirectly the proceeds of crime, but were held abroad, to be liable to attachment. In 2019, the explanation has been added so as to give a wider scope to the authorities. From the objects and reasons of the ‘2002 Act’, it becomes evident that the money laundering posed a serious threat not only to the financial system of the countries but also to their integrity and sovereignty. The ‘2002 Act’ was enacted to prevent money laundering and connected activities. The act of money laundering is a multi-layered, complex and complicated diversion of the property, which is required to be prevented. Consequently, the definition of proceeds of crime has undergone transformative changes from time to time so as to include all the complex acts involved in the offence of money laundering. 3.5. In Axis Bank’s case (Supra), the Delhi High Court has dissected the definition in three parts while covering tainted property and untainted property held in India; and the ‘proceeds of crime’ taken out of the country or any other property of equivalent value thereof. However, this Court is of the considered view that the definition can be divided into two broader categories namely tainted properties and untainted properties. The first part provides about the tainted properties derived or obtained directly or indirectly by any person as a result of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence. Thereafter, the untainted properties are further divided into two parts; the first part deals with a situation where the property derived or obtained from ‘proceeds of crime’ is not traceable. In the aforesaid situation the competent authority is authorized to attach or confiscate any other property of accused, which is of the same value as that of the ‘proceeds of crime’. The second sub-category is a result of amendment brought in 2015 and 2019 in the Act. It provides that if the property derived or obtained from the proceeds of crime has already been taken out of the Country then the property equivalent in value held within the Country or abroad can be made liable to be attached. This position has been explained by the Delhi High Court in an elaborate manner in Axis Bank’s case (supra) and Prakash Industries case (supra). 3.6. It is not disputed that the Supreme Court in Vijay Madanlal Chaudhary’s case (supra) was examining the scope of the ‘2002 Act’ including definition of phrase ‘proceeds of crime’. The submission put forth by the learned counsel that the phrase ‘or the value of any such property’ is superfluous was rejected by the Court and it was held that the definition of ‘proceeds of crime’ is wide enough to not only include to the property derived or obtained as a result of criminal activity related to a schedule offence but also any other property of equivalent value. 3.7. While interpreting a statutory provision, it is the bounden buty of the Courts to interpret it in manner so that each word used by the statute conveys a meaning it was assigned by the Legislature. The words used in statute are of utmost significance. The Court cannot widen or restrict the provisions on its own whims and fancies. When a statute’s language is clear and unambiguous, the general rule of interpretation of statute is to read the provision as a whole and the Court must adhere strictly to the ordinary, plain meaning of the words used. The words in a statute are used precisely, not loosely, and efforts must be made to interpret them in a literal manner to give effect to the objective of the Act. This approach of interpretation is based on the idea that the legislature’s intent is best reflected in the exact words of the statute. 3.8. Moreover, the reasoning adopted in Seema Garg’s case (supra) to the effect that there was no need to insert third part in the definition of the ‘proceeds of crime’ and that ‘value of such property’ is superfluous does not appear sound. It appears that transformative journey of the definition of phrase ‘proceeds of crime’ was not brought to the notice of the Division Bench in Seema Garg’s case (supra). In this case, the attention of the Bench was not drawn to the second broader category of the definition. On the other hand the judgments passed in Vijay Madanlal Chaudhary’s case (supra), Axis Bank’s case (supra) and Prakash Industries case (supra) completely answer the question in favour of ED. 3.10. The perusal of the definition aforesaid shows three limbs. In between every limb word “or” has been used to divide the definition into three parts. The first part refers to the property acquired or derived directly or indirectly out of the criminal activities relating to the scheduled offence. In the first part, all those properties which are acquired directly or indirectly out of criminal activities would be termed to be the proceeds of crime. The properties can be acquired directly or indirectly with the use of proceeds of crime. The use of proceeds directly or indirectly would be relevant part to fall in first part of the definition of `proceeds of crime’. To clarify the definition, it may be illustrated. Assuming in a case of dacoit, the accused secured the money and it has been used for the purchase of property, then such a property would fall in the definition of `proceeds of crime’ having been obtained out of proceeds of crime. In other case where the property was not acquired or derived by the accused himself but he passed on money out of the crime to another person and he acquired the property, then also it would be considered to be the proceeds of crime to acquire the property. In any case, there should be an element for use of the proceeds directly or indirectly obtained out of the crime and thereby the property would have nexus with the crime. 14. In second part “the value of any such property” the definition aforesaid starts with “or” after the first part referred and discussed in the para above. The second part of the definition is commonly considered to be attachment of property of equivalent value. The second part applies when the property obtained or derived directly or indirectly out of the criminal activities is not available or vanished and, therefore, to secure the proceeds of equivalent value till completion of trial, it would fall under “the value of any such property” which is commonly taken to be the property of equivalent value. The case in hand falls in the second category of the definition of “proceeds of crime” because proceeds are not available and, therefore, the property of equivalent value is attached. 15. We are unable to accept the arguments which may otherwise make second part of the definition of “proceeds of crime” to be redundant. It would be for the reason that if the property acquired prior to commission of crime would not fall in the definition of “proceeds of crime”, then the accused would commit the crime and immediately proceeds would be siphoned off or vanished so that it may not remain available for attachment. In fact, the word “the value of any such property” was inserted by the legislature to attach the property of equivalent value, if the proceeds out of commission of crime is not available or vanished. If the second limb of the definition is made dependent on the first limb, it would be literally re-writing the provision or making it redundant to a great extent and for this, jurisdiction does not lie with any court of law which includes even the Constitutional Court. They can declare any provision to be unconstitutional but till then there remains presumption of constitutional validity. 16. The relevant paras are quoted herein. “106. Among the three kinds of attachable properties mentioned above, the first may be referred to, for sake of convenience, as “tainted property” in as much as there would assumable be evidence to prima facie show that the source of (or consideration for) its acquisition is the product of specified crime, the essence of “money laundering” being its projection as “untainted property” (Section 3). This would include such property as may have been obtained or acquired by using the tainted property as the consideration (directly or indirectly). To illustrate, bribe or illegal gratification received by a public servant in form of money (cash) being undue advantage and dishonestly gained, is tainted property acquired “directly” by a scheduled offence and consequently “proceeds of crime”. Any other property acquired using such bribe as consideration is also “proceeds of crime”, it having been obtained “indirectly” from a prohibited criminal activity within the meaning of first limb of the definition. 107. In contrast, the second and third kinds of properties mentioned above would ordinarily be “untainted property” that may have been acquired by the suspect legitimately without any connection with criminal activity or its result. The same, however, are intended to fall in the net because their owner is involved in the proscribed criminality and the tainted assets held by him are not traceable, or cannot be reached, or those found are not sufficient to fully account for the pecuniary advantage thereby gained. This is why for such untainted properties (held in India or abroad) to be taken away, the rider put by law insists on equivalence in value. From this perspective, it is essential that, before the order of attachment is confirmed, there must be some assessment (even if tentative one) as to the value of wrongful gain made by the specified criminal activity unless it be not possible to do so by such stage, given the peculiar features or complexities of the case. It is more especially when the arguments raised by the appellant that the property acquired prior to the commission of crime would not fall in the definition of “proceeds of crime”. In that case, the task of the accused would become very easy to first commit the scheduled offence and after obtaining or deriving the property out of the criminal activities, immediately siphon off or vanish so that it may not remain available for attachment and otherwise the contingency aforesaid would satisfy only the first limb of definition of “proceeds of crime” leaving the second. We are thus unable to accept the argument raised by the appellant so as to make the middle part of the definition of “proceeds of crime” to be redundant”. 49. The paragraphs quoted above not only give interpretation to the definition of “proceeds of crime” but make a reference to the object of the Act of 2002 justifying the provisional attachment of property for “value thereof” to the proceeds of crime when the proceeds of crime are not found available having been vanished or laundered. Therefore, the Respondent was within its right to provisionally attach the properties for “value thereof” as the proceeds of crime were not found available with the Appellant. 50. Ld. Counsel for the Appellant has made allegation that while the properties of the Appellant have been provisionally attached, no action was taken against the warehouse owners. The argument has been made without verification of the facts. The Respondent Directorate has taken action against the warehouse owners also finding their involvement in commission of crime. During the search operation, cash was found with the warehouse owners. It was seized, apart from the material which has been allowed to be retained by the Adjudicating Authority followed by dismissal of appeal by this Tribunal. 51. In view of the above, the allegation made by the Appellant cannot sustain. The Respondent has taken action against all those involved in the case without causing any discrimination, thus I do not find any substance in the above argument and even otherwise, the Appellant has not disputed the allegations of evasion of excise duty and use of single-entry pass for double trips. 52. Ld. Counsel for the Appellant also submitted that the period of criminal activity is allegedly from 01.04.2020 to 08.02.2021 when no movement was possible due to Covid-19 lockdown. The argument has been made without disputing the core allegations of the case and even otherwise, the Appellant appears to be taking contradictory grounds as on the one hand, the Appellant states that the Appellant Company maintained receipts of expenditure in its books of account and on the other hand, the Appellant alleges that no such transportation took place as movement was restricted during Covid-19 lockdown. The transportation of country liquor during the period in question is proved by the documents showing payment of duty for one trip. 53. The next issue now remains in reference to Section 70 of the Act of 2002. It was submitted that the properties of the Appellant Company could not have been attached despite involved in the offence of money-laundering. The argument aforesaid has been raised in ignorance of the fact that the Company is considered to be an artificial person thus run by the person having authority. The company becomes liable for every deed of those who were controlling the company for the purpose of management. Even otherwise, Explanation 2 to Section 70 of the Act of 2002 clarifies that prosecution of company is independent of prosecution or conviction of an individual. Therefore, the last plea raised by Ld. Counsel for the Appellant in reference to Section 70 of the Act of 2002 is not acceptable. 54. In the light of the discussion made above, I find no merit in the appeal. It fails and is dismissed.

Summary: Solana is a proof-of-stake blockchain whose native asset, SOL, pays network transaction fees and can be delegated to validators for staking. Official Solana documentation distinguishes the base transaction fee from an optional prioritisation fee; it also explains that staking rewards depend on network issuance, stake participation, validator performance and commission rather than a guaranteed deposit rate. For Indian taxpayers in tax year 2026-27, SOL ordinarily falls within the virtual digital asset (VDA) definition under the Income-tax Act, 2025. Section 194(1), Table Sl. No. 4 taxes income from transfer of a VDA at 30%, with applicable surcharge and cess, permits only cost of acquisition as a deduction and restricts VDA-loss set-off and carry-forward. Section 393(1), Table Sl. No. 8(vi) separately provides 1% TDS on qualifying transfer consideration, with statutory threshold relief under section 393(4). Staking rewards require their own receipt-stage analysis; a subsequent sale or swap can be a separate VDA transfer. VDA service providers carrying on notified activities are subject to PMLA and FIU-IND registration and reporting requirements, but this is not equivalent to approval of SOL as an investment product. GST depends on the precise taxable supply, while overseas funding, counterparties and remittances call for separate FEMA scrutiny. Business holders must also document their accounting classification, custody, valuation, reconciliation and tax-reporting controls. SOL is the native token used for transaction fees and for staking. The token is not an equity share in a company, a bank deposit, a statutory currency or an assured-income instrument. An Indian taxpayer should distinguish a direct holding of SOL from a token issued by a separate application on Solana, from a wrapped representation of SOL, and from a contractual claim against a custodial platform. The rights and risks of these arrangements are not interchangeable. The Solana documentation explains that every network transaction pays a fee denominated in SOL. A base fee compensates signature verification, while an optional prioritisation fee may affect how the current leader schedules a transaction. The documented base fee is 5,000 lamports per signature, with one lamport representing one-billionth of a SOL. The protocol allocates half of the base fee to burning and half to the validator; the prioritisation fee goes to the validator. These are protocol mechanics, not a quoted rupee transaction charge: actual wallet or platform charges can include additional service fees. Staking, validator delegation and reward variability Solana holders may delegate SOL held in a stake account to a validator. The holder does not thereby become the validator, and validator performance, commission, network staking participation and protocol rules influence rewards. Solana’s staking FAQ describes the original inflation parameters as an 8% initial annual rate, a 15% annual disinflation parameter and a 1.5% long-run rate; those are protocol parameters rather than a fixed investor return and can be affected by governance changes. A current reward percentage shown on a wallet should not be presented as a guaranteed rate. Delegating tokens can involve activation and deactivation timing and platform-specific operational controls. An exchange may offer a custodial staking product with separate terms from native delegation. The taxpayer should distinguish network-issued rewards, promotional credits, lending consideration and platform incentives: their legal source and tax character may differ even when all are labelled “staking rewards”. VDA Tax Under the Income-tax Act, 2025 for Tax Year 2026-27 The Income-tax Act, 2025 applies from 1 April 2026. SOL is a cryptographically secured digital token and ordinarily satisfies the broad VDA definition. Section 194(1), Table Sl. No. 4 contains the special 30% tax rate for income from transfer of any VDA. Applicable surcharge and health and education cess are additional. This is not the listed-equity short-term/long-term capital-gains rate, and holding SOL for more than twelve months does not create a preferential VDA tax rate. The statutory computation allows the cost of acquisition, if any, but not other expenditure or allowances under this special provision. Therefore, a person should not automatically deduct platform commissions, borrowing interest, subscription costs or network gas fees from the special-rate income. The exact acquisition cost, including a possible cost attribution question for rewarded or gifted tokens, must be supported by the facts and applicable law. TaxGuru’s analysis of cryptocurrency taxation under the 2025 Act provides further background. Worked illustration: tax on a profitable SOL disposal Assume 10 SOL were acquired for a documented aggregate cost of ₹1,00,000 and later transferred for ₹1,40,000. The illustrative income from that transfer is ₹40,000 (₹1,40,000 less ₹1,00,000). The special tax at 30% is ₹12,000 before applicable surcharge and cess. At 4% health and education cess and assuming no surcharge, the illustrative amount becomes ₹12,480. This calculation excludes any separate withholding credit and assumes the transaction is within the special VDA transfer rule. It is not a statement of SOL market prices or a forecast. If another SOL or other VDA transfer results in a loss, section 194’s special restrictions prevent routine netting of that loss against taxable VDA income and prevent carry-forward of the VDA loss under the special regime. This makes lot-wise acquisition evidence and disposal-level computation essential even when the net balance in a portfolio is negative. TaxGuru’s VDA framework explanation discusses the statutory lineage; for current-year compliance, the 2025 Act must govern. Crypto-to-crypto swaps and non-cash consideration A SOL-to-another-token swap can be a transfer even where no INR is credited to a bank account. The consideration must be valued using a consistent, defensible INR methodology at the relevant event. A wallet-to-wallet movement under the same beneficial ownership, without a disposal to another person, should be analysed separately from an actual sale, exchange, gift or settlement. Smart-contract interaction does not, by itself, answer whether a taxable transfer occurred. One Per Cent VDA TDS: Section 393 and Forms 141/142 Section 393(1), Table Sl. No. 8(vi) requires 1% deduction on qualifying consideration for transfer of a VDA to a resident, subject to the specific exemptions/thresholds in section 393(4), Table Sl. No. 12. Broadly, the annual consideration thresholds are ₹50,000 for the specified individual/HUF category and ₹10,000 for other payers. These are thresholds for the withholding obligation, not exemptions from income tax on VDA gains. In-kind and partly in-kind consideration calls for the statutory tax-payment safeguards before release. For the new-law period, Form No. 141 Schedule D is the relevant challan-cum-statement mechanism for specified payer cases, while Form No. 142 is the prescribed quarterly exchange reporting statement in the applicable circumstances. Taxpayers should not confuse the 1% TDS on gross consideration with the 30% tax on income. Tax credit depends on correct PAN, payment, reporting and return reconciliation. Staking Rewards: Receipt-Stage Tax and Later Transfer The special 30% provision expressly addresses income from transfer of a VDA. A SOL staking reward credited without a sale requires separate analysis of when the taxpayer obtains beneficial control, whether income has arisen at receipt, its correct head, valuation and the nature of the arrangement. It is not sound to state that every staking credit is automatically tax-free until sale, or that every staking credit is necessarily a 30%-taxed transfer. Where rewarded SOL is later sold or swapped, that disposal is a separate event under the VDA transfer framework. A practical reward register should show the stake-account address, validator, reward epoch, token quantity, date/time of credit or control, INR valuation source, any commission and eventual disposal lot. Where a custodial platform pools assets or provides a contractual reward rather than native protocol delegation, the customer agreement and account statements become important evidence. The tax basis of rewarded tokens must be established before computing a subsequent transfer. PMLA, FIU-IND and Crypto-Asset Reporting The March 2023 PMLA notification brought specified VDA service activities into the anti-money-laundering reporting framework. FIU-IND’s AML/CFT guidelines for reporting entities providing VDA services were updated on 8 January 2026. Covered activities include exchange between VDA and fiat, exchange between VDAs, transfers, safekeeping/administration and related financial services. Registration, KYC, beneficial-ownership checks, record retention, monitoring and suspicious-transaction reporting are service-provider duties under the applicable framework. FIU-IND issued non-compliance notices to fifteen VDA service providers in September 2026, reinforcing that offshore incorporation does not by itself remove Indian AML obligations where the relevant activities are carried on in or for India. TaxGuru’s reproduction of the FIU-IND enforcement release records the enforcement position. FIU registration is not an investment guarantee, insurance scheme or endorsement of any token. Separately, section 509 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 establishes crypto-asset transaction-information obligations for prescribed reporting entities. This reporting architecture should not be described as an identical obligation on every individual wallet holder. Individuals remain responsible for correct return disclosures, tax computation, source-of-funds evidence and reconciliation with information appearing in AIS or other records. GST and FEMA: Two Different Questions GST on token transactions and platform services GST treatment requires identification of the actual supply, supplier, recipient, consideration and place of supply. Exchange commissions, custody services, technical services or separately charged platform fees can raise GST liability under their own classifications. Whether and how a particular direct VDA transfer is taxable under GST is not conclusively answered merely by the 30% income-tax provision. A business accepting SOL as non-cash consideration for taxable goods or services must value the underlying supply under GST rules and separately examine a later token disposal. TaxGuru’s discussion of digital-asset taxation provides context but does not substitute for the governing GST notification and facts. Cross-border remittances and FEMA FEMA compliance depends on the actual remittance and transaction rather than the token’s name. Offshore exchange funding, settlement to a non-resident, payments for imported services, foreign bank accounts and third-party custodians may raise different questions. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme is not a blanket permission to send funds abroad for any crypto trading arrangement. Income-tax recognition of VDA gains does not authorise a transaction under FEMA. Users should retain authorised-dealer documentation and the purpose of each cross-border payment, and should not describe an offshore platform as RBI-authorised without checking the relevant permission. Accounting and Audit for Business Holdings of SOL There is no dedicated Indian accounting standard titled “Crypto-assets”. For an Ind AS reporter, classification should be based on the rights and purpose of holding SOL. The IFRS Interpretations Committee concluded in June 2019 that cryptocurrencies within its fact pattern are generally within IAS 38 for non-dealer holdings, or IAS 2 when held for sale in the ordinary course of business. That IFRS conclusion is informative, but the entity must independently apply the applicable Indian Ind AS or AS, including the facts of any contractual rights and its accounting policy; one cannot mechanically cite the IFRS decision as an Indian statutory rule. Recognition and measurement require evidence of beneficial ownership, acquisition cost, impairment or valuation, custody restrictions and transfers. The audit file should contain blockchain explorer records, exchange statements, private-key governance controls, wallet confirmations, cut-off testing, related-party transfers and evidence that a balance is not double-counted across exchange and self-custody wallets. Tax VDA classification and financial-statement classification need not be identical. Is SOL a virtual digital asset for Indian tax purposes? Ordinarily yes. SOL is a cryptographically secured native blockchain token; a specific exclusion would need to be established rather than assumed. 2. Does holding SOL for more than one year reduce the 30% VDA rate? No. The special transfer-income rate under section 194, Table Sl. No. 4 does not become a listed-equity long-term rate merely through holding period. 3. Is 1% TDS the final tax on a SOL sale? No. TDS is on qualifying consideration and is a credit mechanism; the special income-tax liability is computed separately. 4. Are staking rewards guaranteed? No. Rewards depend on network parameters, stake participation, validator uptime and commission, as well as any custodial platform terms. 5. Is a staking reward automatically taxed at 30% on receipt? Not automatically under the transfer rule. The receipt-stage character and timing require analysis; a later transfer is separately relevant. 6. Does FIU-IND registration guarantee the safety of an exchange? No. FIU registration concerns AML reporting-entity compliance, not insurance, price protection or approval of an investment product. 7. Can every overseas SOL transaction be funded under LRS? No. The transaction and purpose must be tested under FEMA and authorised-dealer requirements; the tax provisions do not grant remittance permission. 8. How should a company show SOL in its accounts? Apply the relevant AS/Ind AS based on the actual rights and purpose of holding, with documented valuation and custody controls. Tax VDA status alone is not an accounting policy. Key Takeaways SOL pays Solana network fees and can be delegated for staking; reward rates are variable and not guaranteed. Income from SOL transfers is generally within the 30% VDA special regime under section 194(1), Table Sl. No. 4 of the Income-tax Act, 2025. The special regime restricts deductions, VDA-loss set-off and carry-forward. Qualifying consideration for VDA transfer is subject to 1% TDS under section 393, subject to statutory thresholds. Staking receipts and later transfers must be analysed separately. PMLA/FIU-IND compliance, section 509 reporting, GST and FEMA are independent obligations.

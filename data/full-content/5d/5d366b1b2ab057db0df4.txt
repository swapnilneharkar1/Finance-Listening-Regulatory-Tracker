Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); JCIT Vs Ashok Kumar Manish Kumar HUF (Supreme Court of India) Section 148 Reopening Barred on Same Form 26AS Mismatch Already Decided by CIT(A) | Supreme Court Dismisses Revenue’s SLP Summary: The Rajasthan High Court quashed a reassessment notice under Section 148 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 where the Assessing Officer relied on the same interest-income discrepancy that the CIT(A) had already examined and decided. The discrepancy had resurfaced through a High Risk e-Verification flag on the Insight Portal. The High Court found that the sanction note relied on the same figures and disclosed no fresh material suggesting escapement of income. On 18 November 2026, the Supreme Court condoned the delay and dismissed the Revenue’s special leave petition, leaving the High Court’s order in place. A TDS Credit Dispute Led to a ₹65.52 Lakh Reopening In Ashok Kumar Manish Kumar HUF v. Joint Commissioner of Income Tax & Ors., the assessee filed its return for AY 2022–23, declaring total income of ₹8,24,770 and claiming TDS credit of ₹7,15,391. While processing the return under Section 143(1), the Centralised Processing Centre restricted the credit to ₹2,83,663, citing a mismatch with Form 26AS. The HUF sought rectification under Section 154. It explained that ₹73,94,574 of interest income had been recorded in the business accounts of M/s Ranka Dyeing Mills, against which interest expenditure of ₹74,83,321 had been accounted for. The net figure was reflected under the business head. A further ₹6,47,842 was disclosed under Income from Other Sources. The CPC rejected the rectification application. CIT(A) Examined the Disclosure Behind the TDS Claim By an order dated 4 December 2025 under Section 250, the CIT(A) accepted the assessee’s reconciliation and directed that full TDS credit be granted after verification with Form 26AS, together with consequential relief. The appellate authority examined where the interest had been accounted for and how it had been disclosed in the return. The Revenue did not challenge the appellate order, which attained finality. Even so, the Assessing Officer issued a Section 148 notice on 23 March 2026, based on a sanction note dated 20 March 2026. The note compared deposit interest of ₹71,99,135 identified during e-verification with deposit interest of ₹6,47,429 shown under Income from Other Sources. It treated the difference of ₹65,51,706 as potential escaped income. That comparison left out the interest disclosure in the business accounts that the CIT(A) had already examined. Insight Portal Flag Did Not Supply Fresh Material The Department invoked the e-Verification Scheme, 2021, notified under Section 135A. The sanction note treated the verification report as information under Section 148(3)(iv) and invoked Section 148A(4) to issue the notice directly under Section 148. The Revenue also argued that the writ petition was premature because the assessee had approached the High Court before responding to the notice. The High Court examined the material underlying the portal flag. It found that the sanction note drew on the same figures and information that had been before the CIT(A). The earlier appeal had resolved the factual premise of the proposed reopening, namely whether the interest had been disclosed. In the absence of fresh material, revisiting that premise amounted to a change of opinion and an impermissible challenge to a final appellate adjudication. The Court set aside the notice, the sanction note and all consequential proceedings. The Court relied on CIT v. Kelvinator of India Ltd. (2010) 320 ITR 561 (SC), which recognised the change-of-opinion doctrine as a check on the reassessment power. What the Supreme Court Decided In Joint Commissioner of Income Tax & Ors. v. Ashok Kumar Manish Kumar HUF, SLP (Civil) Diary No. 67250/2026, Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran condoned the delay and dismissed the SLP on 18 November 2026, recording that they found no good ground to interfere. The dismissal at the special-leave stage leaves the relief granted to the HUF intact. The reasoned ruling on Section 148 remains the Rajasthan High Court judgment. The effect of the dismissal follows the principle in Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala (2000) 6 SCC 359. Author’s Comments The most significant feature is that the original dispute arose from a Section 143(1) intimation, yet the underlying interest disclosure was expressly examined in appeal. The assessee’s strongest point was the finding in the CIT(A)’s order that addressed the very receipts later described as escaped income. In a similar case, the comparison should therefore begin with the appellate findings and the recorded basis for reopening. A receipt-wise reconciliation can show where each amount appearing in Form 26AS was accounted for and whether the subsequent verification report identifies anything beyond the figures already examined. Cases Discussed: Kunhayammed Vs State of Kerala (Supreme Court); (2000) 6 SCC 359; AY: Not Applicable. Non-speaking dismissal of a special leave petition and the doctrine of merger. CIT Vs Kelvinator of India Ltd. (Supreme Court); (2010) 320 ITR 561; AY: Not independently verified. Reassessment on a mere change of opinion and requirement of tangible material.

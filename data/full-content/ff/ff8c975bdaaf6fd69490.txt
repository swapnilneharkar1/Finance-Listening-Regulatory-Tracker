Summary: The article examines the statutory limits of the first appellate authority under Section 107 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, with particular reference to judicial discipline, jurisdictional restrictions and admission of additional evidence. It explains that appellate authorities exercising quasi-judicial functions must follow binding judgments of the Supreme Court and jurisdictional High Courts unless legitimately distinguishable, overruled or stayed. The author identifies several instances of appellate overreach, including introducing grounds absent from the original show cause notice, changing the character of proceedings, indirectly remanding matters despite Section 107(11), enhancing demands without notice, imposing additional pre-deposit conditions and dismissing appeals without reasoned decisions. The article examines the influence of earlier trade tax and VAT regimes, under which appellate authorities possessed wider powers. It further discusses the department’s burden of establishing foundational allegations, the taxpayer’s obligation to rebut them and the separate burden of proving input tax credit eligibility under Section 155. Particular attention is given to Rule 112 of the CGST Rules, which permits additional evidence in specified circumstances and requires a reasoned approach to its admission. The author argues that documents cannot be rejected merely by describing them as an afterthought without examining the applicable statutory requirements. Judicial precedents and statutory provisions are discussed to demonstrate why appellate authorities must exercise their powers within legislative boundaries while ensuring procedural fairness, judicial discipline and effective appellate remedies. Introduction The first appeal under Section 107 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 and the corresponding State Act is the first occasion on which an order of the proper officer is examined by another authority. For most dealers it is also the last forum where facts are examined in any depth. The quality of that examination therefore decides a great deal. Yet a reading of appellate orders passed in Uttar Pradesh over the last few years shows a pattern. Binding judgments of the High Court are set aside with a single line, orders of the adjudicating authority are sustained on reasons that the adjudicating authority never gave, matters are sent back under the name of verification, and documents produced in appeal are rejected with the remark that they are an afterthought, or in the language of the hearing room, “ab kyon dikha rahe ho”. This article examines how far the first appellate authority can go. It asks three questions. Is the authority free to depart from judicial precedent? Where do the Act and the Rules draw the outer line of its jurisdiction, and in what ways is that line crossed? And is a dealer bound to rebut everything at the first stage, so that whatever comes later can be dismissed as an afterthought? 2. Nature of the First Appellate Jurisdiction The appellate authority is a creature of the statute. It has no inherent power. Whatever it does must be traced to Section 107 and to Rules 108 to 113 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017. Section 107(11) states its power in plain words: after making such further inquiry as may be necessary, it shall pass such order as it thinks just and proper, confirming, modifying or annulling the decision or order appealed against, but it shall not refer the case back to the adjudicating authority that passed the decision or order. Three features of this jurisdiction need to be kept in view. First, it is a quasi judicial jurisdiction, even though it is exercised by an officer of the same department. Second, it is a jurisdiction to test an existing order, not to make a fresh assessment. Third, it is hedged by provisos that require notice before any enhancement or any fresh demand of tax, and by Section 75(7), which ties every demand to the grounds stated in the show cause notice. Each instance of overreach discussed below is, in substance, a departure from one of these three features. 3. Judicial Precedent Binds the Appellate Authority Article 141 of the Constitution makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts within the territory of India, and Article 144 requires every civil and judicial authority to act in aid of the Supreme Court. As to High Courts, the Supreme Court held in East India Commercial Co. Ltd. v. Collector of Customs, AIR 1962 SC 1893 that the law declared by the highest court in a State is binding on authorities and tribunals under its superintendence. For an appellate authority sitting in Uttar Pradesh, therefore, a judgment of the Allahabad High Court is not a persuasive opinion. It is the law it must apply. The principle of judicial discipline in fiscal matters was stated in Union of India v. Kamlakshi Finance Corporation Ltd., 1992 Supp (1) SCC 443. The Supreme Court held that revenue authorities exercising quasi judicial functions must follow the orders of higher appellate authorities unreservedly. The mere fact that the order of the higher authority is not acceptable to the department, and is the subject matter of an appeal, furnishes no ground for not following it unless its operation has been suspended by a competent court. Any other course, the Court said, results in undue harassment to assessees and chaos in the administration of tax laws. The Allahabad High Court has applied this principle to GST officers with some firmness. In Rajdhani Udyog v. State of U.P., Writ Tax No. 3684 of 2025, the Court found that the authority had failed to apply judgments of the Court already holding the field, imposed a personal cost of Rs. 5,000 on the officer, and directed the Principal Secretary, Institutional Finance, to file an affidavit and put in place a system to keep officers informed of judicial pronouncements. In practice the authority seldom refuses to follow a judgment openly. The departure takes quieter forms: (a) Silence The judgment is cited in the grounds of appeal, but the order does not discuss it, or disposes of it with the sentence that the case laws cited are not applicable to the facts of the present case. To distinguish a precedent is itself a judicial act. It requires the authority to identify the material fact that is different and to explain why the difference changes the result. A bare statement of inapplicability is not a distinction at all. (b) Treating the judgment as personal to the parties The authority reasons that the High Court allowed the writ petition of another dealer and that the order does not extend beyond that dealer. The operative direction is indeed confined to the parties, but the ratio is the law declared, and it binds every authority within the jurisdiction. (c) Reliance on a pending appeal The authority observes that the department has filed a special leave petition. Kamlakshi Finance answers this completely. Unless the judgment is stayed, it must be followed. (d) Preferring a circular or an instruction A circular issued under Section 168 binds the officers of the department, but it cannot override or dilute the interpretation of a statutory provision by the High Court. Moreover, a quasi judicial authority that decides an appeal according to the direction of a superior officer surrenders the very discretion the statute vests in it, as held in Orient Paper Mills Ltd. v. Union of India, AIR 1970 SC 1498. (e) Treating the ratio as one possible view The authority proceeds on its own reading of the section as if the question were open. Once the jurisdictional High Court has construed a provision, that construction is, for every authority below, the meaning of the provision. (f) Selective choice Where more than one judgment exists, the authority relies on the one that suits the revenue and passes over a later ruling, a ruling of a larger Bench, or a ruling of the Supreme Court that has settled the point. There remain legitimate grounds on which a precedent may not be applied. It may be distinguishable on facts, it may have been overruled, or its operation may have been stayed. Each of these must be found and recorded. What the authority cannot do is substitute its own preference for the law declared. 4. The Statutory Boundaries and How They Are Crossed The second form of overreach lies in the exercise of powers that Section 107 does not give. The following instances recur. (a) Improving the original order This is the most frequent and the least noticed. The adjudicating authority gives one reason, or none, and the appellate authority confirms the demand on a reason of its own: a new basis of computation, a new allegation, or a finding of fraud or suppression that the notice never made. The rule in Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner, (1978) 1 SCC 405 is that an order must be judged by the reasons stated in it and cannot be supplemented by fresh reasons later. Read with Section 75(7), under which no demand shall be confirmed on grounds other than those specified in the notice, the appellate authority cannot sustain a demand on a ground the notice did not contain. To do so is to conduct a fresh adjudication without a notice, without the period of limitation and without a hearing. (b) Changing the character of the proceeding A demand raised under Section 74 is sustained as one under Section 73, or the other way round, or a penalty under Section 129 is upheld as if it were under Section 130. Each of these is a distinct proceeding with its own notice, its own conditions and its own limitation. The power to modify an order does not extend to converting it into a different proceeding. (c) Remand under another name Section 107(11) prohibits reference back. The prohibition is now avoided by directions such as “the adjudicating authority shall verify”, “shall recompute” or “the appellant may produce the documents before the proper officer”. In Kanha Shree Steels v. Assistant Deputy Commissioner, Writ Tax No. 2746 of 2025, decided on 19.08.2025, the Allahabad High Court held that once the appellate authority finds the order deficient, it cannot remit the matter for verification and give the revenue a second innings. The same view was taken in Anand and Anand v. Principal Commissioner, 2026 (2) TMI 956 (All.). Whatever the form of words, a direction that leaves the final decision to the lower authority is a remand. (d) Enhancement without notice The first proviso to Section 107(11) forbids an order enhancing any fee, penalty, fine in lieu of confiscation or the value of confiscated goods unless the appellant is given a reasonable opportunity to show cause. The second proviso goes further: where the authority is of the opinion that any tax has not been paid or short paid or erroneously refunded, or input tax credit has been wrongly availed or utilised, no order requiring the appellant to pay it shall be passed unless a notice is given and the order is passed within the time limit specified under Section 73 or Section 74. An appellate order that increases the burden in disregard of these provisos is without jurisdiction to that extent. (e) Conditions the statute does not prescribe The deposit required for filing an appeal is fixed by Section 107(6). The authority has no power to add further deposits, cash only conditions, or clauses providing that the appeal shall stand rejected if a condition is not met. (f) Bending limitation Section 107(1) and (4) fix the period of limitation and allow condonation of a further one month. The authority cannot extend this, but it equally cannot shorten it by counting from the date of uploading instead of the date of communication, or by treating as fatal a procedural requirement of filing a certified copy that the Rules no longer insist upon in that form. Nor can it reject as not maintainable an appeal that the High Court itself directed the dealer to file. (g) Dismissal without decision Section 107(9) permits adjournment up to three times on sufficient cause, and Section 107(12) requires the order to state the points for determination, the decision on them and the reasons for the decision. An appeal is to be decided, not closed. Dismissal for non prosecution, or rejection without addressing the grounds, is not a decision within Section 107(11). (h) Applying a Rule against the Act The appellate authority cannot declare a Rule invalid; that is for the writ court. But it is equally bound not to apply a Rule, or to read a condition into a section, in a manner that defeats the parent provision. A Rule draws its force from the section under which it is framed and cannot travel beyond it. The authority that extends a Rule beyond the section does indirectly what the legislature has not done. (i) Procedural shortcuts The Allahabad High Court has held that an appellate order cannot be reserved on one date and pronounced later without informing the appellant. Procedure under a taxing statute is part of the jurisdiction, not a matter of convenience. 5. Is This an Effect of the Former Law? Much of what is described above is a habit carried forward from the earlier regime. Under the U.P. Trade Tax Act, 1948 and the U.P. Value Added Tax Act, 2008, the first appellate authority could set aside an assessment and direct a fresh one. Appellate officers wrote such orders for years. Section 107(11) withdrew that power, but the drafting habit has survived, and the prohibited remand now appears in the form of a direction to verify. The same is true of the doctrine of coextensive powers. Under the older fiscal statutes the Supreme Court held that the powers of the first appellate authority were coterminous with those of the assessing authority, so that it could do what the assessing officer could have done and could enhance the assessment or sustain it on a different footing: Commissioner of Income Tax v. Kanpur Coal Syndicate, AIR 1965 SC 325; Jute Corporation of India Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income Tax, 1991 Supp (2) SCC 744. An officer trained on that line feels free to improve the order. The Goods and Services Tax law deliberately narrowed this. Section 75(7) binds the demand to the notice; the provisos to Section 107(11) require notice and adherence to limitation for any enhancement or fresh demand; and the main part of Section 107(11) confines the authority to confirming, modifying or annulling the order. The older doctrine survives only within these limits. The change was not without precedent in central law. The Finance Act, 2001 had already removed the power of remand from the Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 35A of the Central Excise Act, 1944 and from the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) under Section 251 of the Income Tax Act, 1961. Section 107(11) follows that model. The State appellate wing, however, was drawn largely from the Value Added Tax administration, where no such change had taken place. Not every instance of overreach is inherited. Part of it is structural. The first appellate authority is an officer of the department, posted by rotation between assessment, enforcement and appeal, working within an administration that measures performance in terms of revenue. For several years there was also no functioning tribunal above the first appeal to correct its errors. These conditions explain the reluctance to follow judgments adverse to the department. They do not enlarge the jurisdiction. The legislature consciously withdrew the remand and plenary powers that existed under the earlier statutes, and an officer who exercises them today is not reading Section 107 broadly. He is applying a regime that no longer exists. 6. Is Rebuttal Necessary at Every Stage? The question often arises in the form of a finding that the dealer did not rebut the allegation before the adjudicating authority and is therefore bound by it. The finding misunderstands where the burden lies. (a) In proceedings under Section 73, Section 74 or Section 74A, it is for the department to state in the notice the foundational facts and the material on which the allegation rests. A bare allegation, an estimate without basis, or a figure of mismatch unsupported by any inquiry does not become proof merely because it is not answered. The obligation to rebut arises only when the department has first discharged its own burden. (b) Silence is not admission. Even where no reply is filed, the order must decide the matter on the material on record and give reasons. A demand cannot be confirmed only because a reply was not filed. (c) The dealer meets the case in the notice. Under Section 75(7) the demand is confined to the grounds in the notice. A point the notice never raised requires no rebuttal, and absence of rebuttal on such a point cannot be used against the dealer. (d) The department’s own records need no proof. Returns, auto populated statements, e-way bills and payment entries on the common portal are already with the department. The authority cannot hold that they were not proved. (e) Questions of law stand apart. Limitation, jurisdiction, the conditions for invoking Section 74, the bar on parallel proceedings and the effect of a binding precedent go to the root of the matter and may be raised at any stage. A question of jurisdiction does not depend on whether it was rebutted below. (f) Input tax credit is the one area where Section 155 places the burden of proving eligibility on the person claiming credit. Even here the burden is discharged when the dealer produces the invoices, proof of payment, documents of movement and the returns of the supplier. The department cannot then shift the consequence of a supplier’s later default onto the recipient without first proceeding against the supplier. 7. The Plea of Afterthought and Rule 112 Production of additional evidence before the appellate authority is governed by Rule 112, not by impression. Under Rule 112(1), the appellant may produce evidence, oral or documentary, other than that produced before the adjudicating authority, in four situations: where the adjudicating authority refused to admit evidence that ought to have been admitted; where the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause from producing evidence called upon to be produced; where the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause from producing evidence relevant to any ground of appeal; and where the adjudicating authority made the order without giving the appellant sufficient opportunity to adduce evidence relevant to any ground of appeal. Rule 112(2) requires the appellate authority to record in writing the reasons for its admission. Rule 112(3) entitles the adjudicating authority to a reasonable opportunity to examine the evidence and to produce evidence in rebuttal. Rule 112(4) empowers the appellate authority itself to direct the production of any document or the examination of any witness to enable it to dispose of the appeal. Section 107(11) adds the power to make further inquiry, and Section 107(8) allows a ground not specified in the memorandum of appeal where its omission was not wilful or unreasonable. Measured against these provisions, the remark that a document is an afterthought fails in several situations: (a) Where the original order was passed ex parte Where the original order was passed ex parte, without the personal hearing contemplated by Section 75(4), or on a notice too vague to be answered, the case falls within the fourth clause of Rule 112(1). The dealer never had a fair occasion to produce the evidence. Its production in appeal cannot be called an afterthought. (b) Where the document already existed Invoices, e-way bills, bank entries, stock registers and returns prepared in the ordinary course of business are not brought into being by the appeal. The time of production does not affect genuineness. If the authority doubts a document, it has the power to verify it under Rule 112(4) and Section 107(11). It cannot reject it on the ground of timing alone. (c) Where no reasons are recorded A rejection in one line, without examining the request against the four clauses of Rule 112(1), is itself a breach of the Rule and of Section 107(12). (d) Where what is called an afterthought is a ground of law A legal ground is answered on its merits, not by a label. The plea of afterthought does have a legitimate field. It applies where the dealer had full opportunity before the adjudicating authority, availed it, and deliberately withheld the document; where the document carries features suggesting that it was prepared after the order, such as dates, form or inconsistency with the returns; or where the dealer now sets up a factual case contrary to the one taken earlier. Even then, the authority must say which of these it finds and on what basis. That finding is part of deciding the appeal judicially. Without it, suspicion is put in place of a finding. 8. Conclusion The first appellate authority under the GST law has a limited jurisdiction. It tests the order under appeal against the notice, the statute and the law declared by the courts, and then confirms, modifies or annuls that order. Each form of overreach examined here is the same mistake in a different form: the authority either turns itself into a second adjudicating authority, by supplying a new reason, a new basis, a new section or a new procedure, or into a second legislature, by substituting its own reading for that of the High Court or of the statute. The Act was drafted to prevent both. Keeping the authority within its jurisdiction is not a technicality. It is what makes the first appeal an appeal. ****** Disclaimer: The views expressed in this article are the author’s own thoughts on the subject.

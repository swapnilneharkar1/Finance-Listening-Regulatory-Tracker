(Delhi High Court) Nil Project Revenue Does Not Bar Deduction of Business Overheads Under Section 37(1) Can a Developer Claim Overheads Before Recognising Project Revenue? A real estate developer may incur substantial business expenditure before its projects reach the stage at which revenue can be recognised. Does the absence of project revenue require the developer to carry such expenditure as work-in-progress (WIP)? In Experion Developers, the Delhi High Court upheld the Tribunal’s deletion of an ad hoc disallowance of ₹8.73 crore. The deductibility of expenditure depended on its nature, business purpose and attribution to the projects. The absence of recognised project revenue did not, by itself, determine when the expenditure could be deducted. How a Returned Loss Became Assessed Income Experion Developers was engaged in developing townships, housing and commercial premises. For AY 2012–13, it returned a loss of ₹7,82,95,075. The Assessing Officer completed the scrutiny assessment under Section 143(3) at an income of ₹90,15,239. The company had claimed ₹9,70,11,460 as revenue expenditure. The Assessing Officer considered the expenditure directly or indirectly connected with ongoing projects and took the view that it should be included in project cost or WIP, particularly because the company had recognised no project revenue during the year. He allowed 10% of the claim and disallowed the remaining 90%, amounting to ₹8,73,10,314. The Commissioner (Appeals) confirmed the disallowance. Why the Tribunal Deleted the Disallowance The Tribunal found that the company had allocated project-related expenditure to inventory under AS-7 and the ICAI Guidance Note on Accounting for Real Estate Transactions and charged the remaining expenditure to the profit and loss account. The Assessing Officer had accepted the books without invoking Section 145(3). The assessee also pointed out that it had furnished a head-wise classification covering 27 categories of expenditure, and that the authorities had identified no specific expense as wrongly classified. Relying on AB Multiplex Pvt. Ltd. v. ACIT, ITA No. 739/Del/2014, the Tribunal held that once a business is set up, earning revenue in the same year is unnecessary for deduction of otherwise eligible business expenditure under Section 37(1). It also examined the company’s consistent accounting treatment and noted that project income had been offered to tax in later years under the Percentage of Completion Method (POCM). On the evidence before it, the Tribunal held that the flat 90% disallowance was arbitrary. High Court Examines the Nature and Allocation of Costs The Revenue argued that recognising no project revenue while claiming immediate deduction of overheads violated the matching principle. It also questioned the evidence supporting the allocation and argued that the allowance of 10% merely accommodated statutory expenses and the difficulty of verifying the company’s bifurcation. The High Court agreed that the partial allowance was no conclusive admission that the entire expenditure was deductible. It also clarified that consistency in accounting cannot override the Income-tax Act. The Tribunal’s decision, however, rested on its examination of the nature of the expenditure, its allocation to WIP, the business purpose and the applicable accounting guidance. The Guidance Note distinguishes costs directly related to specific projects from costs attributable to project activity generally. The factual question was whether the disputed expenditure belonged in project cost or qualified independently for deduction under Section 37. No Substantial Question of Law Under Section 260A Referring to CIT v. Hamdard National Foundation, 2022 SCC OnLine Del 513 and the Karnataka High Court’s decision in CIT v. Bahubali Neminath Muttin, 2016 SCC OnLine Kar 8048, the Court reiterated that the Tribunal is the final fact-finding authority. The possibility of taking a different view of the evidence is insufficient for interference under Section 260A. The Department failed to demonstrate perversity in the Tribunal’s findings or an error in its application of the statutory provisions or accounting principles. The High Court accordingly dismissed the Revenue’s appeal. Author’s Comments The decision illustrates why revenue recognition and expense classification require separate examination. The POCM threshold governs when project revenue is recognised. The treatment of an expense depends on what it relates to. For instance, site supervision costs attributable to a project may form part of project inventory, while head office expenses with no project attribution may qualify for deduction in the year incurred, subject to the conditions of Section 37(1). In scrutiny proceedings, a head-wise reconciliation showing what was transferred to WIP and why the balance was charged to the profit and loss account can address the issue more directly than a general reliance on accounting policy. Experion’s success rested on the Tribunal’s findings about the actual allocation and the nature of the expenditure. Those findings also explain why the Revenue’s challenge to the deletion of a flat percentage disallowance failed under Section 260A. Cases Discussed: CIT Vs Virtual Soft Systems Ltd. According to the learned Assessing Officer, the expenditure was directly or indirectly connected with projects under development and ought to have been capitalised as „project cost or work-in-progress‟, particularly when no project revenue had been recognised during the year. 5. Aggrieved by the Assessment Order dated 19.03.2015, the Respondent/Assessee filed an appeal before the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals)(hereinafter referred to as „CIT(A)‟). The relevant portion of the Impugned Order reads thus:- “ 6. The application of Accounting Standard and Guidance Notes issued by ICAI and utilization of the same for the purpose of recognition of income under the Income Tax Act has been subject matter of due consideration by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Virtual Soft Systems Limited vs ACIT reported in 404 ITR 409 (SC) wherein the Hon’ble Apex Court had duly given due credence to the Accounting Standards and Guidance Notes issued by ICAI. It is well settled that earning of revenue is not a condition precedent for deduction of expenditure under section 37 of the Act. What is required to be seen is whether the business is set up and whether the expenditure incurred thereon are meant for the purpose of business. That assessing officer concluded that assessee did not have any business income and that since no business was carried out by that assessee during the year, the question of allowability of business expenditure does not arise which stood upheld by that Learned CITA. The facts of that case before the Delhi tribunal are squarely applicable to the facts before us. 7. An appeal under the said provision is maintainable only where the Court is satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law. The learned ITAT is the final fact-finding authority under the Act, and its findings cannot be reopened merely because another view of the evidence may be possible. A Co-ordinate Bench of this in CIT v. Hamdard National Foundation, 2022 SCC OnLine Del 513 has discussed the meaning of the term “substantial question of law” as under: “ 26. The Supreme Court, in Ram Kumar Agarwal v. Thawar Das [Ram Kumar Agarwal v. Thawar Das, (1999) 7 SCC 303] , has reiterated that under Section 100 of the Civil Procedure Code, 1908, the jurisdiction of the High Court to interfere with the orders passed by the courts below is confined to hearing on the substantial question of law and interference with the finding of the fact is not warranted if it involves reappreciation of evidence. Further, the Supreme Court, in State of Haryana v. Khalsa Motors Ltd. [State of Haryana v. Khalsa Motors Ltd., (1990) 4 SCC 659] , has held that the High Court was not justified in law in reversing, in the second appeal, the concurrent finding of the fact recorded by both the courts below. The Supreme Court in Hero Vinoth v. Seshammal [Hero Vinoth v. Seshammal, (2006) 5 SCC 545] , has also held that “in a case where from a given set of circumstances two inferences of fact are possible, the one drawn by the lower appellate court will not be interfered by the High Court in the second appeal. Adopting any other approach is not permissible”. It Signature Not Verified has also been held that there is a difference between a question of law and a “substantial question of law”. Recently, while considering a similar provision in the Electricity Act, 2003, the Supreme Court in Maharashtra State Electricity Distribution Co. Ltd. v. Maharashtra Electricity Regulatory Commission [Maharashtra State Electricity Distribution Co. Ltd. v. Maharashtra Electricity Regulatory Commission, (2022) 4 SCC 657 : 2021 SCC OnLine SC 913] , observed that the word “substantial question of law” means not only a substantial question of law of general importance, but also any substantial question of law arising in a case between the parties on which the decision in the lis depends. A question of law that arises accidentally or collaterally and has no bearing on the final outcome will not be a substantial question of law. Whether the question raised is a question of law and, if so, whether the question is a substantial question of law is also not determined by the enormity of the stakes involved in the same. To be “substantial”, a question of law must be debatable, not previously settled by the law of the land or any binding precedent, and must have a material bearing on the decision of the case and/or rights of the parties before it, if answered either way. Findings of fact recorded by the courts below, which would imply the CIT(A) and the learned ITAT in these appeals, cannot be reopened. Sufficiency or adequacy of the evidence to support a finding is a matter for the decision of the court of facts. (Emphasis Supplied) 21. Similarly, in Santosh Hazari v. To be a question of law „involving in the case‟ there must be first a foundation for it laid in the pleadings and the question should emerge from the sustainable findings of fact arrived at by court of facts and it must be necessary to decide that question of law for a just and proper decision of the case. It will, therefore, depend on the facts and circumstance of each case whether a question of law is a substantial one and involved in the case, or not; the paramount overall consideration being the need for striking a judicious balance between the indispensable obligation to do justice at all stages and impelling necessity of avoiding prolongation in the life of any lis.” 18. In Hero Vinoth (Minor) v. The Appellant/Department, by proposed Questions A and B, contends that the learned ITAT erred in allowing the deduction of Rs.9,70,11,460/- as revenue expenditure in a year in which no project revenue had been recognised. The allowability of expenditure has to be determined in accordance with the nature of the expenditure and whether it is attributable to the project or is otherwise incurred wholly and exclusively for the business. In the case on hand, the learned ITAT considered the accounting method followed by the Assessee, the allocation of expenditure to work-in- progress, the business purpose of the expenditure and the applicable accounting guidance. It also took note of the fact that the Assessing Officer had disallowed 90% of the expenditure on an ad hoc basis and that the Signature Not Verified Assessing Officer‟s action in making the disallowance of Rs.8,73,10,314/- was “totally arbitrary in nature”. The learned ITAT‟s conclusion was based on its assessment of the material on record which cannot be said to be perverse and does not warrant any interference. 24. Questions C and E concern the significance attributed by the learned ITAT to the partial allowance allowed by the Assessing Officer and the rule of consistency. It is indeed true that the partial allowance by the Assessing Officer cannot constitute a conclusive admission that the entire expenditure is deductible. The rule of consistency in the method of accounting cannot override the provisions of the IT Act. However, the learned ITAT also relied upon its factual findings concerning the nature and allocation of expenditure. 25. Question D, which challenges the allowance of indirect and overhead expenditure while project revenue remained unrecognised, raises the same underlying issue regarding the distinction between expenditure attributable to projects and expenditure otherwise deductible under Section 37. The applicable accounting guidance, which is the Guidance Note, distinguishes between costs directly related to specific projects and costs attributable to project activity generally. On the findings recorded by the learned ITAT, the Department has not demonstrated that the learned ITAT‟s conclusion rests upon an erroneous interpretation of the applicable statutory provisions or accounting principles. 26. As regards Question F, no specific material has been identified to demonstrate that a substantial question of law arises from the present Appeal. 27. In the light of the aforesaid discussion, we are of the considered opinion that the proposed substantial questions of law do not give rise to a Signature Not Verified substantial question of law warranting interference by this Court under Section 260A of the IT Act. 28. Accordingly, the appeal is dismissed. Application(s), if any, pending, shall stand closed.

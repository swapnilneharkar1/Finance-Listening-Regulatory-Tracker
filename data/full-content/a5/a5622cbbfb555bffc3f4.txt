Articles 3 min read Foreign currency deposits good insurance; need strategic approach for USD 160-bn repayment cliff: Uday Kotak PTI Published On Oct 26, 2026 at 06:46 PM IST var news_msid = 135212890; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook Mumbai Financial services industry veteran Uday Kotak on Monday said that India will have to repay over USD 160 billion on deposits raised through the foreign currency non-resident (bank), or FCNR(B), window and urged policymakers to be strategic while moving to the repayment "cliff" in three to five years.Speaking at the annual Business Standard BFSI Insights Summit here, Kotak also flagged risks from excessive financialisation of gold, which is leading to a surge in India's imports of the precious metal and putting a risk on the external balances.Terming the over USD 133 billion raised from diaspora as part of the concessional swap facility for FCNR(B) deposits as good insurance amid the current geopolitical upheaval, Kotak reminded of the need to prepare for the repayments. Advt When that "cliff" arrives between years 3 and 5 from now, we will have to repay over USD 160-170 billion to the depositors, including interest, he said."There will be a payback time. We don't need to panic about it today, but we must be strategic about how we build India's economic structure and the financial structure for that time which will inevitably come," said Kotak, the founder and non-executive director of Kotak Mahindra Bank.He said India got the timing of the FCNR(B) scheme very correct as the scheme ended days ahead of the US Treasury hitting the 5 per cent mark, and added that our cost of raising the funds would have been higher by 1 percentage point otherwise."Some people have called the FCNR(B) a steroid, but I think about it as a strong insurance policy," he added.The surge in US treasury yields compared to the Indian benchmark yield has created a situation where the interest rate differential between the two has reduced, Kotak said.Noting the interest rate hikes by RBI, Kotak said the move only takes care of the short end and added that more work will be required from the policymakers for the long end curve."The long-end curve is a different curve, which is more dependent on markets, unless central banks support that curve downwards," he added.Pointing out that large individual investors pay 39 per cent in taxes on interest income versus under 15 per cent on capital gains, Kotak said that this differential is leading to larger sums of money moving into equity or quasi-equity instruments. Advt "Is this leading to a significant skew of money into equity-oriented investments and thereby keeping Indian equity valuations higher than the global benchmarks?" he asked.In his remarks that come amid concerns on rupee depreciation and also heavy selling by foreign institutional investors, Kotak said, "We need to think about our tax structures, our interest rate structures, our capital gains structures because we may be able to get equity investors in rupees but the foreign investor exits in dollars."Kotak said that even after paying for crude at elevated levels, India's current account could be in a surplus mode but for gold imports, and flagged this as an area for a relook by policymakers. Advt "The danger of finalisation of gold in the economy as a financial instrument at a time when we have such challenges on the capital and current account would need a strategic thinking of how do we address this problem," he said.Kotak, however, clarified that he respects Indian households' buying of gold as a hedge, but it is the financial investments through instruments like exchange traded funds or collateral for bank lending which is the worry."We have to be careful not to financialise gold as a part of our financial sector. And that is where the focus has to be," he said, adding that we also need to look at what the country can afford at a point in time.

Summary: Payments of management and support service fees by Indian subsidiaries to overseas parent companies require independent examination under income-tax law, applicable tax treaties, transfer pricing provisions and GST. A management fee may qualify as fees for technical services under Section 9(1)(vii) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, where the payment relates to managerial, technical or consultancy services. Where chargeable under domestic law, the applicable foreign-company tax and withholding provisions must be considered, together with treaty relief based on the nature of the services, permanent establishment status and any make-available requirement. Separately, the Indian subsidiary must establish that the services were actually rendered, related to its business and appropriately priced. Agreements, correspondence, deliverables, allocation keys, cost records and benchmarking analyses are therefore important. Pure shareholder activities and duplicative charges require particular scrutiny. Transfer pricing compliance also involves reporting the international transaction in the prescribed accountant’s report, with Form 3CEB applicable under the earlier law and Form 48 under the Income-tax Rules, 2026. On the GST side, imported management services may attract IGST under reverse charge, including specified related-party services supplied without consideration. The time of supply for services received from overseas associated enterprises also requires attention because accounting entries may trigger liability before payment. The article provides a documentation checklist covering income tax, treaty eligibility, transfer pricing, GST and FEMA to help Indian subsidiaries manage their compliance and litigation exposure. Is the payment taxable in India? A management or service fee falls within “fees for technical services” (FTS) under Explanation 2 to section 9(1)(vii) of the 1961 Act only if it is for managerial, technical or consultancy services. Pure shareholder activity, such as group reporting or protecting the parent’s investment, is not a service to the subsidiary and may not give rise to FTS at all. Where it is FTS, domestic taxation is at 20% on a gross basis under section 115A plus surcharge and cess, and withholding falls under section 393(2) of the 2025 Act. 2. Treaty position Many treaties tax FTS only if the services are “ancillary” to the royalty or “make available” technical knowledge, skill or know-how. If no knowledge is transferred, the payment may fall under business profits and be taxable only if the parent has a PE in India. Treaties without a make-available clause usually tax FTS at a capped gross rate. The treaty should be read for the specific country, and the TRC, Form 10F and a no-PE declaration collected before remittance. Services rendered by parent employees in India can create a service PE, so the days-based test needs to be tracked. 3. Deduction in the subsidiary’s hands Even where withholding is correctly done, the Indian company must show the services were rendered, benefited the business, and were charged at arm’s length. Tax officers routinely ask for: the service agreement and a description of each service; evidence of receipt: emails, reports, time sheets, deliverables; the allocation key and why it fits (headcount, revenue, usage); the mark-up, if any, and a benchmarking analysis. An allocation of “group costs” with no evidence of benefit is the pattern most likely to be disallowed. The transfer pricing officer can propose a nil arm’s length price for a service a third party would not pay for (duplicative or purely shareholder services). 4. Transfer pricing mechanics The international transaction must be reported in Form 3CEB (under the 2025 Act the corresponding forms are in the Income-tax Rules 2026, notified on 20 March 2026). Common approaches are cost-plus or TNMM on the cost base, with a low-value-adding intra-group service approach in OECD guidance sometimes cited for routine services. Indian transfer pricing does not have a codified simplified method for such services, so the OECD-style approach is persuasive rather than binding. Where the amounts are large, the documentation should be ready before the return is filed, not after the notice arrives. 5. GST reverse charge The import of services from a related person outside India is a taxable supply under IGST. The Indian recipient pays GST under reverse charge under section 5(3) of the IGST Act, and claims input tax credit if the services are used for taxable business. Points that need checking: The activity must be a “supply”, which includes services from a related person even without consideration (Schedule I of the CGST Act), though the open-market value rules in the valuation rules then apply. Time of supply for services from an associated enterprise is the earlier of the date of entry in the books and the date of payment (section 13 of the CGST Act). Accruals matter. Where the Indian entity cannot use the credit (exempt supplies, blocked credits), the GST becomes a cost. 6. Documentation checklist Test What the file should hold Income tax Service agreement, evidence of services, TRC, Form 10F, no-PE declaration Treaty Article analysis and make-available assessment Transfer pricing Benchmarking, allocation keys, Form 3CEB data GST RCM payment, ITC eligibility, time-of-supply check FEMA Remittance through an authorised dealer on the basis of the agreement Practical takeaway: treat the management fee as a three-front exercise. Approve the agreement with the evidence of benefit in mind, price it with a benchmark, and run the tax and GST positions together. A fee that is deductible but wrongly withheld, or correctly withheld but not benchmarked, is still a dispute. Sudheer Nagavarapu is a Partner at A2 Consultants, Hyderabad.

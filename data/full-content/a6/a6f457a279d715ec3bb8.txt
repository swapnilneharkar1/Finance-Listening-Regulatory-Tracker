Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Schueco India Private Limited Vs DCIT (ITAT Mumbai) Summary: The Mumbai Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) deleted an aggregate transfer pricing adjustment of ₹2,34,14,923 in the case of Schueco India Private Limited for AY 2021-22, comprising ₹1,13,63,830 towards know-how and licence fees and ₹1,20,51,093 towards intra-group services. The assessee, a subsidiary of Schueco International KG, Germany, paid royalty of ₹4,54,55,319 at 4% of the net invoice price and benchmarked the transaction using the Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) method. Although the Transfer Pricing Officer (TPO) determined the arm’s-length price (ALP) at nil, the Dispute Resolution Panel (DRP) retained seven comparable licence agreements, resulting in an arm’s-length range of 3% to 4%, but substituted the median rate of 3% for the actual royalty rate. The Tribunal held that under Rule 10CA(5) of the Income-tax Rules, where the actual transaction price falls within the prescribed arm’s-length range, including its upper boundary, the actual price must be accepted as the ALP. Accordingly, it deleted the royalty adjustment without adjudicating the alternative dispute concerning the excluded comparables. Regarding intra-group services, the assessee had paid ₹93,48,993 for Small Business Solutions (SBS), ₹11,25,875 for Customer and Project Services (CPS) and ₹15,76,225 for administrative support services. These transactions were benchmarked using the Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM), with the foreign associated enterprise as the tested party and a contractual mark-up of 5%. The TPO rejected the benchmarking and determined the ALP of all three services at nil, citing deficiencies in the need, evidence, benefit and computation tests. The Tribunal examined contemporaneous service tickets, user records, agreements, invoices, system-access information, operational correspondence and cost-allocation workings. It found that the services had been rendered and possessed commercial or economic utility. No specific duplication or shareholder activity was established. The Tribunal further held that the absence of employee-wise time sheets could not invalidate cost allocations based on locations, users and relative revenue without examining and rejecting those allocation keys on their merits. Rule 10AB requires an objective pricing foundation, and the TPO could not determine the ALP at nil without identifying comparable uncontrolled transactions or other reliable economic material. The Tribunal also rejected the DRP’s alternative disallowance of ₹1,20,51,093 under Section 37(1) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, observing that transfer pricing determination and deductibility of business expenditure involve separate statutory enquiries. The evidence established a direct and proximate nexus between the services and the assessee’s business operations. Genuine business expenditure could not be disallowed merely because the Revenue considered it unnecessary, commercially imprudent or disproportionate to the benefits ultimately realised. Consequently, both transfer pricing adjustments and the alternative disallowance were deleted. The jurisdictional and limitation-related grounds were left open, while the ground challenging initiation of penalty proceedings under Section 270A was treated as premature. The assessee’s appeal was allowed. The Tribunal further clarified that the benefit test for intra-group services cannot be applied with hindsight, and the assessee is not required to demonstrate an immediate increase in revenue or quantifiable profits from every service received. It is sufficient to establish that an independent enterprise could reasonably expect such services to enhance, preserve or facilitate its commercial position. The Tribunal also observed that centralisation of business functions by a foreign associated enterprise does not automatically constitute shareholder activity or duplication of services. Furthermore, different categories of intra-group services must be examined independently unless their aggregation is justified by a close transactional linkage. The Revenue cannot attribute a nil arm’s-length value to all services merely because of perceived deficiencies in the supporting evidence for one category. Accordingly, the Tribunal emphasised that transfer pricing adjustments must be founded on objective economic analysis, reliable evidence and the prescribed statutory benchmarking methodology rather than subjective assessments of commercial necessity or perceived benefits. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT MUMBAI This appeal has been preferred by Schueco India Private Limited against the final assessment order dated 26 September 2024 passed by the Assessing Officer under section 143(3) read with sections 144C(13) and 144B of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (“the Act”), pursuant to the directions dated 30 August 2024 issued by the learned Dispute Resolution Panel (“DRP”) under section 144C(5) of the Act, for the assessment year 2021–22. The assessee filed its return of income on 13 March 2022 declaring a total income of ₹5,50,70,329. In the final assessment order, the total income has been assessed at ₹7,84,85,252 after making an aggregate transfer-pricing adjustment of ₹2,34,14,923, comprising an adjustment of ₹1,13,63,830 in respect of payment of know- how and licence fees and a further adjustment of ₹1,20,51,093 in respect of intra-group services. The assessee has challenged both the adjustments on their factual as well as legal sustainability. Certain grounds have also been raised challenging the validity of the reference made under section 92CA(1), the jurisdiction of the Transfer Pricing Officer (“TPO”), the scope of the directions issued by the DRP under section 144C(8), and the limitation for passing the final assessment order. However, as the appeal is being examined and decided on the merits of the transfer-pricing adjustments, these legal grounds are, for the present, left open. 2. The assessee was incorporated in India on 6 September 2011 as a subsidiary of Schueco International KG, Germany (“Schueco KG” or “the AE”), which is stated to be part of a multinational group having business operations in several countries. The Schueco group is engaged in the development and supply of window, door, sliding and façade systems made from aluminium, steel and other materials. The assessee carries on the business of sale and distribution of aluminium systems, steel profile systems, fittings, accessories and related products used for façades, doors and windows in residential, commercial and industrial projects. Its products are sold principally to fabricators, who use the profiles, fittings and accessories for fabrication and installation in building projects. According to the assessee, its functions are not confined to the purchase and resale of finished goods. It assists its customers and fabricators at various stages of a project by advising them on appropriate system selection and design, defining workshop and production layouts, imparting training on new system construction, and providing technical support in relation to issues arising during fabrication and execution. For discharging these functions, the assessee claims to draw upon the know-how, construction drawings, product specifications, technical information and system- related inputs developed and supplied by Schueco KG. 3. During the relevant previous year, the assessee reported various international transactions in Form No. 3CEB. The transactions relevant to the present appeal were, first, payment of ₹4,54,55,319 to Schueco KG towards know-how and licence fees, calculated at 4 per cent of the net invoice price of the relevant sales; and, secondly, payment of ₹1,20,51,093 towards intra-group services. The latter comprised ₹93,48,993 for Small Business Solutions (“SBS”) running services, ₹11,25,875 for Customer and Project Services (“CPS”), and ₹15,76,225 for administrative support services. The assessee benchmarked the royalty transaction by applying the Comparable Uncontrolled Price (“CUP”) method. Insofar as the intra-group services were concerned, it applied the Transactional Net Margin Method (“TNMM”), treating the foreign AE as the tested party and contending that the services were charged on a cost-plus basis with a mark-up of 5 per cent. The treatment of these transactions by the assessee, the TPO and the DRP may be summarised as under:- Nature of international transaction Amount of transaction Method adopted by assessee Adjustment proposed by TPO Adjustment surviving after DRP Know- how and licenc e fees ₹ 4,54,55,319 CUP; royalty paid at 4% of net invoice price ₹ 4,54,55,319 ₹ 1,13,63,830 SBS running services ₹ 93,48,993 TNMM; AE as tested party CPS services ₹ 11,25,875 TNMM; AE as tested party Administrative support services ₹ 15,76,225 TNMM; AE as tested party Total intra- group services ₹ 1,20,51,093 TNMM; cost plus 5% ₹ 1,20,51,093 ₹ 1,20,51,093 Aggregate adjustment ₹ 5,75,06,412 ₹ 5,75,06,412 ₹ 2,34,14,923 4. The assessee paid know-how and licence fees of ₹4,54,55,319 to Schueco KG under a licence agreement furnished before the TPO along with its submission dated 20 October 2023. As explained by the assessee, the agreement conferred the right to use the know-how, technical data, improvements and trade marks associated with Schueco aluminium systems. The licensed know-how was stated to encompass construction drawings and specifications, technical information relating to inventions, developments, design, production and quality control, as well as other confidential information concerning Schueco products and systems. The trade-mark rights covered the Schueco name and label, names of the profile systems and product-related designations. The assessee submitted that these rights were used not only in relation to the products sold by it but also while furnishing technical guidance to fabricators and customers throughout the course of a building project. In support of its benchmarking, the assessee applied the CUP method and relied upon ten licence agreements identified through the RoyaltyRange database. Its transfer-pricing study reported an arm’s-length range of 3 per cent to 5 per cent, with a median of 4.25 per cent, as against the actual royalty rate of 4 per cent paid by the assessee. 5. The TPO issued a show-cause notice dated 12 October 2023 proposing to reject the assessee’s benchmarking and determine the ALP of the royalty transaction at nil by applying the Other Method. The principal premise of the show-cause notice was that the assessee had been carrying on business in India since September 2011 and had, over a period of about a decade, developed its own presence and goodwill in the Indian market. The TPO observed that the assessee had itself contributed towards building the group’s brand in India through its distribution and marketing activities and, therefore, questioned the commercial justification for requiring the Indian entity to pay know-how and licence fees to its AE. In response, the assessee explained the technical and commercial content of the licensed rights, the continuous product-development and testing functions undertaken by Schueco KG, the role of the Schueco Technology Centre, the technical assistance made available to the Indian entity and the significance of the brand in positioning the products in the premium segment. It further explained that it assisted customers in choosing suitable systems and resolving technical issues, and that it drew upon the AE’s construction drawings, standardised processes, fabrication information and project-specific technical support while discharging those functions. 6. The TPO did not accept the explanation. He observed that, according to the transfer-pricing study itself, the assessee either procured finished goods from its associated enterprises or arranged to have certain goods manufactured through third-party vendors for resale in India. In his view, insofar as finished goods were purchased from the AEs, any technical know-how or licence required for manufacturing those goods would be utilised by the manufacturing entities and not by the assessee acting as a distributor. He held that the assessee had not satisfactorily demonstrated why a distributor of finished products was required to pay royalty for manufacturing-related know-how. He further observed that the assessee had been operating in India for nearly a decade and had incurred expenditure on advertisement, publicity and sales promotion, thereby itself contributing to the recognition and development of the Schueco brand in the Indian market. According to the TPO, the assessee had also not quantified or demonstrated the marketing expenditure incurred by Schueco KG which resulted in a corresponding benefit to the Indian entity. The TPO additionally referred to the purchase of certain capital goods from the AE for imparting training to fabricators and questioned the justification for a separate recurring payment towards technical know-how when equipment intended for such training had already been purchased from the AE. 7. Proceeding on the aforesaid reasoning, the TPO concluded that an independent distributor operating under comparable circumstances would not have paid a third party for know- how and licence rights of the nature claimed by the assessee. He rejected the CUP analysis undertaken in the transfer- pricing study, applied the Other Method, determined the ALP 8 of the payment at nil and proposed an adjustment of the entire amount of ₹4,54,55,319. While doing so, the TPO did not accept the assessee’s explanation that the licensed rights were used in the rendition of project-specific technical support to customers and fabricators and were not restricted to the actual process of manufacturing. He also did not accept the assessee’s reliance upon market positioning, business growth, access to technical experts, continuous training and supplier-related guidance as sufficient justification for the royalty payment. 8. The assessee carried its objections against the proposed adjustment before the DRP. The DRP did not sustain the TPO’s determination of the ALP at nil. It examined the licence agreements selected by the assessee and rejected three of them, namely, those pertaining to Fatigue Solutions Corp., Red Cardinal Holdings Inc. and OCL India Ltd. In the case of Fatigue Solutions Corp., the DRP observed that the licence pertained principally to measurement and testing of metal- fatigue phenomena and that similarity in one aspect of the design process could not render an entity engaged in metallurgical testing comparable with the comprehensive know-how and trade-mark licence under consideration. In the case of Red Cardinal Holdings Inc., the DRP noted that the licensed technology related to the development, manufacture and promotion of code-engineered containers and also 9 involved recycling activities, which, according to it, represented a different technological and commercial field from aluminium profiles and systems used in façades, doors and windows. Insofar as OCL India Ltd. was concerned, the DRP observed that its licence related to the manufacture of porous plugs and monolithic refractory products, and held that the mere presence of metal as a common underlying material did not establish comparability of the technology or know-how. 9. After making the aforesaid exclusions, the DRP granted relief of ₹3,40,91,489 out of the adjustment of ₹4,54,55,319 proposed by the TPO and sustained the balance adjustment of ₹1,13,63,830. As stated in the grounds of appeal and the written submissions, the percentile computation emerging from the comparables retained by the DRP reflected the 35th percentile at 3 per cent and the 65th percentile at 4 per cent, while the median was taken at 3 per cent. The adjustment sustained by the DRP represents the difference between the actual royalty paid at 4 per cent and the rate of 3 per cent adopted by it. The assessee thereafter filed a rectification application before the DRP, contending that once its actual royalty rate of 4 per cent fell within the range beginning at 3 per cent and ending at 4 per cent, Rule 10CA(5) required the actual transaction price to be treated as the ALP and did not permit substitution of the median. This rectification plea, however, did not result in any alteration of the adjustment before the final assessment order was passed. 10. The second transfer-pricing dispute concerns payment of ₹1,20,51,093 for three categories of intra-group services received from Schueco KG. The first and largest component is ₹93,48,993 towards SBS running services. SBS is stated to be the accounting and enterprise-resource application used by the assessee since 2012 and is based on Microsoft Dynamics AX. According to the assessee, the application was used at its Mumbai office and Bengaluru warehouse by personnel working in finance, procurement, operations and planning. Its functions included maintenance of the general ledger, recording of bank and vendor entries, generation of sales and purchase orders, management and movement of inventory, fixed-asset accounting, statutory reporting, and extraction of financial, procurement, inventory and sales reports. The consideration charged by Schueco KG was stated to cover software operations, licences, application support, centrally hosted desktop services, systems and network infrastructure, user administration and backend assistance. The assessee stated that it did not have an independent internal IT team capable of maintaining and supporting the application and that the central IT team of Schueco KG attended to operational tickets, system changes and compliance-related modifications. 11. The second component is ₹11,25,875 towards CPS services. CPS is described as an online customer-relationship and project-management system developed and maintained by Schueco KG for uniform customer classification and segmentation. According to the assessee, the system enabled its management, sales, project-management and operations personnel to record and retrieve current customer and project information, monitor sales-related data, undertake sales steering and prepare uniform reports and analyses. The assessee stated that approximately 35 of its employees used the CPS system during the relevant period and that Schueco KG provided training as well as continuing backend support. The third component is ₹15,76,225 towards administrative support services, which were stated to include controlling and preparation of management reports, investment monitoring, planning and forecasting, treasury functions, collection and processing of banking information, HR reporting and support, compliance and data-protection assistance, legal and tax support, corporate communication, maintenance of transfer- pricing documentation and employee training. 12. The assessee benchmarked these services by treating Schueco KG, the service provider, as the tested party under TNMM. It submitted that the AE was the simpler entity in relation to the controlled services and that reliable information concerning its costs and pricing was available. The services were stated to have been charged at cost plus 5 per cent, though no mark-up was applied to approximately 24 per cent of the total cost; the mark-up was stated to be confined principally to personnel cost and depreciation. Separate searches were undertaken for the three service categories. The assessee reported an arm’s-length range of 4.30 per cent to 10.25 per cent, with a median of 5.17 per cent, for SBS services; 2.33 per cent to 6.97 per cent, with a median of 3.92 per cent, for CPS services; and 1.59 per cent to 7.39 per cent, with a median of 4.57 per cent, for administrative support services. It also placed an alternative entity-level analysis, stating that its operating margin on sales was 3.8 per cent as compared with the range of 1.44 per cent to 5.80 per cent and the median of 2.81 per cent earned by companies engaged in comparable trading activities. 13. The TPO, however, required the assessee to establish not merely the existence of service agreements and invoices, but the actual rendition of the services, the need for obtaining them, the economic or commercial benefit expected or received, the costs actually incurred by the AE, the basis on which those costs were allocated to the assessee, and whether an independent enterprise in comparable circumstances would have been willing to make a similar payment. In the show-cause notice dated 12 October 2023, he observed that an intra-group charge could be accepted at arm’s length only where the taxpayer demonstrated that the services had in fact been received and had conferred a benefit of economic or commercial value. He further referred to the need to exclude shareholder or stewardship activities, duplicative services, passive-association benefits, incidental group benefits and services whose availability was not of sufficient value to induce an independent enterprise to pay for them. The TPO also required evidence of the underlying costs and a rational and scientific allocation methodology connecting the relevant cost pool with the assessee. 14. In response, the assessee furnished copies of the service agreements, invoices, particulars of the teams providing the services, details of SBS and CPS users, extracts of communications and service tickets, examples of issues raised and resolved, system-access information, sales orders and invoices generated through SBS, and illustrations of changes made by the AE in response to requirements of the Indian entity. In relation to SBS, the evidence included instances concerning planned purchase orders, classification of inventory articles, amendments to master data, export- customer information, sales invoicing, GST and TCS-related system changes, creation of ledger accounts, user access, fixed-asset errors and other operational matters. The assessee also furnished cost-allocation workings, stating that SBS infrastructure costs were allocated by reference to the number of locations using the application and licence costs by reference to the number of users. For CPS and administrative support services, the assessee stated that the relevant costs were allocated with reference to its revenue of approximately ₹126.55 crore as a proportion of group revenue, resulting in an allocation factor of approximately 0.8 per cent. It was further submitted that the relevant administrative-service cost pool of Schueco KG was approximately €2 million, of which about €16,000 would have been allocable to the assessee on that basis, though only €14,000 was charged; similarly, against the allocable CPS cost of approximately €13,000 from a cost pool of about €1.6 million, only €10,000 was charged. 15. The TPO examined the material but remained unconvinced. He observed that the agreements, emails, tickets and invoices did not establish that the entire quantum of services for which the payment had been made was actually rendered to the assessee. According to him, the material did not demonstrate that similar payments would have been made by an independent enterprise for comparable services. He held that the assessee had failed to correlate the costs incurred by the AE with specific activities performed for its benefit and had not shown that the amount allocated was commensurate with the benefit received or expected. Particular emphasis was placed upon the absence of employee-wise time sheets; in the TPO’s view, without time records it was not possible to understand how the AE had estimated the resources devoted to the Indian entity. He also observed that some of the claimed services appeared to represent ordinary interaction between a supplier and its customer, while others could fall within group, stewardship or duplicative activities for which an independent entity would not ordinarily pay separately. 16. On this basis, the TPO held that the assessee had failed what he described as the need, evidence and benefit tests, as well as the computation test relating to the amount charged. He rejected the TNMM analysis undertaken by the assessee, including the selection of the foreign AE as the tested party, and adopted the Other Method. Without identifying an independent comparable transaction or assigning a separate value to any particular category of service, he determined the ALP of the entire amount paid for SBS, CPS and administrative support services at nil and proposed an adjustment of ₹1,20,51,093. Thus, although the services were differently described and supported by separate agreements, cost pools and allocation keys, the TPO treated them collectively while arriving at the nil ALP. 17. The assessee objected before the DRP and relied upon the agreements, user information, system records, email correspondence, service tickets, invoices, cost workings and allocation keys furnished during the proceedings. It submitted that SBS was indispensable to its accounting, procurement, sales and inventory operations; that the centralised support of Schueco KG was required because the assessee did not maintain its own internal IT team; that CPS was actively used by its employees for customer and project management; and that the administrative services related to functions ordinarily required for the conduct of any business. It also submitted that the TPO had not identified any duplicative or shareholder activity, had not examined the category-wise evidence, and had determined the ALP at nil without undertaking the analysis contemplated by the Other Method. The assessee further pointed out that the payments were subjected to withholding tax in India and that Schueco KG had offered the receipts to tax, though these aspects were relied upon only as supporting circumstances. 18. The DRP sustained the transfer-pricing adjustment of ₹1,20,51,093. As recorded in the final assessment order, it held that the assessee had failed to establish adequately the exact nature of the services rendered, their nexus with its own business and the basis on which the expenditure had been allocated. Apart from affirming the nil ALP determined by the TPO, the DRP examined the allowability of the expenditure under section 37(1) of the Act. The final assessment order records that an opportunity was granted to the assessee to explain the admissibility of the expenditure and that, after considering its response, the DRP concluded that the business purpose and connection of the expenditure had not been satisfactorily established. It accordingly held that the amount not only had a nil ALP for transfer-pricing purposes but was also not allowable as a deduction under section 37(1). Giving effect to these directions, the Assessing Officer made an aggregate addition of ₹2,34,14,923, consisting of ₹1,13,63,830 towards know-how and licence fees and ₹1,20,51,093 towards intra-group services, and initiated penalty proceedings under section 270A of the Act. 19. Before us, the learned counsel for the assessee submitted at the outset that the grounds challenging the validity of the reference made under section 92CA(1), the jurisdiction of the TPO, the scope of the directions issued by the DRP under section 144C(8), and the limitation for passing the final assessment order need not be adjudicated at this stage if the substantive additions are decided on merits in favour of the assessee. He, therefore, confined his principal submissions to the two transfer-pricing adjustments relating to the payment of know-how and licence fees and the payment for intra-group services. The learned counsel submitted that both adjustments proceed on an erroneous conflation of the existence, commercial necessity and pricing of the international transactions. According to him, once the transactions are evidenced by agreements and their actual rendition or utilisation is established through contemporaneous material, the jurisdiction of the TPO is to determine whether the price paid is at arm’s length by applying one of the prescribed methods; the TPO cannot substitute his own perception of commercial necessity for a methodical determination of the ALP. 20. In relation to the payment of know-how and licence fees, the learned counsel submitted that the TPO proceeded on an incomplete understanding of the assessee’s functional profile by treating it as a mere trader or passive distributor of finished products. He drew our attention to the assessee’s business model and submitted that the products sold by it consisted of profiles, fittings and accessories used by fabricators for the construction of façades, windows and doors. These products were not ordinary finished consumer goods capable of being sold without corresponding technical support. The assessee was required to advise fabricators and customers regarding appropriate systems, design parameters, workshop layouts and methods of construction and installation, besides providing technical assistance when problems arose during the fabrication or execution of a project. The assessee also procured certain products locally from third-party vendors, for which construction drawings, product specifications, quality parameters and other technical inputs made available by Schueco KG were required. Therefore, the proposition that the know-how was used only by the manufacturing AEs and not by the assessee was factually incorrect and overlooked the manner in which the Indian business was actually conducted. 21. The learned counsel further submitted that the payment was made for a composite bundle of valuable rights comprising technical know-how, construction drawings, specifications, technical data, product improvements and the right to use the Schueco trade marks and product-system names. Schueco KG continuously developed and tested new window, door and façade systems and maintained a specialised technology centre for testing the quality, reliability and durability of its products. The Indian entity obtained access to these developments and technical resources without having to establish an independent research, design and testing facility of its own. Similarly, the right to use the Schueco name and trade marks enabled the assessee to market the products as part of an internationally recognised system and to position them in the premium segment. According to the learned counsel, an independent entity seeking comparable technical information, designs, drawings, product support and trade-mark rights would either have had to develop those assets at substantial cost or acquire them from a third party. The payment could not, therefore, be regarded as devoid of commercial or economic value merely because the assessee also carried out distribution and local marketing functions. 22. Addressing the TPO’s observation that the assessee had incurred advertisement, publicity and sales-promotion expenditure and had thereby itself contributed to the brand in India, the learned counsel submitted that ordinary local marketing expenditure incurred by a distributor does not establish either ownership of the underlying trade mark or the absence of value in the licensed brand. No material had been brought on record to show that the assessee had developed any separate marketing intangible or that the expenditure incurred by it was beyond what an independent distributor would ordinarily incur to promote its own sales. Nor had the TPO undertaken any analysis of the nature and quantum of the alleged brand-building expenditure, the functions performed, the assets employed or the risks assumed by the assessee. The fact that the assessee had carried on business in India for about a decade also could not extinguish the AE’s legal ownership of the technology and trade marks or establish that the assessee no longer required access to continuously evolving technical information. The purchase of certain capital goods for training fabricators was likewise said to be irrelevant, because the acquisition of training equipment and the recurring availability of technical know-how, drawings, improvements and trade-mark rights represented different economic benefits. 23. On benchmarking, the learned counsel submitted that the assessee had applied the CUP method and identified ten licence agreements through the RoyaltyRange database. The original analysis yielded an arm’s-length range of 3 per cent to 5 per cent with a median of 4.25 per cent, whereas the assessee had paid royalty at 4 per cent of the net invoice price. More importantly, even after the DRP rejected three of the licence agreements, the range determined on the basis of the comparables retained by it began at 3 per cent, being the 35th percentile, and ended at 4 per cent, being the 65th percentile. Therefore, the actual royalty rate of 4 per cent fell precisely at the upper end of the arm’s-length range accepted by the DRP. Referring to Rule 10CA(4) and (5), the learned counsel submitted that where the dataset comprises six or more entries and the actual price falls within the 35th-to- 65th-percentile range, the price at which the international transaction has actually been undertaken is statutorily deemed to be the ALP. The median assumes relevance only where the actual price is outside the range. Consequently, the DRP could not determine the range as 3 per cent to 4 per cent and simultaneously substitute the median rate of 3 per cent for the actual rate of 4 per cent. It was precisely this apparent inconsistency that the assessee had pointed out in its rectification application before the DRP. 24. Without prejudice to the application of Rule 10CA(5), the learned counsel also assailed the rejection of the three licence agreements. In relation to Fatigue Solutions Corp., he submitted that the licensed technology concerned measurement and analysis of metal fatigue, which had a direct bearing on the durability and structural integrity of products made from metal and was relevant to the technical design of building systems. As regards Red Cardinal Holdings Inc., it was submitted that the agreement concerned technology, know-how and intellectual property connected with code-engineered containers used for safe and sustainable construction, and thus had a sufficient nexus with construction-related technology. In the case of OCL India Ltd., the learned counsel submitted that the licence included the right to manufacture, use and sell products with the aid of specified metal-processing know-how. He contended that an uncontrolled licence need not involve an identical product and that comparability must be tested with reference to the rights granted, the nature of technology, contractual conditions and economic circumstances, with reasonably accurate adjustments being made where required. However, he reiterated that this alternative controversy would become academic once the statutory consequence under Rule 10CA(5) was applied to the range already determined by the DRP. 25. The learned CIT DR, on the other hand, relied upon the reasoning of the TPO and the directions of the DRP. He submitted that the assessee was substantially engaged in purchasing and distributing aluminium profiles, accessories and other products, and had not demonstrated the extent to which the licensed manufacturing know-how was actually utilised by it independently of the manufacturers. The mere availability of group technical information or the ability to approach the AE for assistance did not establish that the entire royalty base and rate represented an arm’s-length consideration. The assessee had already incurred expenditure on local advertising and promotion and had itself developed the market for Schueco products in India. According to the learned CIT DR, the TPO was entitled to examine whether an independent distributor placed in comparable circumstances would have made an additional royalty payment after performing the local marketing and distribution functions. He further submitted that the three agreements rejected by the DRP involved materially different products and technology and that the DRP was justified in excluding them from the dataset. 26. Insofar as Rule 10CA was concerned, the learned CIT DR supported the adjustment as finally sustained by the DRP and submitted that the computation made by the Panel ought to be read in the context of the comparables found to be reliable by it. He contended that the assessee’s original benchmarking could not be accepted merely because the rate of 4 per cent fell within the range claimed in its transfer- pricing study. The comparability of the underlying licence agreements and the correct construction of the final dataset were required to be verified before the benefit of the range mechanism could be extended. He accordingly submitted that the adjustment sustained after the DRP’s directions did not call for interference. In rejoinder, the learned counsel clarified that the assessee was not seeking acceptance of the range computed in its original study; its principal submission was founded upon the 3 per cent to 4 per cent range emerging after the exclusions made by the DRP itself. Once the 65th percentile stood determined at 4 per cent, the actual rate of 4 per cent necessarily fell within the range, and no separate question of commercial prudence could override the deeming provision contained in Rule 10CA(5). 27. Turning to the intra-group services, the learned counsel submitted that the TPO had dealt with three materially different categories of services as though they constituted one undifferentiated management-fee payment and had determined the ALP of the entire amount at nil without evaluating the category-wise evidence. According to him, the arm’s-length character of an intra-group service charge may require examination of whether a genuine business need existed, whether the service was actually rendered, whether it provided or was reasonably expected to provide an economic or commercial benefit, whether the activity duplicated a function already performed by the recipient, and whether it represented a shareholder or stewardship function undertaken solely because of the provider’s ownership interest. However, these tests had to be applied to the actual functions and evidence. They could not be invoked in the abstract to disregard the transaction or to dispense with the statutory requirement of determining its ALP by a recognised method. 28. Elaborating upon the need test, the learned counsel submitted that SBS was the assessee’s principal accounting and enterprise-resource application and was used continuously by its finance, procurement, operations and planning personnel at the Mumbai office and Bengaluru warehouse. It supported the maintenance of accounts, vendor and bank entries, purchase and sales orders, inventory management, fixed-asset records, statutory processes and the generation of financial, procurement, inventory and sales reports. The assessee did not maintain a separate internal IT 26 team capable of hosting, maintaining or modifying the system. The software licences, infrastructure, user administration, application maintenance and backend support were centrally provided by Schueco KG. Similarly, CPS was not a general facility made available merely because the assessee belonged to the group; it was an operative customer and project-management application used by approximately 35 employees of the assessee for customer classification, project information, sales monitoring, sales steering and uniform reporting. The administrative services addressed routine business requirements relating to controlling, treasury, planning, HR, compliance, data protection, legal, tax, communication and training. Thus, each service had an identifiable connection with the day-to- day conduct of the assessee’s business. 29. On the rendition test, the learned counsel referred to the service agreements, invoices, composition of the relevant service teams, lists of SBS and CPS users, access records, communications, service tickets and examples of issues raised by the Indian personnel and resolved by the AE. He submitted that the records included specific instances concerning preparation and correction of purchase orders, classification of inventory articles, amendments to article and customer master data, sales invoicing, export transactions, creation of ledger accounts, allocation of user access, fixed- asset entries and implementation of changes necessitated by Indian GST, TCS and electronic-invoicing requirements. The documents also contained examples of network support, new financial-year series, pricing uploads, software upgrades and assistance in generating credit notes. These were contemporaneous operational records identifying the Indian employee raising the issue, the concerned AE support personnel, the nature of the request and, in several instances, the solution provided. Therefore, this was not a case in which rendition was sought to be proved merely through agreements, debit notes or generic email correspondence. 30. With regard to the benefit test, the learned counsel submitted that the appropriate enquiry was whether the activity provided, or could reasonably have been expected to provide, economic or commercial value to the recipient such that an independent enterprise would have been willing either to pay for it or to perform it internally. The assessee was not required to demonstrate an immediate or arithmetically measurable increase in revenue attributable to each support activity. Services relating to accounting systems, inventory controls, customer information, compliance, treasury, HR, legal and tax functions enabled the business to operate efficiently, maintain its records, comply with statutory requirements and manage its customers and projects. The benefit of such services lay in the availability and use of the systems and specialised support and could not always be traced to a particular sale or unit of revenue. The assessee’s growth over the years was relied upon as part of the commercial background and not as the sole proof of benefit. 31. As regards the duplicate-service test, the learned counsel submitted that neither the TPO nor the DRP had identified any corresponding function already performed by the assessee or procured by it from an independent service provider. In particular, the uncontroverted explanation was that the assessee did not maintain an internal IT team capable of providing SBS backend support. The Indian personnel used the system for their respective business functions, whereas its hosting, configuration, user controls, upgrades and resolution of technical issues were undertaken centrally by Schueco KG. The performance of accounting or sales functions by Indian employees could not be regarded as duplication of the AE’s IT support merely because both concerned the same software environment. Likewise, no specific local department or external service provider had been identified as performing the same CPS, treasury, compliance, data-protection or other support functions for which the charge had been made. 32. On the shareholder-activity test, the learned counsel submitted that an expenditure could be excluded from an intra-group service charge where the activity was undertaken solely because of the AE’s ownership interest, such as costs of the parent’s shareholder meetings, preparation of consolidated accounts for its own reporting, raising funds to acquire or hold subsidiaries, or compliance obligations resting only upon the shareholder. However, the authorities had not identified any specific activity or amount falling within this description. They had referred generally to shareholder, stewardship and group activities without examining the actual services for which the assessee was charged. Activities such as supporting the Indian entity’s accounting software, customer-management system, statutory compliance, local tax matters, HR administration or treasury operations could not be characterised as shareholder activities merely because they were performed centrally by the parent or another group entity. If any particular element of the cost pool represented a shareholder activity, it had to be identified and excluded on the basis of evidence; the entire charge could not be reduced to nil on a general presumption. 33. The learned counsel then addressed the quantification and allocation of the service charges. He submitted that SBS infrastructure expenditure was allocated with reference to the number of locations using the application, while licence costs were allocated with reference to the number of users. These allocation keys bore a direct relationship to the availability and consumption of the relevant resources. Insofar as CPS and administrative services were concerned, the assessee’s share of group revenue, stated to be approximately 0.8 per cent, was used as the allocation key. For the relevant year, the administrative-service cost pool of Schueco KG was approximately €2 million, against which the amount allocable to the assessee at 0.8 per cent was approximately €16,000; nevertheless, only €14,000 was charged. Similarly, from the CPS cost pool of approximately €1.6 million, the proportionate amount came to approximately €13,000, whereas only €10,000 was charged. A mark-up of 5 per cent was applied only to specified personnel costs and depreciation, while approximately 24 per cent of the underlying costs were passed on without any mark-up. The learned counsel submitted that the TPO’s insistence on employee-wise time sheets overlooked the fact that the principal allocation keys for the respective services were locations, users and revenue, rather than hours spent by individual employees. 34. On the benchmarking methodology, the learned counsel submitted that Schueco KG was treated as the tested party because, in relation to the services, it performed the comparatively less complex service-provider functions and reliable information concerning its costs and remuneration was available. The foreign comparables yielded ranges of 4.30 per cent to 10.25 per cent for SBS services, 2.33 per cent to 6.97 per cent for CPS services, and 1.59 per cent to 7.39 per cent for administrative support services. The mark-up of 5 per cent fell within each of these ranges. Even if the Indian entity were considered as the tested party, its operating margin of 3.8 per cent was stated to be higher than the median margin of 2.81 per cent of comparable trading companies, and the service charges formed part of its operating-cost base. According to the learned counsel, the TPO rejected both analyses without undertaking any examination of the comparables, the tested party or the margins and merely invoked the Other Method. No comparable uncontrolled transaction, independent quotation, valuation, market price or other reliable economic material was identified to support a nil price. The alleged failure of the benefit test was thus used as a substitute for the determination of ALP contemplated by section 92C read with Rules 10B, 10C and 10AB. 35. The learned counsel also challenged the DRP’s alternative disallowance under section 37(1). He submitted that a transfer-pricing adjustment and a disallowance of business expenditure operate in different fields. The first concerns the arm’s-length price of a controlled transaction, whereas the second concerns whether the expenditure was laid out wholly and exclusively for the purposes of business and was not otherwise prohibited. Once the agreements, system records, tickets, user information, invoices and cost workings established the receipt and business use of the services, the expenditure could not be disallowed merely because the authorities considered that the assessee did not derive a sufficiently commensurate benefit. Commercial expediency had to be viewed from the standpoint of the businessperson. The fact that tax had been withheld from the remittances and the AE had filed its return of income in India was relied upon as corroborative of the disclosed and genuine nature of the transaction, though the learned counsel fairly submitted that these circumstances by themselves did not determine the ALP or conclusively establish deductibility. 36. The learned CIT DR strongly supported the orders of the authorities below. He submitted that, in an intra-group arrangement, agreements and invoices could be generated between related parties and would not, by themselves, establish that services of corresponding value had actually been rendered. Even where certain emails or service tickets were produced, the assessee was required to show that the samples were representative of the entire charge and that the cost allocated to India related to activities actually performed for its benefit. According to him, many of the documents demonstrated routine interaction within the group or normal support associated with the supply of products and systems. The assessee had not furnished employee-wise time records or sufficiently detailed evidence showing the personnel involved, the time devoted, the nature of each activity and the costs attributable to India. In the absence of such evidence, the TPO was justified in holding that the allocation lacked reliability. 37. The learned CIT DR further submitted that the presence of software users or the availability of a group system did not automatically establish that every cost included in the central pool represented a chargeable service to the assessee. A centralised activity might serve the interests of the group as a whole, confer only an incidental benefit upon a subsidiary, duplicate a locally performed function or arise from the parent’s need to supervise and protect its investment. The assessee was required to segregate such non-chargeable activities before allocating the cost. Insofar as administrative support was concerned, the service descriptions were broad and covered controlling, treasury, HR, compliance, legal, tax and communication; without activity-wise evidence, it was not possible to ascertain whether the charge included shareholder or stewardship functions. The learned CIT DR also contended that revenue might not constitute an appropriate allocation key for every service, because the level of revenue does not necessarily correspond to the extent to which legal, HR, treasury or compliance support is consumed. 38. On the selection of the method, the learned CIT DR submitted that the assessee had not established that the foreign AE was the least complex entity or that sufficiently reliable segmented financial information was available to treat it as the tested party. The foreign comparable companies and the basis on which they were selected also required close examination. The mere fact that the contractual mark-up of 5 per cent fell within the ranges asserted by the assessee would not establish the arm’s-length character of the total charge unless the underlying cost base, allocation and actual rendition were first shown to be reliable. He accordingly submitted that the TPO was justified in rejecting the assessee’s TNMM analysis and determining the ALP under the Other Method. He also supported the DRP’s conclusion under section 37(1), contending that the assessee had failed to establish the precise business nexus and actual services corresponding to the expenditure claimed. 39. In rejoinder, the learned counsel submitted that the Revenue’s arguments continued to proceed on general propositions without confronting the transaction-specific evidence. The TPO had neither demonstrated that the services were duplicated nor identified any component attributable to shareholder activity. No defect had been pointed out in the reconciliation of the service charges with the invoices and books, nor had the TPO brought any independent material to show that the stated allocation keys were commercially irrational. The absence of time sheets could not negate services allocated on the basis of identifiable users, locations or revenue, particularly when contemporaneous records demonstrated the actual operation of the systems and the support furnished by the AE. Above all, even if the authorities entertained doubts regarding a particular cost component or allocation key, the proper course was to determine an arm’s- length charge by applying the statutory methodology; the ALP of three operationally distinct services could not be reduced to nil through a composite conclusion that the assessee had failed the need, evidence and benefit tests. 40. We have heard the rival submissions and examined the orders of the authorities below and the material referred to by the parties. The two adjustments, though both arise under the transfer-pricing provisions, involve distinct transactions and require separate examination. The payment of royalty turns principally upon the nature of the licensed rights and the statutory consequence of the arm’s-length range determined by the DRP, whereas the intra-group service dispute requires an assessment of the need, actual rendition and expected benefit of each service, the possibility of duplication or shareholder activity, the reliability of the cost- allocation mechanism and, ultimately, the method by which the ALP has been determined. 41. We have carefully considered the rival submissions, perused the orders of the TPO and the DRP and examined the material referred to before us. The two adjustments arise from separate international transactions and must be adjudicated independently. The first concerns payment for know-how, technical information and licence rights, where the immediate question is the statutory consequence of the arm’s-length range determined by the DRP. The second concerns three categories of intra-group services, where the enquiry extends to the nature and actual rendition of the services, their commercial or economic utility to the assessee, the possibility of duplication or shareholder activity, the reliability of the cost-allocation mechanism and, ultimately, the manner in which their ALP has been determined. While commercial need and benefit may form part of the examination of an intra- group service, they cannot supplant the pricing exercise mandated by section 92C read with the applicable Rules. Equally, the mere existence of an agreement or an invoice cannot, by itself, establish that a service has been rendered or that the entire amount charged represents an arm’s-length consideration. The matter has, therefore, to be determined by examining the contemporaneous evidence and thereafter applying the prescribed transfer-pricing methodology. 42. The assessee paid ₹4,54,55,319 to Schueco KG at 4 per cent of the net invoice price under the know-how and licence agreement. The rights covered by the agreement, as borne out from the material placed before the authorities, were not confined merely to the use of a trade name on products purchased from the AE. They included technical know-how, construction drawings and specifications, technical information relating to product development, design, production and quality control, improvements in the Schueco systems and the right to use the associated trade marks and system names. The functional profile placed before us also shows that the assessee was not merely purchasing packaged finished goods and reselling them without any corresponding technical activity. It sold profiles, fittings and accessories to fabricators, who utilised them in the construction of façades, windows and doors, and it assisted such fabricators and customers with system selection, designs, workshop layouts, training and resolution of technical issues during the execution of projects. Certain products were also locally sourced or manufactured through third-party vendors with the aid of specifications and technical inputs originating from Schueco KG. Thus, the description of the assessee as a distributor, though broadly correct, does not by itself exhaust the functions actually undertaken by it or establish that the licensed rights had no utility in its business. 43. The TPO’s reasoning proceeds principally on the premise that technical know-how is required only by the entity physically manufacturing the product and not by an entity engaged in its distribution. Such a proposition cannot be accepted as an invariable transfer-pricing principle. The commercial use of technical information depends upon the nature of the product and the functions performed by the licensee. In the case of engineered building systems, the distributor may also require drawings, specifications, performance parameters, installation information and continuing technical support in order to advise customers, train fabricators and ensure that components are properly configured for a particular project. The fact that an associated enterprise or a third-party vendor manufactures a component does not necessarily mean that the Indian entity supplying the system and providing project-related assistance makes no use of the underlying technical information. Nor can the need for continuous access to product developments and technical improvements be negated merely because the assessee had been carrying on business in India for about a decade. 44. The other reasoning adopted by the TPO was that the assessee had incurred expenditure on advertisement, publicity and sales promotion and had thereby itself contributed to the recognition of the Schueco brand in India. There is, however, no functional or economic analysis in the TPO’s order demonstrating that the assessee had undertaken any non-routine brand-development activity, incurred advertisement expenditure beyond that ordinarily borne by an independent distributor, or developed a marketing intangible economically owned by it. Local selling and promotional expenditure incurred by a distributor to augment its own sales cannot, without further examination, lead to the conclusion that the trade mark licensed by the foreign owner had ceased to possess value or that the distributor should not pay for its use. Likewise, the purchase of certain capital goods for training fabricators cannot be equated with, or treated as a substitute for, continuing access to construction drawings, system specifications, technical improvements and trade- mark rights. These matters may be relevant while examining the functions of the parties and the comparability of licence agreements, but they do not furnish an independent method for determining the ALP at nil. 45. Be that as it may, the entire debate regarding the TPO’s determination at nil assumes limited significance because the DRP itself did not approve that determination. It examined the licence comparables, excluded three agreements and, on the dataset retained by it, determined the 35th percentile at 3 per cent and the 65th percentile at 4 per cent. The surviving adjustment of ₹1,13,63,830 was made by adopting 3 per cent against the actual rate of 4 per cent. The assessee’s principal contention is that, once the DRP’s own range extended up to 4 per cent, the transaction price fell within the arm’s-length range and had to be accepted under Rule 10CA(5). The issue, therefore, is not whether the original range of 3 per cent to 5 per cent claimed by the assessee in its transfer-pricing study should be accepted; it is whether the actual rate of 4 per cent can be substituted by the median after the DRP’s own exercise placed that rate within the final range. 46. Rule 10CA(4) provides that where the most appropriate method is a method other than the Profit Split Method referred to in section 92C(1)(d) or the Other Method referred to in section 92C(1)(f), and the dataset constructed in accordance with sub-rule (2) consists of six or more entries, an arm’s-length range beginning from the 35th percentile and ending at the 65th percentile shall be constructed. Under Rule 10CA(5), if the price at which the international transaction has actually been undertaken is within that range, the actual transaction price shall be deemed to be the ALP. Rule 10CA(6) applies to the different situation where the actual transaction price falls outside the range; only in that situation is the ALP taken to be the median of the dataset. The statutory scheme thus draws a clear distinction between an actual price falling within the range and one falling outside it. The deeming consequence under sub-rule (5) cannot be neutralised by importing the median into a case where the actual price is already within the range. 47. In the present case, the CUP method was applied to the licence transaction, the final dataset retained after the DRP’s exclusions consisted of the requisite number of entries, and the range determined by the DRP began at 3 per cent and ended at 4 per cent. The assessee’s actual royalty rate of 4 per cent lies at the upper boundary of that range. A boundary value is as much within the range as any value lying between its two endpoints; otherwise, the expression “beginning from” the 35th percentile and “ending on” the 65th percentile would lose its ordinary and statutory meaning. Consequently, Rule 10CA(5) mandates that the price actually paid at 4 per cent be deemed to be the ALP. The substitution of 3 per cent merely because it was regarded as the median is therefore inconsistent with the sequence and consequence contemplated by Rule 10CA(4) to (6). 48. Once the transaction stands resolved by applying Rule 10CA(5) to the range determined by the DRP, the assessee’s alternative challenge to the rejection of Fatigue Solutions Corp., Red Cardinal Holdings Inc. and OCL India Ltd. does not require adjudication. The inclusion of those agreements could only operate in favour of widening or otherwise altering the range claimed by the assessee; their exclusion does not 42 take the actual rate of 4 per cent outside the range finally determined by the DRP. We, therefore, leave the comparability of those agreements open. The adjustment of ₹1,13,63,830 sustained on account of payment for know-how and licence fees is deleted, and the relevant grounds raised by the assessee are allowed. 49. We now advert to the adjustment of ₹1,20,51,093 relating to SBS running services, CPS services and administrative support services. In the context of an intra-group service, the arm’s-length principle requires examination of whether a genuine need for the activity existed in the recipient’s business; whether the service was actually rendered or made available; whether it conferred, or was reasonably expected to confer, an economic or commercial benefit; whether the same activity was already performed by the recipient itself or procured from another service provider; and whether any part of the activity was undertaken solely because of the provider’s shareholder interest. These may conveniently be described as the need, rendition, benefit, duplicate-service and shareholder-activity tests. They are not five watertight or mutually exclusive compartments, but interconnected facets of the enquiry whether an independent enterprise in comparable circumstances would have been willing to pay for the activity or to perform it in-house. 50. There are, however, two important qualifications. First, the benefit test is not a test of hindsight. The assessee is not required to establish that every service produced an immediate increase in revenue or an arithmetically measurable profit. It is sufficient if, at the time the activity was undertaken, an independent enterprise could reasonably have expected it to enhance, preserve or facilitate its commercial position. Services relating to accounting systems, inventory control, customer information, statutory compliance, treasury, legal or human-resource functions may be necessary to operate a business even though the financial benefit from each individual activity cannot be traced to a particular sale. Secondly, an examination of need, rendition and benefit cannot dispense with the statutory pricing exercise. If a chargeable service has been rendered, the TPO must determine its ALP by applying one of the methods prescribed under section 92C read with Rules 10B and 10AB. Dissatisfaction with the degree of benefit cannot, by itself, constitute a method for arriving at nil. 51. The jurisdiction of the TPO is to determine the arm’s- length price and not to decide how an assessee ought to conduct its business or whether it could have managed without incurring the expenditure. The Hon’ble Delhi High Court in CIT v. EKL Appliances Ltd. [2012] 345 ITR 241 (Delhi) explained that the Revenue ordinarily cannot question the commercial expediency of a transaction and substitute its own judgment for that of the assessee, though the TPO is entitled to examine the transaction and determine its arm’s- length value under the prescribed provisions. In CIT v. Cushman and Wakefield (India) (P.) Ltd. [2014] 367 ITR 730 (Delhi), it was further explained that the question whether an expenditure is deductible and the determination of the ALP fall within their respective statutory fields: the TPO determines the ALP, whereas the Assessing Officer examines deductibility under the general provisions. The authorities can certainly examine whether the alleged service was actually rendered, because a non-existent service can have no arm’s-length price; but once rendition is established, its value must be determined through the statutory methodology rather than through a general perception that the service was unnecessary or insufficiently beneficial. 52. SBS is not described in the record as an amorphous managerial facility available to all group entities. It is the accounting and enterprise-resource application used by the assessee in its daily operations. It was utilised by its finance, procurement, operations and planning teams at its Mumbai office and Bengaluru warehouse for general-ledger and bank entries, recording vendor invoices, generation of purchase and sales orders, management and movement of inventory, fixed- asset accounting and extraction of financial, procurement, 45 inventory and sales reports. The service charge covered the software licences, centrally hosted infrastructure, application maintenance, user administration, system modifications and backend support provided by the specialised team of Schueco KG. The assessee’s statement that it did not have an internal IT team independently capable of hosting and supporting the SBS application has not been displaced by any contrary material. 53. The evidence referred to in the record is also not confined to the service agreement and invoices. It includes the names of Indian users, access details, operational tickets, email exchanges and instances of specific assistance provided during the relevant period. The illustrations concern correction of planned purchase orders, classification of inventory articles, changes in article and customer master data, amendments to sales-invoice processes, export transactions, ledger creation, user access, fixed-asset errors, pricing uploads, new financial-year series, network issues and software upgrades. There are also instances of modifications or support relating to Indian GST, TCS and electronic- invoicing requirements. These are activities connected with the operation and maintenance of the system used by the assessee and do not constitute merely general advice or passive membership benefits. 54. The TPO referred to some interactions as routine exchanges between a customer and supplier. That observation does not deal with the substance of the SBS service. Schueco KG was not merely supplying a finished product and responding to ordinary product-related queries; it hosted and maintained the enterprise application through which significant accounting, procurement, sales and inventory functions of the assessee were carried out. The use of the application by the Indian employees and the provision of backend technical support by the AE are complementary functions, not duplication of each other. No corresponding internal IT department or independent vendor providing the same backend services has been identified. Nor is the maintenance of the assessee’s operating and accounting systems an activity undertaken solely because Schueco KG held shares in the assessee. On the material brought on record, the SBS services satisfy the need, rendition and benefit tests and do not fail the duplicate-service or shareholder-activity tests. 55. CPS was the customer-relationship and project- management system used for maintaining customer and project information, customer classification and segmentation, sales tracking, sales steering and uniform reporting. The material referred to by the assessee included the list of approximately 35 users drawn from its management, sales, project-management and operations functions, besides evidence of training and backend assistance by Schueco KG. The existence of identified Indian users and the operational use of the system for managing customer and project data demonstrate that the assessee did not receive merely an incidental benefit arising from its association with the group. Access to an operative customer- management platform, together with its maintenance and support, constitutes a service for which an independent enterprise without a comparable internal platform could reasonably be expected to pay. 56. No material has been brought on record to show that the assessee maintained another customer-relationship system performing the same functions or had separately engaged an independent provider for identical services. Nor has the TPO identified any element of the CPS charge that related to the preparation of shareholder reports, protection of the parent’s investment or another activity undertaken solely in the capacity of shareholder. The mere fact that the system standardised customer information across the Schueco group does not transform the benefit received by the assessee into an incidental or shareholder benefit when its own employees used the system for its Indian customers and projects. The CPS services, therefore, also satisfy the relevant service tests. 57. Administrative support services require closer scrutiny because their description covers a wider set of functions, including controlling, treasury, planning and forecasting, HR reporting, compliance, data protection, legal, tax, communication and training. A broad contractual description would not, by itself, prove that each activity was rendered or was chargeable. However, the assessee did not rely only upon the agreement. It referred to the correspondence and supporting material concerning the respective functions and furnished the underlying cost pool, allocation working and invoices. The activities were stated to include preparation and coordination of management information and forecasts, investment monitoring, banking and treasury support, HR- related reporting, compliance and data-protection assistance, local legal and tax support and maintenance of transfer- pricing documentation. These are functions having an evident nexus with the conduct, regulation and administration of the Indian business. 58. The authorities below have referred generally to possible shareholder, stewardship or duplicative activities, but no particular activity included in the charge has been identified as having been undertaken solely to protect the parent’s investment or comply with an obligation resting exclusively upon the shareholder. There is equally no finding identifying the department or service provider in India that performed the same activity for the assessee. The distinction is material. An activity does not become a shareholder activity merely because it is centrally performed by the parent entity, and an incidental group benefit cannot be presumed merely because standardised processes are used across the group. The character of the activity must be determined from the purpose it serves. Where controlling, treasury, compliance, HR, legal or tax support is directed towards the operations and obligations of the Indian entity, it constitutes a service to that entity even though centralisation may also achieve economies for the group. 59. The assessee is not required to establish a separate stream of revenue generated by each administrative activity. An independent enterprise ordinarily incurs expenditure on accounts, planning, treasury, HR, compliance, legal and tax functions because these enable it to conduct and regulate its business. Their benefit lies in supporting the business and mitigating operational and regulatory risks, and not necessarily in generating identifiable revenue on a transaction-by-transaction basis. In the absence of any specific finding that a component was not rendered, was duplicated, or related solely to shareholder activity, the entire administrative-support charge could not be rejected by describing the evidence collectively as generic. The material placed before the authorities, considered together with the nature of the assessee’s business and the absence of any identified duplication, establishes the need, rendition and expected benefit of these services. 60. Establishing that services were rendered does not, by itself, establish that any amount charged for them is at arm’s length. The cost pool, allocation key and mark-up must also bear a reasonable relationship to the services and the benefits expected by the recipient. In the present case, the assessee explained that SBS infrastructure costs were allocated by reference to the number of locations using the system, while licence costs were allocated by the number of users. These keys bear a rational connection with the resources supplied: infrastructure is deployed at the locations where the system operates and licence expenditure ordinarily varies with the number of authorised users. For CPS and administrative support services, the assessee’s share of group revenue, stated at approximately 0.8 per cent, was adopted. Revenue may not be a suitable allocation key for every conceivable administrative activity, but its use cannot be rejected in the abstract; it is a recognised proxy for the relative scale of business where services are not susceptible to direct tracing and no more accurate measure of consumption is available. 61. The assessee also furnished the relevant cost pools and the amounts allocated from them. Against the administrative- service cost pool of approximately €2 million, the proportionate allocation at 0.8 per cent was stated to be approximately €16,000, although only €14,000 was charged. Against the CPS cost pool of approximately €1.6 million, the allocable amount was stated to be approximately €13,000, whereas the amount charged was €10,000. A mark-up of 5 per cent was applied only to personnel cost and depreciation, while approximately 24 per cent of the underlying expenditure was passed on without a mark-up. These figures and workings were furnished before the authorities and have not been shown to be arithmetically irreconcilable with the invoices or books. The TPO’s principal objection was the absence of employee-wise time sheets. Time records may be material where costs are allocated by reference to employee hours, but they cannot be regarded as the only permissible evidence of allocation. Where the stated keys are locations, users and relative revenue, the absence of time sheets does not, without examining and rejecting those keys on their own merits, render the entire cost allocation unreliable. 62. The assessee benchmarked the service-provider’s remuneration under TNMM and reported an arm’s-length range of 4.30 per cent to 10.25 per cent for SBS, 2.33 per cent to 6.97 per cent for CPS and 1.59 per cent to 7.39 per cent for administrative support services. The 5 per cent mark- up fell within each of these ranges. It also furnished an entity-level analysis showing its operating margin at 3.8 per cent against the median of 2.81 per cent earned by the selected trading comparables. The latter analysis is, at best, corroborative because entity-level profitability cannot invariably establish the arm’s-length character of every separately identifiable controlled transaction. Nevertheless, the TPO did not analyse the foreign comparables, demonstrate that the AE could not be treated as the tested party, or substitute another reliable set of comparables. The assessee’s analysis was rejected substantially because the TPO was dissatisfied with the need, evidence and benefit demonstrated for the services. 63. After rejecting TNMM, the TPO referred to the Other Method and determined the ALP at nil. Rule 10AB permits determination of the ALP on the basis of the price which has been charged or paid, or would have been charged or paid, for the same or a similar uncontrolled transaction, having regard to all relevant facts. The flexibility of the Other Method does not dispense with the need for an objective pricing foundation. The TPO must identify some comparable uncontrolled price, independent quotation, valuation, market information, third-party arrangement or other reliable economic material from which the price that would have been charged between independent parties can reasonably be inferred. In the present case, no uncontrolled transaction or external pricing material was identified. Nor was any separate value assigned to SBS, CPS or administrative support services. The entire payment was valued at nil because the assessee was held to have failed the need, evidence, benefit and computation tests. 64. Such an approach cannot be regarded as a determination of ALP under the Other Method. A conclusion that evidence is insufficient may justify further verification or rejection of a particular cost component, but it does not establish that the arm’s-length value of an operative accounting system, customer-management platform and business-support services is nil. A nil value would require a finding, supported by the record, that no service was rendered, that the service was wholly duplicative or shareholder-related, or that an independent enterprise would not have paid anything for it. The contemporaneous operational records in the present case negate the first proposition; no specific duplication has been identified; and no particular shareholder activity or corresponding amount has been isolated. The nil determination, therefore, does not emanate from a recognised pricing analysis and lacks adequate evidentiary foundation. 65. It is also significant that the three service categories were treated collectively, notwithstanding their different functions, allocation keys and supporting records. Even if a doubt existed regarding some part of the broadly described administrative services, that could not justify attributing a nil value to SBS and CPS, whose operational use was demonstrated through identified users, system functions and support records. Transfer-pricing analysis must ordinarily follow the transaction as actually undertaken, unless aggregation is justified by close linkage. Aggregation cannot be employed merely to allow a perceived weakness in one service category to erase the value of other independently demonstrated services. On the evidence before us, the services were rendered, possessed economic or commercial utility for the assessee, were not shown to be duplicative or shareholder-related, and were charged through disclosed cost-allocation mechanisms with a mark-up falling within the ranges produced by the assessee. In the absence of a contrary benchmarking exercise by the TPO, the adjustment of ₹1,20,51,093 cannot be sustained. 66. The DRP additionally held that the payment for intra- group services was not allowable under section 37(1) because the assessee had failed to establish the exact nature of the services and their nexus with its business. This conclusion must be examined separately from the transfer-pricing adjustment. The determination of the ALP addresses whether the consideration paid to the AE corresponds to what independent parties would have agreed, whereas section 37(1) addresses whether the expenditure was laid out wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the assessee’s business and is not capital, personal or otherwise prohibited. A finding on one enquiry does not automatically conclude the other. 67. The nature and use of the services have already been examined in detail. SBS was used in the assessee’s accounting, procurement, sales, inventory, fixed-asset and statutory-compliance processes; CPS was used by its personnel for customer and project management; and the administrative services related to controlling, treasury, HR, compliance, legal, tax and allied business functions. Contemporaneous system records, user details, service tickets, communications, agreements, invoices and cost- allocation workings were produced. These materials establish a direct and proximate nexus between the expenditure and the conduct of the assessee’s business. There is no finding that the expenditure was capital or personal in nature, prohibited by law, or incurred for a purpose extraneous to the business. The circumstance that tax was withheld on the remittance and the AE disclosed the receipt is not decisive of allowability, but it is consistent with the transaction having been recorded and subjected to the applicable tax process. 68. The test under section 37(1) is not whether the Revenue considers the expenditure prudent, indispensable or proportionate to the benefit ultimately realised. The assessee is entitled to arrange its business and obtain services which it considers commercially appropriate, provided the expenditure is genuine and incurred for the purposes of business. The authorities may examine whether the services were in fact rendered and whether the expenditure has a business nexus, but cannot disallow an otherwise genuine business expenditure merely because, in their view, the assessee could have conducted its affairs differently or derived a greater benefit. Since the actual receipt and business use of the services stand demonstrated from the material on record, the alternative disallowance of ₹1,20,51,093 under section 37(1) is also unsustainable and is deleted. 69. We accordingly hold that the royalty rate of 4 per cent falls within the arm’s-length range of 3 per cent to 4 per cent determined by the DRP and, by virtue of Rule 10CA(5), the price actually paid is required to be treated as the ALP. The adjustment of ₹1,13,63,830 in respect of know-how and licence fees is deleted. The alternative dispute concerning the comparability of the three licence agreements excluded by the DRP is left open as academic. 70. Insofar as the intra-group services are concerned, the contemporaneous evidence establishes the commercial need for and actual rendition of SBS, CPS and administrative support services, together with the economic or commercial utility expected from them. The Revenue has not identified any specific duplication or shareholder activity forming part of the charge. The cost-allocation mechanisms have been disclosed and the contractual mark-up of 5 per cent falls within the ranges produced in the assessee’s benchmarking analysis. The TPO, after rejecting that analysis, did not undertake an objective determination under Rule 10AB and arrived at nil without identifying any comparable uncontrolled transaction or other reliable pricing material. The transfer- pricing adjustment of ₹1,20,51,093 is consequently deleted. For the reasons separately recorded, the alternative disallowance of the same amount under section 37(1) is also deleted. 71. Since the substantive additions have been deleted on merits, the grounds challenging the validity of the reference under section 92CA(1), the jurisdiction of the TPO, the legality of the DRP’s directions and the limitation for passing the final assessment order are left open and are not adjudicated. The ground challenging the initiation of penalty proceedings under section 270A is premature and requires no adjudication at this stage. In the result, the appeal of the assessee is allowed in terms indicated above. 72. In the result, appeal of the assessee is allowed. Order pronounced on 5th October, 2026.

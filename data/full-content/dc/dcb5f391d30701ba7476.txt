(Bombay High Court) Bona Fide Capital Increase Causing Share Dilution Not Oppression: Bombay High Court The Bombay High Court has dismissed a shareholder’s appeal challenging an increase in share capital that allegedly reduced his holding from 50% to approximately 8%. The Court upheld the finding that the capital enhancement was undertaken to meet genuine financial requirements for factory reconstruction, rather than to oppress the appellant. The judgment was delivered by Justice Abhay Ahuja on 5 November 2026 in Shri Rajiv Kant Laxman v. Bobby Electronics Pvt. Ltd. and Others, Company Appeal No.30 of 2014, bearing Neutral Citation No.2026:BHC-OS:24315. Background of the Dispute The appellant, a non-resident Indian residing in London, claimed to hold 50% equity in Bobby Electronics Pvt. Ltd. He alleged oppression and mismanagement, including unauthorised share allotments, improper appointment of directors, diversion of funds and denial of inspection. His principal grievance was that the respondents increased the company’s authorised capital from ₹10 lakh to ₹70 lakh, reducing his percentage holding while increasing the second respondent’s holding to approximately 92%. The Company Law Board, by its order dated 4 December 2013, dismissed his petition under the Companies Act, 1956. The appellant challenged that decision under Section 10F. After an earlier dismissal by the High Court, the Supreme Court remanded the matter in 2017 for fresh consideration, particularly of the alleged unauthorised capital increase and dilution without notice. Limited Scope of Appeal Under Section 10F The High Court emphasised that Section 10F permits an appeal on a question of law. It does not ordinarily authorise fresh appreciation of evidence or substitution of the Company Law Board’s factual conclusions. Interference may be warranted where findings are perverse, unsupported by evidence, arbitrary or contrary to law. A question of law may also arise from disregarding relevant material or relying upon irrelevant considerations. The appellant therefore needed to establish a legal defect in the Board’s conclusions. Offering an alternative interpretation of the evidence was insufficient. Capital Enhancement Supported by Business Necessity The Court accepted that the company required funds to reconstruct its factory following an MIDC demolition notice dated 28 August 2006. The reconstruction resulted in a new facility measuring 1,011.66 square metres. Supporting material included occupation and completion certificates, company ledgers, bills and documents evidencing investment and increased fixed assets. The Court found that the capital enhancement was a gradual response to genuine business exigencies, culminating in the extraordinary general meeting held on 20 March 2009. It upheld the finding that the exercise served the company’s commercial survival and long-term interests. The appellant had not demonstrated that this conclusion was perverse or unsupported by evidence. Dilution Alone Did Not Establish Oppression The Court recognised the fiduciary obligation of directors to exercise share-allotment powers in the company’s interests. However, a bona fide exercise of those powers does not become oppressive merely because it changes the shareholders’ relative percentages. In this case, the Court accepted the commercial justification for raising capital. It also noted that the appellant’s original number of shares had not been reduced or cancelled. His percentage changed following further allotments. The Court additionally considered his conduct in demanding repayment of his investment when the company required funds. An amount of ₹2.5 lakh had been returned to him. These circumstances weighed against his challenge to the financial necessity for the capital increase. Notice and Directorship Claims Rejected The appellant challenged the absence of notice and relied upon provisions of the Articles of Association concerning shareholder rights. The Court rejected these submissions in the context of the disputed corporate records and the appellant’s asserted membership and directorship. It did not accept his claim that the allotments formed an unlawful scheme to dilute his interest. Regarding other directors’ appointments, the appellant failed to establish that his consent was required. The Court also considered the delay in challenging those appointments. It reiterated that a directorship is a personal fiduciary office and cannot be bequeathed by Will. The challenge to the appointments therefore failed. Siphoning Allegations and Suppression of Facts The Court declined to reopen the factual dispute concerning alleged siphoning of funds through a leave and licence arrangement. It found no established departure from probity and fair dealing sufficient to sustain the oppression claim. The Court also upheld the finding of suppression of material facts concerning parallel proceedings before the civil court at Thane. A passing reference to litigation did not amount to adequate disclosure of its material prayers and proceedings. Subsequent disclosure or withdrawal of the suit did not retrospectively cure the initial omission. The duty of full disclosure extended to the adjudicating authority. Final Decision Finding no perversity or legal error warranting interference, the High Court dismissed the appeal, disposed of the pending interim applications and made no order as to costs. The ruling illustrates that a dilution challenge requires examination of the purpose of the allotment, supporting evidence, corporate procedure and parties’ conduct. On the facts presented, the appellant failed to establish oppression or a basis for appellate interference. Cases Discussed Nanalal Zaver v. Bombay Life Assurance Co.

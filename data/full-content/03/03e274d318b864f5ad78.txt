Vs ITO (ITAT Rajkot) Interest-Free Advances to Sister Concerns Do Not Warrant Disallowance U/S 36(1)(iii) Where Commercial Expediency Is Established: ITAT Rajkot Rajkot ITAT deletes ₹16.18 lakh interest disallowance In Akshar Ginning & Pressing Ind. v. ITO, Ward-4, Veraval, ITA No. 464/RJT/2026, decided on 20 November 2026 for AY 2016–17, the Rajkot ITAT held that the coexistence of interest-bearing borrowings and interest-free advances to associate concerns was insufficient to sustain a disallowance under Section 36(1)(iii). The firm’s books and supporting documents established that the advances served its own business or were made out of commercial expediency. The Assessing Officer had failed to establish any diversion of borrowed funds for non-business purposes. The Tribunal accordingly deleted the entire disallowance of ₹16,17,789. How the disallowance arose in the fresh assessment The firm filed its return declaring total income of ₹22,775. The original scrutiny assessment, completed on 16 December 2018, determined income at ₹1,61,242. The Principal Commissioner subsequently held that the assessment was erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue and set it aside under Section 263 by order dated 30 March 2021, directing the Assessing Officer to make a fresh assessment. During the fresh proceedings, the Assessing Officer noted that the firm had debited ₹1,01,18,780 in interest expenditure, comprising deposit interest of ₹18,12,102 and bank interest of ₹83,06,678. Its accounts reflected secured loans of ₹6,85,08,743 and unsecured loans of ₹1,95,97,057. The officer also found that the firm had advanced money to sister concerns without charging interest. The Assessing Officer considered the firm’s explanation inadequate to establish that the advances came from its own funds. He inferred that a portion of the borrowings had funded the advances and, after working out the proportionate amount at ₹1,33,98,242 and applying a 12% interest rate, made a disallowance of ₹16,17,789 in the fresh assessment dated 22 March 2022. The CIT(A)/NFAC confirmed the disallowance on 2 March 2026. The transaction records supported the business explanation Before the Tribunal, the firm explained that some advances were made to farmers or towards business expenses, while the remaining advances supported the functioning of family concerns within the group. It submitted that the associate concerns had their own banking facilities and that the transfers were temporary, with funds rotating during the year. To substantiate this explanation, it produced day-to-day transaction statements showing the movement of funds between the concerns. The Tribunal examined the books and supporting documents and accepted that the advances were made either for the firm’s own business purposes or to facilitate the activities of associate concerns as a matter of commercial expediency. The temporary nature of the advances and the pattern of transactions supported that conclusion. The Revenue relied on the Assessing Officer’s findings, which did not establish a link between the borrowings and any non-business use. Commercial expediency is the governing test Section 36(1)(iii) allows deduction of interest on capital borrowed for the purposes of business or profession. The Tribunal explained that the expression “for the purposes of business” is wider than the immediate earning of profits. An advance can serve a business purpose even when the assessee derives no immediate direct financial benefit. The Bench relied on S.A. Builders Ltd. Vs CIT(A) [2007] 288 ITR 1 (SC). The Supreme Court held that where borrowed funds are advanced interest-free to a sister concern, the relevant inquiry is why the advance was made and how the recipient used it. An advance can qualify on grounds of commercial expediency even in the absence of a legal obligation to provide the funds. The Bench also cited Hero Cycles (P.) Ltd. Vs CIT [2015] 379 ITR 347 (SC) as reiterating that principle. The Tribunal deleted the disallowance in full The Assessing Officer was required to examine the actual source of the advances, the availability of interest-free funds, the nature of the transactions and their commercial purpose. The Tribunal found that the firm’s evidence established business purpose and that the officer had failed to demonstrate diversion of interest-bearing funds for non-business purposes. It deleted the ₹16,17,789 disallowance and allowed the firm’s appeal. Author’s comments The ruling makes clear why the source of an advance and its purpose deserve separate examination. The source inquiry traces the money. The purpose inquiry determines whether the advance served a commercial objective connected with the assessee’s business. Even where borrowed funds are traced to a sister concern, the test in S.A. Builders still requires examining the reason for the transfer and the recipient’s use of the money. In this case, the day-to-day transaction statements mattered because they showed how the funds moved during the year and supported the explanation that the advances were temporary and arose in the course of business dealings. The relationship between the concerns explained who received the funds. The books and supporting documents explained why the advances were made, which was decisive for the interest deduction. Cases Discussed: S.A. Builders Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Income-tax (Appeals), Chandigarh & Anr. (Supreme Court of India); [2007] 288 ITR 1 (SC). Interest on borrowed funds advanced to sister concerns is deductible where the advances are made as a measure of commercial expediency. The Revenue must examine the purpose of the advance and the manner in which the recipient utilised the funds rather than merely relying on the relationship between the parties. Hero Cycles (P.) Ltd. Vs CIT (Supreme Court of India); [2015] 379 ITR 347 (SC); AY: 1988-89. Reiterated the commercial expediency principle governing the allowability of interest expenditure on borrowed funds advanced to associated concerns. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT RAJKOT Captioned appeal filed by the Assessee, pertaining to assessment year (AY) 2016-17, is directed against the order under section 250 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Act’) passed by the National Faceless Appeal Centre (NAC) Delhi/Commissioner of Income-tax (Appeals) [in short ‘NFAC/Ld.CIT(A)’], dated 02.03.2026, which in turn arises out of an assessment order passed by the Assessing Officer u/s 143(3) r.w.s. 263 of the Act, dated 22.03.2022. 2. Grounds of appeal raised by the assessee, are as follows; 1. The Ld. CIT(A) erred on facts as also in law in confirming disallowance of interest of Rs.16,17,789/- estimated @12% on interest free advances. The disallowance made u/s. 36(1)(iii) of the Act may kindly be deleted. 3. Brief facts qua the issue are that assessee firm had filed its return of income, declaring total income of Rs.22,775/- for the A.Y.2016-17. The case of the assessee was selected for complete scrutiny under CASS and the assessment was completed on 16.12.2018, at an assessed income of Rs. 1,61,242/-. Later on, the Ld. Pr. Commissioner of Income Tax, Rajkot, vide order u/s 263 dated 30-03-2021, held that the assessment order passed by the assessing officer u/s 143(3) on 16-12-2018 was erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue. Accordingly, in exercise of powers u/s 263 of the Income Tax Act, 1961, the said assessment order was set aside with a direction to the assessing officer to make a fresh assessment. 4. Consequently, a notice u/s 142(1) of the Act, was issued to the assessee on 21.02.2022. Further, various statutory notices were issued to the assessee during the assessment proceedings. The assessee submitted its response on 24.02.2022. During the assessment proceeding, the assessing officer observed that the assessee -firm had debited interest expenditure of Rs.1,01,18,780/- (deposit interest of Rs.18,12,102/- and bank interest of Rs.83,06,678/-) to its Profit & Loss account under the head “Financial Expenses”. The assessee had reflected secured loans of Rs.6,85,08,743/- and unsecured loan of Rs.1,95,97,057/-, which was considered as major sources of funds on which interest was paid. However, the assessee had given advanced loan to its sister concerns without charging any interest. The assessee did not clearly demonstrate that these interest free advances were given out of its own funds. Therefore, the assessing officer calculated that a portion of the borrowed funds had been used for giving these interest free advances. The assessing officer worked out the proportionate amount at Rs. 1,33,98,242/- and applied an interest rate of 12%, resulting in disallowance of Rs. 16,17,789/- u/s 36(1)(iii) of the Act. 5. Aggrieved by the order of the Assessing Officer, the assessee carried the matter in appeal before the Ld. CIT(A), who has confirmed the action of the Assessing Officer, therefore, the assessee is in further appeal before this Tribunal. 6. Learned Counsel for the assessee submitted that part of the advances were for business purposes such as advances to farmers and for business expenses, and the remaining advances were given to family concerns for functioning of group business. It was further contended that the business concerns had their own banking facilities, and the advances were temporary in nature, that there was rotation of funds during the year, and there was no intention of tax avoidance. In support of its claim, the assessee submitted day to day transaction statements with associate concerns. Therefore, Ld. Counsel contended that disallowance of Rs.16,17,789/- u/s 36(1)(iii) of the Act, may be deleted. 7. On the other hand, Ld. DR for the revenue relied on the findings of the Assessing Officer. 8. I have heard, both the parties and carefully gone through the submission put forth on behalf of the assessee along with the documents furnished and the case laws relied upon, and perused the fact of the case including the findings of the ld CIT(A) and other materials brought on record. I find merit in the submissions of Ld. Counsel for the assessee. The assessee under consideration has established, with reference to the books of account and the supporting documentary evidence, that the advances were made either for the purposes of its own business or as a measure of commercial expediency in facilitating the business activities of associate concerns, and that the interest-bearing borrowed funds were not diverted for non-business purposes. I note that section 36(1)(iii) of the Act provides for deduction of interest paid in respect of capital borrowed for the purposes of the business or profession. The provision requires the following conditions to be satisfied, viz: (i)The assessee must have borrowed capital, (ii)Interest must have been paid or payable on such borrowed capital and (iii)The borrowing must have been for the purposes of the business or profession. The expression “for the purposes of business” is of wider amplitude than the expression “for the purpose of earning profits”. Consequently, expenditure incurred on grounds of commercial expediency may qualify for deduction even where the immediate benefit is not directly realised by the assessee itself. In the case of S.A. Builders Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income-tax (Appeals), Chandigarh & Anr. (2007) 288 ITR 1 (SC),the Hon’ble Supreme Court held that where borrowed funds are advanced to a sister concern without charging interest, the relevant test is whether the advance was made as a measure of commercial expediency. The Hon’ble Court observed that commercial expediency is an expression of wide import and includes expenditure which a prudent businessman incurs for the purpose of business. Such expenditure may be allowable even if it was not incurred under a legal obligation, provided it was incurred on grounds of commercial expediency. The Hon’ble Supreme Court further held that the Revenue authorities must examine the purpose for which the money was advanced and the manner in which the recipient concern utilised the funds. The fact that the borrowed amount was not utilised by the assessee in its own business but was advanced to a sister concern is not, by itself, sufficient to disallow the interest expenditure. Therefore, interest on borrowed funds advanced to a sister concern is allowable where the advance is made for commercial expediency and is connected with the business purpose of the assessee, even if, the assessee does not derive an immediate direct financial benefit. 9. I also note that the said principle has been reiterated by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Hero Cycles (P.) Ltd. v. CIT (2015) 379 ITR 347 (SC). In the present case, the assessee has furnished transaction statements and other documentary evidence to explain the nature and movement of funds between the assessee and the associate concerns. The Assessing Officer was, therefore, required to examine the actual source of the advances, the availability of interest-free funds, the nature of the transactions and the commercial purpose for which the funds were advanced. The mere fact that the assessee had borrowed funds and simultaneously advanced amounts to associate concerns, without establishing a nexus between the borrowed funds and the advances made for non-business purposes, would not be sufficient to sustain the disallowance. Further, where the advances are temporary in nature, are made in the course of regular business dealings and are supported by the day-to-day transaction statements demonstrating the rotation of funds, in these circumstances, addition should not be made in the hands of the assessee. Accordingly, the documentary evidence furnished by the assessee establishes that the advances were made for business purposes or as a measure of commercial expediency, and the Assessing Officer has failed to establish diversion of interest-bearing borrowed funds for non-business purposes, the disallowance of interest of Rs.16,17,789/- made under Section 36(1)(iii) is liable to be deleted. Accordingly, I delete the addition. 10. In the result, appeal filed by the assessee, is allowed. Order is pronounced in the open Court Today on 20/11/2026.

Rai, dismissed the Revenue’s Special Leave Petition challenging the Bombay High Court’s decision quashing reassessment proceedings for Assessment Year 2015-16. The Supreme Court condoned the delay and held that the impugned High Court order did not warrant interference. However, it expressly clarified that the question of law was kept open. Consequently, the Bombay High Court’s decision remained undisturbed in the particular case, but the Supreme Court did not independently adjudicate the legal questions concerning approval under Section 151 or issuance of a draft assessment order under Section 144B of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The underlying dispute involved a reassessment notice dated 31 March 2021 and a final assessment order dated 25 March 2022, both of which had been challenged by the assessee before the Bombay High Court. The assessee contended before the Bombay High Court that the reassessment notice issued under Section 148 was without jurisdiction because the reopening was initiated beyond four years from the end of AY 2015-16 without obtaining sanction from the competent authority prescribed under Section 151. The petitioner submitted that approval for reopening had been obtained from the Additional Commissioner, Range 3(2), Mumbai, whereas the applicable statutory provisions required sanction from the Principal Chief Commissioner, Chief Commissioner, Principal Commissioner or Commissioner. The assessee also challenged the final assessment order dated 25 March 2022 on the independent ground that the Assessing Officer had failed to issue a draft assessment order under Section 144B(1)(xvi) before finalising the assessment. It was argued that issuance of the draft assessment order was a mandatory requirement of the faceless assessment procedure and that its omission rendered the final assessment order legally unsustainable. During the hearing, the Revenue’s counsel referred to the Department’s affidavit-in-reply dated 24 June 2022 and acknowledged that approval for reopening had been obtained from the Additional Commissioner of Income Tax. The Revenue also did not dispute that no draft assessment order had been issued to the petitioner. Considering these admitted facts, the Bombay High Court held that the reassessment notice dated 31 March 2021 was clearly without jurisdiction and accordingly quashed and set it aside. Although the invalidity of the reassessment notice was sufficient to dispose of the petition, the High Court separately examined the procedural objection concerning the final assessment order. It recorded that there had admittedly been a breach of Section 144B(1)(xvi) because the mandatory draft assessment order had not been issued. On this additional ground, the Court also quashed the final assessment order dated 25 March 2022. The writ petition was allowed without any order as to costs. Aggrieved by the Bombay High Court’s judgment, the Revenue approached the Supreme Court through a Special Leave Petition. The Supreme Court condoned the delay but declined to interfere with the High Court’s order and dismissed the petition, expressly keeping the question of law open. The combined outcome highlights two important statutory requirements governing the reassessment proceedings involved in the case: obtaining sanction from the authority prescribed under the applicable provisions of Section 151 and complying with the draft assessment order procedure under Section 144B(1)(xvi). The Bombay High Court treated the defective approval as a jurisdictional infirmity and the absence of a draft assessment order as an additional ground for invalidating the final assessment. However, because the Supreme Court expressly reserved the question of law, its dismissal of the Special Leave Petition should not be interpreted as an independent or conclusive pronouncement on these statutory issues. The decision must also be understood in the context of the provisions applicable to the relevant assessment proceedings.

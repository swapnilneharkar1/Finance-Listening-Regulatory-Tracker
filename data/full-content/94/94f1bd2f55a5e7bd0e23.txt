Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Deepak Kumar Thyari Rajagopal Vs Shyam EPC Private Limited (NCLT Amaravati) Summary : The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), Amaravati Bench, admitted an application filed under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) by Deepak Kumar Thyari Rajagopal, Sole Proprietor of Garuda Industries, against Shyam EPC Private Limited, following the corporate debtor’s failure to repay financial assistance advanced for its business operations. The financial creditor claimed an outstanding amount of Rs. 1,13,95,808, comprising principal and accrued interest. The principal question before the Tribunal was whether the transaction constituted a financial debt, whether default had occurred and whether the statutory minimum default threshold of Rs. 1 crore prescribed under Section 4 of the IBC had been satisfied. The corporate debtor disputed the nature of the transaction and the computation of outstanding dues, contending that the financial assistance formed part of a broader commercial arrangement that the financial creditor had failed to fulfil. After examining the loan agreement, banking records, correspondence and record of default, the Tribunal concluded that the essential requirements for admission of a Section 7 application had been established. The dispute originated when the corporate debtor approached the financial creditor on 1 September 2025 seeking funds for purchasing coal and coke required for its engineering, procurement and construction projects. The financial creditor transferred Rs. 1,05,00,000 through RTGS on the same date. Subsequently, the parties executed a Loan Agreement dated 27 September 2025, providing for a loan facility of up to Rs. 5 crore, subject to contractual conditions governing disbursement and repayment. The agreement stipulated interest at 1.5% per month, equivalent to 18% per annum, and required repayment of the amount advanced, together with interest, within 60 days of disbursement. Importantly, the agreement provided that further amounts would not be disbursed until the amounts already availed had been repaid. It also contained provisions treating non-payment of principal or interest on the due dates as an event of default. As security, the agreement contemplated the pledge of 29,55,400 equity shares of the corporate debtor, representing its entire issued and paid-up equity share capital. These contractual provisions became central to determining the nature of the transaction and the obligations arising from the actual disbursement. According to the financial creditor, the repayment period expired on 31 October 2025, but the corporate debtor failed to discharge its liability. Consequently, the financial creditor issued a demand letter dated 1 November 2025, seeking repayment of Rs. 1,08,15,863, including interest. The corporate debtor responded through its letter dated 21 November 2025, acknowledging the underlying business transaction and expressing its intention to comply with the terms of the agreement. Nevertheless, repayment was not made. The financial creditor thereafter issued a legal demand notice dated 23 December 2025, demanding Rs. 1,10,85,123, comprising the principal amount of Rs. 1,05,00,000 and accrued interest of Rs. 5,85,123 calculated up to 22 December 2025. As the default continued, the financial creditor calculated the outstanding amount at Rs. 1,13,95,808 as on 20 February 2026 and instituted insolvency proceedings. The application was supported by the loan agreement, bank statement evidencing disbursement, ledger account, demand correspondence and records maintained with National E-Governance Services Limited (NeSL). The corporate debtor opposed admission primarily on the ground that the transaction was not an independent lending arrangement but part of a larger commercial understanding concerning the procurement and sale of coal and coke. It asserted that the financial creditor had agreed to finance purchases and subsequently buy back the materials for sale in the market at a premium, with the corporate debtor receiving 15% of the purchase amount, subject to adjustment of interest. According to the corporate debtor, although the agreement contemplated financial assistance of Rs. 5 crore, only Rs. 1.05 crore had been disbursed, and the financial creditor had failed to provide further amounts despite assurances. It was argued that the failure to fulfil these reciprocal commercial obligations prevented the contemplated business arrangement from materialising and that no enforceable default could therefore be attributed to the corporate debtor. The corporate debtor also maintained that its letter dated 21 November 2025 could not be treated as an unconditional admission of liability without considering the wider commercial circumstances. However, during the hearing on 14 October 2026, the corporate debtor admitted that it possessed no documentary evidence supporting its asserted commercial arrangement. A further objection concerned the difference between the amounts appearing in the financial creditor’s application and the NeSL record. While Part IV of Form 1 disclosed an outstanding amount of Rs. 1,13,95,808, the NeSL Form D recorded a default amount of Rs. 1,12,56,000, with the date of default stated as 31 October 2025. The corporate debtor contended that this discrepancy demonstrated the absence of a complete and reconciled documentary record and rendered the petition unsuitable for admission. The Tribunal rejected the objection by distinguishing the statutory determination required under Section 7 of the IBC from a detailed adjudication of the precise amount recoverable. It explained that the relevant inquiry was whether a financial debt existed, whether repayment had been defaulted upon when due and whether the default satisfied the minimum threshold prescribed under Section 4. The Tribunal relied upon Milind Kashiram Jadhav Vs State Bank of India & Ors., Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1589 of 2023, decided on 25 April 2024, which referred to Suzlon Synthetics Ltd. Vs Stressed Asset Stabilization Fund, (2022) 145 taxmann.com 594 (NCLAT-New Delhi), for the proposition that admission proceedings do not require determination of the exact amount of debt in default. The Tribunal further relied upon the Supreme Court’s judgment in Deepak Raheja & Anr. Vs Omkara Assets Reconstruction Private Limited & Anr., Civil Appeal No. 12195 of 2025, decided on 15 October 2026. Referring to the principles governing admission under Section 7, the Tribunal observed that the adjudicating authority must ascertain the existence of financial debt and the occurrence of default rather than undertake the functions of a civil court determining the exact outstanding liability. Applying these principles, the Bench examined the loan agreement dated 27 September 2025, the bank statement establishing transfer of Rs. 1.05 crore, the contractual obligation to repay within 60 days, the subsequent demand letters and the corporate debtor’s response acknowledging the transaction. It also considered the NeSL Form D record, which carried the status “Deemed to be Authenticated” and recorded the date of default as 31 October 2025. The Tribunal found that these materials established the existence of financial debt and default. Since even the amount reflected in the NeSL record exceeded Rs. 1 crore, the discrepancy in the precise outstanding figure did not affect satisfaction of the statutory threshold. The Bench separately examined whether the application was within limitation and whether the procedural requirements for admission had been complied with. It noted that the stated date of default was 31 October 2025, whereas the petition had been e-filed on 26 March 2026 and physically filed on 27 August 2026 after rectification of Registry objections. Accordingly, the petition was held to be well within the prescribed three-year limitation period. The Tribunal also considered the completeness of the application under Rule 4 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2016. Earlier procedural deficiencies, including issues relating to supporting documents and service upon the corporate debtor, had been addressed during the proceedings. The financial creditor additionally clarified that it was not a related party of the corporate debtor within the meaning of Section 5(24) of the IBC. The proposed Interim Resolution Professional, Mr. Srinivas Gudla Rao, had submitted his written consent and declared that no disciplinary proceedings were pending against him. Having examined these matters, the Tribunal concluded that the application was complete and that the statutory conditions for admission were fulfilled. Consequently, by its order dated 30 October 2026, the NCLT Amaravati Bench admitted the Section 7 petition against Shyam EPC Private Limited and commenced the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP). The Tribunal declared a moratorium under Section 14 of the IBC, restricting the institution or continuation of proceedings against the corporate debtor, disposal or encumbrance of its assets, enforcement of security interests and recovery of property in its possession, subject to the statutory provisions and exceptions. It appointed Mr. Srinivas Gudla Rao as Interim Resolution Professional and directed him to perform the statutory functions associated with the commencement of CIRP. In accordance with Sections 17 and 18 of the IBC, the powers of the board of directors stood suspended, with management and control of the corporate debtor’s affairs vesting in the IRP. The decision establishes that where actual disbursement, a contractual repayment obligation and default exceeding the statutory threshold are demonstrated, an unsupported allegation of a wider commercial understanding or a discrepancy in the exact calculation of dues does not, by itself, prevent admission of a Section 7 insolvency application.

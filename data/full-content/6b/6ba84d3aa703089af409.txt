Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Advaith Motors Private Limited Vs Commissioner of Central GST (GSTAT Bengaluru) Summary: The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal, Bengaluru Bench, allowed Advaith Motors Private Limited’s appeal against differential Compensation Cess of ₹9,76,21,566, interest and a penalty of ₹97,62,157 on sales of Hyundai Creta 1.6L, Tucson 2.0L and Alcazar 2.0L. The dispute concerned supplies from 11 September 2017 to 31 July 2023 and whether ground clearance under Entry 52B of Notification No. 01/2017-Compensation Cess (Rate) had to be measured in laden or unladen condition. The dealer had charged cess at 20% under Entry 52A, following the manufacturer’s invoices, while the Department sought 22% under Entry 52B. The first three requirements of the higher-rate entry—SUV identity, engine capacity exceeding 1,500 cc and length exceeding 4,000 mm—were admitted; ground clearance of at least 170 mm was the contested requirement. The Tribunal held that before the July 2023 amendment, the notification neither defined ground clearance nor prescribed an unladen measurement. It treated the statutory vehicle-testing framework, including Rule 126 of the Central Motor Vehicles Rules, AIS-007 and Clause 5.8 of IS 9435:2004, as the appropriate guide. That standard required measurement on a fully laden vehicle. The Tribunal rejected the distinction drawn by the authorities between “minimum ground clearance” and “ground clearance”, explaining that “minimum” identified the lowest measurement point rather than a different vehicle dimension. It also held that Entry 52B was a charging entry, so any unresolved ambiguity concerning the higher rate would favour the taxpayer. The Tribunal found that Notification No. 03/2023-Compensation Cess (Rate) substituted the entire entry, widened its vehicle coverage and expressly introduced unladen ground clearance, thereby enlarging the burden rather than merely explaining the earlier law. It relied on the principles governing prospective fiscal amendments and on Circular No. 247/04/2025-GST dated 14 February 2025, which bound the Revenue and stated that the amendment applied on or after 26 July 2023. Although the notification itself specified commencement on 27 July 2023, the Tribunal expressly adopted 26 July 2023 consistently with the circular and the Bombay and Kerala High Court decisions discussed in the order. The Bombay ruling was adopted as persuasive authority, without deciding the wider controversy about the binding effect of a non-jurisdictional High Court judgment. The Tata Motors advance ruling was considered for its reasoning, recognising its limited statutory binding effect. On evidence, the ARAI certificates recorded laden ground clearance of 165 mm for the Creta, 135 mm for the Tucson and 154 mm for the Alcazar, each below 170 mm. The Department relied instead on Hyundai South Africa’s website and commercial automobile portals without establishing that the foreign-market vehicles matched the Indian variants or identifying the load condition and measurement method. The Tribunal held that admissibility of computer printouts under Section 145 did not establish their reliability. The Revenue bore the burden of proving the higher-rate classification and could have used its powers under Section 70 to obtain manufacturer specifications or ARAI test reports. Unverified website figures could not prevail over statutory certificates for the vehicles actually sold. The Tribunal nevertheless rejected the dealer’s separate contention that it could not be required to pay more cess than the manufacturer had charged. Under Section 59, each registered supplier must independently self-assess its outward supplies; the manufacturer’s rate and the traders’ FAQ did not displace that duty. This finding did not affect the relief because the higher-rate classification failed on the applicable measurement and evidence. For the days after the adopted cut-off, the Department neither separately quantified nor proved any short payment, while its own material recognised payment at 22% after the amendment. The Tribunal also noted unreconciled computation differences but did not base the result on them. It set aside the entire demand, consequential interest and penalty, together with the adjudication and first appellate orders, without costs. The challenges concerning a consolidated multi-year notice and the constitutional nature of Compensation Cess were not pressed and were not adjudicated. The question involved in this appeal is whether, for supplies made before 26.07.2023, the date of Notification No. 03/2023-Compensation Cess (Rate), the expression “ground clearance” used in Entry 52B of Notification No. 01/2017-Compensation Cess (Rate) dated 28.06.2017 was to be measured with the vehicle laden or unladen. The answer decides whether the Appellant owed Compensation Cess at 22% or at 20% on three Hyundai models that it sold between 11.09.2017 and 31.07.2023. 2. The Appellant, M/s. Advaith Motors Private Limited, is an authorised dealer of Hyundai Motor India Limited (“HMIL”) in Bengaluru. It challenges Order-in-Appeal No. 06/2024-25 COMMR-APPEALS-1 dated 25.04.2024 of the Commissioner of Central Tax (Appeals-I), Bengaluru (“the First Appellate Authority”). That order upheld Order-in-Original No. 10/2023-24 (GST-ADC) dated 19.12.2023 of the Additional Commissioner of Central Tax, Bengaluru South Commissionerate (“the Adjudicating Authority”). The Adjudicating Authority confirmed differential Compensation Cess of Rs. 9,76,21,566/-, interest under Section 50 and a penalty of Rs. 97,62,157/- under Section 73(9) of the CGST Act. 3. The appeal lies under Section 112 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (“the CGST Act”). That Act applies to the Compensation Cess by virtue of Section 11(1) of the Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act, 2017, which is extracted below. A reference to a provision of the CGST Act, or of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017 (“the CGST Rules”), is a reference to that provision as so applied. 4. In this order, “the Cess Act” means the Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act, 2017; and “the Rate Notification” means Notification No. 01/2017-Compensation Cess (Rate) dated 28.06.2017. Any section cited in this order without explicitly naming the underlying statute refers to a section of the CGST Act, as made applicable to Cess by virtue of Section 11(1) of the Cess Act. Section 11(1) of the Cess Act reads as follows: “11. (1) The provisions of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, and the rules made thereunder, including those relating to assessment, input tax credit, non-levy, short-levy, interest, appeals, offences and penalties, shall, as far as may be, mutatis mutandis, apply, in relation to the levy and collection of the cess leviable under section 8 on the intra-State supply of goods and services, as they apply in relation to the levy and collection of central tax on such intra-State supplies under the said Act or the rules made thereunder.” Facts 5. The Appellant bought Hyundai Creta 1.6L (petrol and diesel), Tucson 2.0L (petrol and diesel) and Alcazar 2.0L (petrol) from HMIL. HMIL charged cess at 20% under Entry 52A of the Rate Notification. The Appellant sold the vehicles to retail buyers and charged cess at the same rate. 6. The Adjudicating Authority held as follows. The Explanation of July 2023 only clarified the existing entry. In its words, “Ground clearance for a vehicle varies in a range — minimum in Laden condition and maximum in unladen condition. Explanation only sought to clarify which of these is to be taken for taxation.” The ARAI figure is a “minimum ground clearance” measured at full payload for road safety, and cannot be imported into a taxing entry. Entries 47 and 48 of the Rate Notification refer to the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 while Entry 52B does not. The Appellant, as supplier, is answerable for the rate on its own supplies. The Tata Motors advance ruling binds only the applicant under Section 103. The Appellant had not shown that unladen ground clearance was below 170 mm, and had begun to pay 22% after the amendment. The Adjudicating Authority confirmed cess of Rs. 9,76,21,566/-, interest under Section 50(1) and a penalty of Rs. 97,62,157/- under Section 73(9). It declined to impose a further penalty under Section 125. Findings of the First Appellate Authority 15. The Appellant appealed on 22.02.2024. Section 112(1) reads: “(1) Any person aggrieved by an order passed against him under section 107 or section 108 of this Act or the State Goods and Services Tax Act or the Union Territory Goods and Services Tax Act may appeal to the Appellate Tribunal against such order within three months from the date on which the order sought to be appealed against is communicated to the person preferring the appeal, or the date, as may be notified by the Government, on the recommendations of the Council, for filing appeal before the Appellate Tribunal under this Act, whichever is later.” By Notification S.O. 4220(E) dated 17.09.2025, issued under this sub-section, an appeal against an order communicated before 01.04.2026 may be filed up to 30.06.2026. The Court held that an Income-tax Tribunal anywhere in the country must respect the law laid down by a High Court, though of a different State, so long as there is no contrary decision of any other High Court. It stressed that the Income-tax Act is an all-India statute. It drew upon East India Commercial Co. Ltd. v. Collector of Customs, AIR 1962 SC 1893, where the Hon’ble Supreme Court held that a tribunal cannot ignore the law declared by the High Court which exercises superintendence over it, and upon K.S. Venkataraman & Co. (P) Ltd. v. State of Madras, AIR 1966 SC 1089; (ii). Khanbhai Esoofbhai v. Collector of Central Excise, 1999 (107) E.L.T. 557, decided by a Larger Bench of the Customs, Excise and Gold (Control) Appellate Tribunal. Amritlal Lalubhai v. Collector of Central Excise, 1985 (21) E.L.T. 908, decided by a Special Bench of the same Tribunal. Whether the demand, interest and penalty can be sustained. Discussion and findings Grounds not pressed 69. Two grounds raised in the memorandum of appeal were not pressed at the hearing. The first is that Compensation Cess is a fee, on which the GST Council could not make recommendations under Article 279A. That ground is, in substance, a challenge to the validity of Section 8(2) of the Cess Act and of the levy itself. It was held by a Constitution Bench of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India, (1997) 3 SCC 261, that a tribunal which is a creature of an Act cannot declare that very Act to be unconstitutional. The second ground is that a single notice covering several financial years vitiates the proceedings. As neither ground was pressed, we do not examine them. The Appellant is free to raise the first before the appropriate forum. Issue (i): Laden or unladen ground clearance before 26.07.2023 70. Before the amendment, Entry 52B applied only when four conditions were met together. The Board said so in paragraph 6.2 of Circular No. 189/01/2023-GST dated 13.01.2023: “6.2 In this regard, it is clarified that Compensation Cess at the rate of 22% is applicable on Motor vehicles, falling under heading 8703, which satisfy all four specifications, namely: – these are popularly known as SUVs; the engine capacity exceeds 1,500 cc; the length exceeds 4,000 mm; and the ground clearance is 170 mm and above.” A vehicle that failed any one condition fell in Entry 52A and bore cess at 20%. The first three conditions are admitted. The case turns on the fourth. 71. Until 26.07.2023 neither the Cess Act nor the Rate Notification defined “ground clearance” or said how it was to be measured. The Adjudicating Authority itself found that a vehicle’s ground clearance “varies in a range — minimum in Laden condition and maximum in unladen condition”. A vehicle therefore has no single ground clearance until the law says how it is to be measured for determining the applicability of the cess. The entry, read alone, is silent. The answer must come from the law that governs how motor vehicles are measured in India. 72. It was held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Akbar Badruddin Jiwani v. Collector of Customs, (1990) 2 SCC 203, that a word left undefined in a fiscal entry is read in the sense in which those who deal in the goods understand it. No evidence of trade usage was led by either side. But “ground clearance” is a technical term of automobile engineering. Those who make, test and register motor vehicles in India understand it in the sense fixed by the standard under which vehicles are type-approved. Here the trade meaning and the technical meaning are the same. Where a fiscal entry uses a technical term without defining it, and another law prescribes how that very attribute of the goods is to be measured, the meaning given by that law is the safest guide. Motor vehicles are type-approved under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 and the CMV Rules. Rule 126 of the CMV Rules requires every manufacturer to submit a prototype to a notified testing agency, such as ARAI, for a certificate that the vehicle complies with the Act and the Rules. ARAI measures dimensions under AIS-007, which adopts IS 9435. Clause 5.8 of IS 9435:2004 reads: “5.8 Minimum Ground Clearance – The distance between the ground and the lowest point of the centre part of the vehicle. The centre part is that part contained between two planes parallel to and equidistant from the longitudinal median plane (of the vehicle) and separated by a distance which is 80 percent of the least distance between points on the inner edges of the wheels on any one axle. NOTE — This measurement has to be done on fully laden vehicle to the maximum authorized GVW.” 73. No Indian standard brought to our notice measures ground clearance in unladen condition. The Revenue did not point to one. Its own notice records that the ARAI figure “is in fact the ground clearance in laden condition”. During the disputed period the laden measure was the only measure of ground clearance prescribed under the law governing motor vehicles. That is the measure Entry 52B must be taken to have used. 74. The authorities below drew a line between “minimum ground clearance” and “ground clearance”. Clause 5.8 does not support that line. The word “minimum” in Clause 5.8 points to where the distance is taken: from the ground to the lowest point of the centre part of the vehicle. The load is dealt with separately, by the Note. What Clause 5.8 measures is what anyone means by the ground clearance of a vehicle. The word “minimum” does not turn it into some other dimension. 75. The Revenue says a vehicle is sold unladen, so the unladen figure governs. We do not accept this. The entry describes the vehicle, not the state in which it leaves the showroom. A dimension used to classify goods is measured in the way the law prescribes for that dimension. The manner of delivery is not part of the test that the entry lays down. 76. The argument based on Entries 47 and 48 of the Rate Notification does not help the Revenue either. Those entries say that “the specification of the motor vehicle shall be determined as per the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (59 of 1988) and the rules made there under.” That shows the Central Government treated motor-vehicle law as the source of vehicle specifications. The absence of such a sentence in Entry 52B leaves the term undefined. It does not supply an unladen meaning that the words of the entry do not carry. When the Central Government wished to adopt the unladen measure, it said so in plain words in July 2023. 77. The Revenue rightly says that the meaning of the old entry and the reach of the amendment are separate questions. We have answered the first question on its own terms. Even if the old entry were open to two readings, the result would be the same. Entry 52B imposes a higher rate. It is a charging entry, not an exemption. It was held by a Constitution Bench of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Commissioner of Customs (Import), Mumbai v. Dilip Kumar and Company, (2018) 9 SCC 1, that an ambiguity in a charging provision goes to the benefit of the subject, while an exemption notification is construed strictly and an ambiguity in it goes to the Revenue. A doubt about which ground clearance Entry 52B meant must be resolved in favour of the Appellant. 78. The amendment of July 2023 points the same way. Clause (xlv) of Notification No. 03/2023 did more than add an Explanation. It substituted the whole entry. It widened the class from vehicles “popularly known as” SUVs to all utility vehicles “by whatever name called”, including MUVs, MPVs and XUVs, and it fixed the unladen measure. By changing the measure, the substituted entry brought into Entry 52B vehicles that were outside it, and so enlarged the burden of cess. That is a change in the law, not a clarification of it. 79. It was held by a Constitution Bench of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Commissioner of Income Tax v. Vatika Township (P) Ltd., (2015) 1 SCC 1, that a provision which imposes a new burden, or enlarges an existing one, is presumed to be prospective unless it provides otherwise, expressly or by necessary implication. It was held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Union of India v. Martin Lottery Agencies Ltd., (2009) 12 SCC 209, that an Explanation which changes the law does not operate retrospectively merely because it is called an Explanation. Paragraph 2 of Notification No. 03/2023 provides that it shall come into force on 27.07.2023. It contains nothing, express or implied, to make it apply to supplies made before that date. The substituted entry, with its Explanation, therefore operates only prospectively. The date from which it applies, 26.07.2023, is explained below. 80. The authorities below relied on Sree Sankaracharya University of Sanskrit v. Dr. Manu, 2023 SCC OnLine SC 640, and S. Sundaram Pillai v. V.R. Pattabiraman, (1985) 1 SCC 591. It was held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in those decisions that a provision which only clarifies or declares the existing law may apply to the past, and that the label given to a provision does not decide its nature. The test is whether the provision explains the law as it stood or changes it. Applied here, that test shows that the Explanation changed the measure, and with it the rate, for vehicles such as these. The principles laid down in those decisions therefore lead to a prospective reading. 81. The question of retrospective operation is also closed for the Revenue by the Board’s own circular. Section 168(1) reads: “168. (1) The Board may, if it considers it necessary or expedient so to do for the purpose of uniformity in the implementation of this Act, issue such orders, instructions or directions to the central tax officers as it may deem fit, and thereupon all such officers and all other persons employed in the implementation of this Act shall observe and follow such orders, instructions or directions.” 82. On the recommendation of the GST Council at its 55th meeting, the Board issued Circular No. 247/04/2025-GST dated 14.02.2025 under Section 168(1). Paragraphs 5.1 to 5.3, numbered as they appear in the circular, read: “5.1 Representations were received that there are different views in some jurisdictions regarding the effective date of amended entry 52B in notification No. 1/2017-Compensation Cess (Rate) dated 28.06.2017. 5.2 Prior to the 50th GST Council meeting, vide entry at S.No. 52B in the notification No. 01/2017-Compensation Cess (Rate) dated 28.06.2017, motor vehicles of engine capacity exceeding 1500 cc, popularly known as SUVs, including utility vehicles attracted 22% Compensation Cess. 5.3 Following the 50th GST Council meeting, vide notification No. 03/2023-Compensation Cess (Rate) dated 26.07.2023, the entry 52B was substituted to provide that the cess will be applicable to all motor vehicles known as utility vehicles by whatever name called, with engine capacity exceeding 1500cc, length exceeding 4000mm and ground clearance of 170mm and above. Further, a new explanation was added that ground clearance means ground clearance in unladen condition. 5.3 As per the recommendation of the GST Council, it is hereby clarified that the amendment carried out vide notification No. 03/2023-Compensation Cess (Rate) dated 26.07.2023 will apply on or after 26.7.2023.” 83. It was held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Ranadey Micronutrients v. Collector of Central Excise, (1996) 10 SCC 387, and in Commissioner of Central Excise v. Dhiren Chemical Industries, (2002) 10 SCC 64, that circulars issued by the Board bind the Department, which cannot take a stand contrary to them. It was held by a Constitution Bench of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Commissioner of Central Excise v. Ratan Melting & Wire Industries, (2008) 13 SCC 1, that circulars of the Board bind the authorities under the statute, though not the courts. The circular was issued after both the orders under appeal, so the authorities below did not have it. It binds the Revenue before us. The Revenue does not question the circular. It concedes that, after the circular, its case for the period before 26.07.2023 cannot rest solely on the retrospective operation of Notification No. 03/2023. The circular states that the amendment applies on or after 26.07.2023, the date of the notification, although paragraph 2 of the notification brings it into force on 27.07.2023. 84. The Hon’ble High Court of Bombay decided the same question in Mercedes-Benz India Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India, Writ Petition No. 5208 of 2025, decided on 29.04.2025, 2025 (5) TMI 552. It was held by the Hon’ble High Court that: “Now the Board itself [albeit based on the GST Council recommendation and clarification] has clarified that the amendment carried out vide Notification No. 3/2023-CC (Rate) dated 26th July, 2023 is prospective in nature. This clarification issued by the Board is binding on the Respondents. The disputed period in the present case is from September 2017 to July 2022. Hence, for the aforesaid period, the ground clearance of motor vehicles for the purpose of determining applicable Cess rate under the Cess Notification had to be considered in a laden condition, as was being done by the Petitioner.” 85. The Hon’ble High Court of Kerala, in Popular Motor World Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India, W.P.(C) No. 35881 of 2024, decided on 04.07.2025, 2025 SCC OnLine Ker 4864, set aside a show cause notice issued to a motor-vehicle dealer on the same issue. It did so because the amendment applies only on or after 26.07.2023, and it left the Department free to proceed afresh in accordance with law. 86. Both High Courts have thus taken 26.07.2023, the date of issue of the notification, as the date from which the substituted entry applies. The Board’s circular says the same. We therefore take 26.07.2023 as that date. 87. The Revenue says that the Bombay judgment is not from the jurisdictional High Court. That is correct. The Appellant relied on Commissioner of Income Tax v. Smt. Godavari Devi Saraf, (1978) 113 ITR 589 (Bom.), and on the decisions of the Customs, Excise and Gold (Control) Appellate Tribunal that followed it, to contend that the judgment binds this Tribunal. The same High Court later held in Commissioner of Income Tax v. Thana Electricity Supply Ltd., (1994) 206 ITR 727 (Bom.), that the decision of one High Court does not bind courts and tribunals outside its territorial jurisdiction, though it has persuasive value. We need not decide which view governs this Tribunal, and we express no opinion on it. It is enough that the judgment is the considered view of a High Court on the same entry and the same circular, and is entitled to respect for that reason. We have examined its reasoning for ourselves. It rests on the Board’s circular, which binds the Revenue, and it accords with the conclusion we have reached independently above. The Hon’ble High Court of Kerala has proceeded on the same footing. No contrary view of any High Court, including the Hon’ble High Court of Karnataka, has been brought to our notice. We find the reasoning of the Hon’ble High Court of Bombay sound, and we respectfully adopt it as persuasive authority. 88. The view also accords with the reasoning of the Maharashtra Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling in Tata Motors. We accept the Revenue’s point that, under Section 103(1), that ruling binds only the applicant and its jurisdictional officer. Section 103(1), as it then stood, reads: “(1) The advance ruling pronounced by the Authority or the Appellate Authority under this Chapter shall be binding only on— (a) the applicant who had sought it in respect of any matter referred to in sub-section (2) of section 97 for advance ruling; (b) the concerned officer or the jurisdictional officer in respect of the applicant.” We refer to the ruling only for its reasoning. 89. We hold that, for supplies made before 26.07.2023, ground clearance under Entry 52B was to be measured in laden condition, and that the substituted entry, with its Explanation, does not apply to those supplies. Issue (ii): ARAI certificates or website data 90. The ARAI certificates are issued after statutory testing of the very models that the Appellant sold in India. They record laden ground clearance of 165 mm for the Creta 1.6L, 135 mm for the Tucson 2.0L and 154 mm for the Alcazar 2.0L. Each figure is below 170 mm. The Revenue says that the Appellant had to prove the figure for each variant. The certificates cover the models in dispute. The Revenue placed nothing on record to show that any variant sold in India had a different laden ground clearance. 91. Against these, the Revenue relied on the website of Hyundai South Africa and on commercial portals. The South African figures relate to vehicles made for another market. For the Alcazar, the notice relied on the “Grand Creta”, a vehicle sold abroad under another name. Nothing on record shows that those vehicles share the specifications of the variants sold in India. The portals do not disclose the source of their figures, the load condition or the method of measurement. Such material is meant for buyers. It is not evidence of a measurement made according to law. 92. The Revenue says the Appellant relies on technical material that helps it and rejects material that does not. The comparison is not apt. The Appellant relies on statutory certificates for the Indian variants. It rejects unverified figures for vehicles made for another market. These are not two pieces of evidence of the same kind. 93. The burden of proving that goods fall under the entry that the Revenue invokes lies on the Revenue. It was so held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Hindustan Ferodo Ltd. v. Collector of Central Excise, (1997) 2 SCC 677, and in HPL Chemicals Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, (2006) 5 SCC 208. Both authorities below placed the burden on the Appellant by faulting it for not proving the unladen figure. That was an error. The notice itself records that the Indian website of Hyundai did not state ground clearance and that two of the variants had been discontinued. If the Revenue needed the declared specifications of the Indian variants, the source was HMIL. Section 70(1) gave the proper officer the power to obtain them. It reads: “70. (1) The proper officer under this Act shall have power to summon any person whose attendance he considers necessary either to give evidence or to produce a document or any other thing in any inquiry in the same manner, as provided in the case of a civil court under the provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.” 94. Figures gathered from the internet are not proof of a measurement made according to law. The demand here was of nearly ten crore rupees. The Revenue had the power under Section 70 to obtain the specifications from the manufacturer itself. The point in dispute received little investigation. The notice accepted three of the four conditions from documents. On the fourth, the only one in contest, no vehicle was examined, no statement was recorded on the technical question, and no question was put to the manufacturer. The Revenue printed pages from websites and drew its conclusion from them. The law gave the Revenue several means to prove its case. None was used: (a) Summoning the manufacturer. HMIL made every vehicle in question. A summons under Section 70 would have produced its declared figures for each Indian variant, laden and unladen, with its type-approval papers and the method behind them. HMIL was asked nothing. (b) Obtaining the test reports. ARAI tested the prototypes under Rule 126. The Revenue could have asked ARAI, under Section 70, for its test reports and for any unladen figure recorded during testing. It did not. (c) Testing the source. The notice relied on the website of Hyundai South Africa and on Indian portals. It did not establish who published the figures, when, for which variant, under what load, or by what method. It did not show that the South African “Grand Creta” shares the build of the Indian Alcazar. A printout tells us what a website said on the day it was printed. It does not tell us whether what it said was true. 95. Section 145 may make a computer printout admissible as a document. It reads: “145. Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law for the time being in force,— (a) a micro film of a document or the reproduction of the image or images embodied in such micro film (whether enlarged or not); or (b) a facsimile copy of a document; or (c) a statement contained in a document and included in a printed material produced by a computer, subject to such conditions as may be prescribed; or (d) any information stored electronically in any device or media, including any hard copies made of such information, shall be deemed to be a document for the purposes of this Act and the rules made thereunder and shall be admissible in any proceedings thereunder, without further proof or production of the original, as evidence of any contents of the original or of any fact stated therein of which direct evidence would be admissible.” Admissibility, however, is not proof. A document may be received in evidence and still carry little weight. The weight of a document depends on its source and on whether its contents can be tested. Figures of unknown origin, for vehicles built for another market, cannot prevail over statutory certificates for the very vehicles sold. It was held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Omar Salay Mohamed Sait v. Commissioner of Income Tax, AIR 1959 SC 1238, that a finding which rests on suspicion, conjecture or surmise, and not on evidence, cannot stand. The finding that the vehicles had an unladen ground clearance of 170 mm and above is a finding of that kind. We say this not to fault the officers but to mark what a demand of this kind requires. When the rate of tax turns on a physical measurement, the Revenue must prove the measurement by evidence that can be tested: the manufacturer’s declaration, the certifying agency’s report, or a measurement made in the presence of the assessee. A tax demand cannot be built on material that the assessee cannot question and the adjudicator cannot verify. 96. Even if the unladen figures were correct, they would not matter for supplies made before 26.07.2023, because the laden figure governs those supplies. The Revenue concedes that the Appellant’s payment at 22% after 26.07.2023 cannot by itself create a liability for the past, but it says that the payment corroborates its case. We do not agree. From 26.07.2023 the substituted entry adopted the unladen measure, and the Appellant paid 22% under it. A payment made in obedience to a new entry shows only that the new entry was obeyed. It says nothing about what the earlier entry required, and so it cannot corroborate a demand for the earlier period. We hold that the ARAI certificates are the reliable evidence of ground clearance, and that the vehicles did not satisfy the fourth condition of Entry 52B during the disputed period. Issue (iii): The rate charged by the manufacturer 97. We do not accept the plea that a dealer cannot be asked to pay more than the manufacturer charged. Section 59 reads: “59. Every registered person shall self-assess the taxes payable under this Act and furnish a return for each tax period as specified under section 39.” The dealer’s sale to its customer is a separate supply. The dealer is the person liable to pay cess on it and must apply the correct rate. The FAQ relied on says that classification “in general” follows the manufacturer’s. It does not relieve a dealer of its own duty. The statement of the Appellant’s officer on 17.12.2021, that the Appellant could not confirm whether cess was being charged correctly, shows that this duty was lost sight of. The rate charged by the manufacturer may show good faith, but it is not a defence in law. The plea is rejected. It does not affect the result, in view of our finding on Issues (i) and (ii). Issue (iv): Demand, interest and penalty 98. The demand rests on reading the unladen measure into the period before 26.07.2023. That reading fails. The demand also covers supplies up to 31.07.2023. For supplies from 26.07.2023 the substituted entry applies, and those supplies stand on a different footing. The Revenue did not separate them, did not quantify any short payment on them, and did not show that cess was charged at 20% on any of them. Its own notice records that the Appellant charged 22% on the Tucson after the amendment, and that the Creta 1.6 and Alcazar 2.0 variants had been discontinued. The Revenue’s own written submissions proceed on the footing that the Appellant paid cess at 22% after 26.07.2023. A demand must rest on proof of short payment. No short payment is shown for those days. 99. The figures in the notice also do not reconcile. Two per cent of the taxable value of Rs. 4,88,10,85,294.86 is Rs. 9,76,21,705.90, which is the year-wise total. The notice instead took the difference between its figures at 22% and at 20%. The figure at 20%, Rs. 97,62,17,199.06, is not 20% of the stated taxable value. The orders below confirmed Rs. 9,76,21,566/- without reconciling this. The First Appellate Authority also described the period as starting in November 2017, while the notice starts on 11.09.2017. The amount confirmed in an order must match the computation on which it rests. In view of our conclusion, nothing turns on these differences. 100. Accordingly, the demand of Rs. 9,76,21,566/- is set aside in full. 101. Interest and penalty follow the tax. Section 50(1) reads: “50. (1) Every person who is liable to pay tax in accordance with the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder, but fails to pay the tax or any part thereof to the Government within the period prescribed, shall for the period for which the tax or any part thereof remains unpaid, pay, on his own, interest at such rate, not exceeding eighteen per cent., as may be notified by the Government on the recommendations of the Council.” It was held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Pratibha Processors v. Union of India, (1996) 11 SCC 101, that interest is compensatory and is geared to the tax actually withheld. With no tax unpaid, no interest is due. 102. Section 73(9) reads: “(9) The proper officer shall, after considering the representation, if any, made by person chargeable with tax, determine the amount of tax, interest and a penalty equivalent to ten per cent. of tax or ten thousand rupees, whichever is higher, due from such person and issue an order.” A penalty under Section 73(9) can be imposed only on a person from whom tax is determined to be due. With no tax due, no penalty can be imposed. The Revenue itself accepts that a penalty resting on a demand that fails ordinarily cannot survive. It distinguished H.M.M., Balakrishna Industries, Hyva India and Krishna Sahakari Sakkare Karkhane only on the footing that the demand here stood. That footing is gone. The penalty was imposed under Section 73(9) alone, so the Revenue’s reference to Section 122(2)(a) does not arise. The penalty of Rs. 97,62,157/- falls with the demand. We need not examine the other grounds raised against interest and penalty. Conclusion 103. For these reasons, the appeal is allowed in the following terms: (i). Order-in-Appeal No. 06/2024-25 COMMR-APPEALS-1 dated 25.04.2024 and Order-in-Original No. 10/2023-24 (GST-ADC) dated 19.12.2023 are set aside. (ii). The demand of differential Compensation Cess of Rs. 9,76,21,566/-, the interest under Section 50 and the penalty of Rs. 97,62,157/- under Section 73(9) of the CGST Act, read with Section 11 of the Cess Act, are set aside. (iii). There shall be no order as to costs. (iv). The appeal is disposed of accordingly. Pronounced in Open Tribunal on this the 27th day of October, 2026.

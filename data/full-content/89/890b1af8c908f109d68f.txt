He believes cities like Bengaluru should have more authority over their financial resources. Moreover, families might benefit from increased independence in how public funds are spent. Implementing cash transfers could empower citizens, improve financial inclusion, and heighten accountability in welfare delivery. Drawing a comparison with China, he argued that states should have greater freedom to compete and retain the gains of their economic activity. His argument is about the fiscal space available to states. But it also raises a question that takes the debate much further. If a state seeks greater fiscal freedom from the Union, should a city not seek the same from its state?Consider Karnataka and Bengaluru. Bengaluru accounts for an estimated 60% of Karnataka’s GST revenue, according to the State’s Fifth Finance Commission. If Karnataka argues that it should have greater control over the resources it generates, Bengaluru can make a similar case to the State. If the city accounts for a significant share of Karnataka's economic activity and tax collections, should it not have greater freedom over how those resources are spent? The argument can be extended to neighbourhoods within a city. A prosperous street might ask why the taxes generated by its residents are spent elsewhere rather than on its own roads, drainage or public spaces. Taken to its logical conclusion, this reasoning reaches the smallest unit of governance: the individual. Advt The individual is the final unit of fiscal space. Friedrich Hayek argued in 1945 that knowledge of time, place and circumstance is dispersed across millions of people and cannot be fully possessed by a central planner. A government deciding whether a household needs subsidized grain, electricity or another benefit makes that judgement from a distance. A household receiving cash can make the decision with a better understanding of its own circumstances. Cash transfers therefore do more than provide financial assistance. They give individuals greater control over how a part of public resources is used.The evidence challenges the familiar claim that cash makes people less willing to work or encourages wasteful consumption. A 2017 study by Banerjee, Handa, Kreindler and Olken, published in the World Bank Research Observer, analyzing seven randomized trials across six countries found no systematic evidence that government cash transfers discouraged employment or reduced working hours. A separate World Bank review of 19 studies found that cash transfers almost universally had no significant impact, or a negative impact, on spending on alcohol and tobacco.Indian evidence offers further insight. A 2026 working paper by the Economic Advisory Council to the Prime Minister examined women's cash-transfer programmes in Maharashtra and Odisha using monthly bank-account data. In Maharashtra, beneficiaries' month-end balances increased by 84 per cent and spending by 46 per cent. In Odisha, the corresponding increases were 45 per cent and 28 per cent. The study also found faster UPI adoption among beneficiaries in Maharashtra, suggesting that direct transfers can deepen financial inclusion and expand participation in the digital economy. Advt Cash transfers also offer a more traceable route for welfare delivery. The Direct Benefit Transfer system, supported by the Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, and mobile architecture, enables payments to be recorded against beneficiaries and reduces opportunities for duplication and leakage. A BlueKraft Digital Foundation assessment, published by the Ministry of Finance, estimated cumulative savings of ₹3.48 lakh crore through DBT.Fiscal freedom, however, must come with discipline. The Sixteenth Finance Commission found that large-group unconditional cash transfers rose from 3 per cent of revenue subsidies in 2018-19 to 20.2 per cent in the 2025-26 Budget Estimates across 21 states. It has called for clear eligibility criteria, periodic reviews, and exit provisions. That warning should shape how transfers are designed, not end the debate about their merits. Cash should replace inefficient or poorly targeted subsidies where markets function and households can access the goods and services they need. It should not simply be added to existing commitments. A state that replaces a costly subsidy with a transparent transfer can give households greater choice while making public expenditure more accountable. Advt The argument that begins with a state's demand for fiscal freedom ultimately leads to the individual. Governments seek the discretion to decide how resources should be used within their jurisdictions. What they call a freebie may, in fact, be an extension of the very fiscal freedom they seek for themselves. The word “freebie” should not end policy arguments. It should invite a broader understanding of fiscal freedom, one that extends beyond governments and places greater trust in individuals to decide what their households need.

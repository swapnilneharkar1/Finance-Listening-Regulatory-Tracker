(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in a common judgment delivered on 26 September 2022, examined whether the Central Reserve Police Force (CRPF) was justified in terminating the services of two personnel, Satish Chandra Yadav and Pushpendra Kumar Yadav, for suppressing information regarding criminal proceedings in their recruitment verification forms. Both appellants had challenged the decisions of the Delhi High Court affirming their removal from service. The central issue was whether furnishing false information or withholding material facts concerning criminal antecedents could justify termination, particularly where the criminal proceedings subsequently resulted in acquittal. The Court considered the obligation of candidates seeking appointment to a disciplined force to make truthful disclosures, the employer’s authority to assess suitability, and the legal principles governing suppression of criminal antecedents. Satish Chandra Yadav was recruited as a temporary Constable (General Duty) in the CRPF on 28 July 2014. While completing Verification Form CRP-25 on 2 September 2014, he answered negatively to questions concerning arrest, prosecution, detention and the pendency of criminal proceedings. Subsequent verification by the district authorities revealed that Criminal Case No. 1015 of 2008 had been registered against him at Police Station Khalilabad, Sant Kabir Nagar, Uttar Pradesh, involving offences under Sections 147, 323, 324, 504 and 506 of the Indian Penal Code. The record showed that he had surrendered before the Chief Judicial Magistrate and obtained bail in June 2008. His services were terminated on 11 March 2016 under Rule 5(1) of the Central Civil Services (Temporary Service) Rules, 1965. Although he was acquitted on 13 January 2016, the departmental authorities maintained that he had knowingly concealed material information at recruitment. Satish Chandra Yadav challenged the termination before the Delhi High Court. In the first round of litigation, the High Court directed reconsideration by the revisionary authority. However, the authority reiterated the termination through its order dated 5 January 2018. The subsequent writ petition was dismissed on 15 April 2019. Before the Supreme Court, his counsel argued that the prosecution arose from a family dispute, did not involve moral turpitude, and had ultimately resulted in acquittal. It was submitted that he was approximately 19 years old at the relevant time and that the verification form was vague. Relying on Avtar Singh v. Union of India, (2016) 8 SCC 471, counsel contended that the employer was required to consider the nature of the allegations, the surrounding circumstances and whether the omission was deliberate rather than treating every nondisclosure as an automatic ground for termination. The connected appeal concerned Pushpendra Kumar Yadav, who had joined the CRPF as a Sub-Inspector (General Duty) following recruitment initiated in August 2011. He had answered negatively to questions about criminal proceedings in his verification form despite the existence of Crime Case No. 261 of 2002 registered at Police Station Khajni, Gorakhpur, involving offences under Sections 147, 148, 323, 325, 307, 504 and 506 IPC. The prosecution originated from a village land-related dispute, and the parties had executed a written settlement before the local panchayat on 7 July 2002. Nevertheless, the criminal proceedings remained pending when the verification form was completed on 20 August 2011. Following a departmental inquiry under Section 11 of the CRPF Act, 1949, read with Rule 27 of the CRPF Rules, 1955, he was removed from service on 23 September 2016. His departmental remedies failed, and the Delhi High Court dismissed his writ petition on 4 February 2020. Pushpendra Kumar Yadav argued that he had genuinely believed the criminal dispute had ended following the panchayat settlement. He submitted that he was studying away from his village when the incident occurred, had not understood the relevant questions in the verification form, and had not received summons requiring his appearance. He also relied on an earlier police verification report indicating that nothing adverse had been found against him. The criminal trial ultimately ended in acquittal on 28 July 2015. His counsel emphasised that he had served the CRPF for approximately five years, including substantial service in Kashmir, and had received recognition for his performance. It was argued that dismissal would seriously affect his future employment prospects and that the circumstances warranted a more considerate assessment of his conduct. The Union of India opposed both appeals, maintaining that the appellants had deliberately suppressed material facts concerning their criminal antecedents. The Additional Solicitor General argued that honesty, integrity and transparency were essential requirements for service in a disciplined paramilitary organisation. The subsequent acquittals did not erase the obligation to disclose pending proceedings when specifically asked. The respondent relied on Avtar Singh v. Union of India, Commissioner of Police v. Raj Kumar, (2021) 8 SCC 347, and Union of India v. Methu Meda, (2022) 1 SCC 1, to support the employer’s discretion to assess suitability. It was further submitted that judicial review of recruitment and service-suitability decisions was limited and that courts should not substitute their own assessment for that of the competent employer in the absence of illegality, mala fides or failure to consider relevant material. The Supreme Court undertook an extensive examination of earlier judicial precedents dealing with false declarations, criminal antecedents, acquittals and employment in public service. It considered decisions including Union of India v. M. Bhaskaran, Delhi Administration v. Sushil Kumar, Kendriya Vidyalaya Sangathan v. Ram Ratan Yadav, Kamal Nayan Mishra v. State of Madhya Pradesh, R. Radhakrishnan v. Director General of Police, Union of India v. Bipad Bhanjan Gayen, and Commissioner of Police v. Mehar Singh. The Court also discussed the principles formulated by the three-Judge Bench in Avtar Singh. It recognised that the legal consequences of nondisclosure depend on the facts of each case, including the nature of the post, the wording of the verification form, the seriousness of the underlying proceedings, the candidate’s knowledge and the employer’s assessment. The Court emphasised that uniformed services require particularly high standards of honesty and integrity, while the employer must nevertheless exercise its discretion fairly and with due consideration of relevant circumstances. Applying these principles to Satish Chandra Yadav, the Supreme Court found that his explanation for answering negatively to questions about pending criminal proceedings was unacceptable. He was aware of the criminal case, had surrendered before the Magistrate and had obtained bail. The Court concluded that his failure to disclose the proceedings was deliberate and that the CRPF was justified in terminating his services during probation. The Court observed that the appointment was subject to verification of the particulars furnished by him and that incorrect declarations bearing upon suitability could legitimately result in discontinuance of service. His subsequent acquittal did not establish that the original disclosure was truthful or prevent the employer from acting upon the suppression. In Pushpendra Kumar Yadav’s case, the Supreme Court applied the same reasoning. The record established that criminal proceedings remained pending when he completed the verification form, notwithstanding the earlier village settlement. His explanation that he believed the matter had ended did not justify the incorrect answers furnished to the recruiting authority. The Court considered the distinction that he had rendered approximately four years of service before termination but held that this did not alter the legal position. Consequently, both appeals were dismissed without any order as to costs, and the termination/removal orders were sustained. The judgment reinforces that deliberate concealment of material criminal antecedents can justify exclusion or removal from a disciplined force, even where the underlying criminal proceedings subsequently end in acquittal, subject to a fair assessment of the particular facts.

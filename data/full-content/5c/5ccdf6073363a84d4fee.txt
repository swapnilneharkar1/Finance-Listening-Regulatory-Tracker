Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Vanshika Yadav Vs Union of India & Ors (Supreme Court of India) Summary: Background of NEET UG 2024 Examination and Litigation: The Supreme Court, in Vanshika Yadav Vs Union of India & Ors., considered a batch of writ petitions challenging the validity of the National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (Undergraduate), 2024, conducted by the National Testing Agency (NTA). The examination was held on 5 May 2024 for more than 23 lakh candidates at 4,750 centres across 571 Indian cities and 14 overseas cities. NEET UG is the principal entrance examination for admission to undergraduate medical courses, including MBBS, with approximately 1,08,000 MBBS seats available across government and private institutions. The examination comprised 180 questions across Physics, Chemistry, Botany and Zoology, carrying a maximum of 720 marks. Following the examination, allegations emerged concerning question-paper leakage, irregular distribution of examination materials, suspicious scoring patterns and the award of compensatory marks. Several candidates sought cancellation of the examination, a nationwide re-test, suspension of counselling, investigation into alleged malpractices and directions to improve examination security. The Supreme Court examined whether the established irregularities were sufficiently widespread to compromise the integrity of the entire examination and justify subjecting millions of candidates to another test. Allegations of Paper Leakage and Compensatory Marks: The controversy intensified after reports emerged that the NEET UG 2024 question paper had been leaked at Hazaribagh in Jharkhand and Patna in Bihar. Criminal investigations commenced in several states, and the investigation concerning the principal paper-leak allegations was eventually transferred to the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI). When NTA declared the results on 4 June 2024, it was revealed that 1,563 candidates had received compensatory or grace marks because they allegedly lost examination time at certain centres. The award of these marks attracted objections concerning transparency, equal treatment and its impact on the merit list. A committee subsequently recommended withdrawal of the compensatory marks and offered affected candidates the option of either appearing in a fresh examination or retaining their original scores without grace marks. The Supreme Court considered this corrective arrangement fair and reasonable. A re-test for the affected candidates was conducted on 23 June 2024. The Court distinguished this limited corrective measure from the much broader demand to cancel the entire examination conducted for more than 23 lakh candidates. Supreme Court’s Interim Proceedings and Investigative Directions: During hearings on 8 July 2024, the Supreme Court identified three principal considerations for determining whether a nationwide re-test was justified: whether the breach occurred at a systemic level, whether it compromised the integrity of the entire examination and whether beneficiaries of malpractice could be separated from innocent candidates. The Court required NTA to disclose the chain of custody of question papers, the circumstances of leakage, the locations affected, the possible modes of dissemination and the period between the suspected leak and the examination. The CBI was directed to provide investigation status reports and information concerning suspected beneficiaries. The Union Government and NTA were also asked to examine whether data analytics could identify suspicious examination results. On 18 July 2024, the Court directed NTA to publish anonymised city-wise and centre-wise results to facilitate independent scrutiny. The Ministry of Education obtained a data-analysis report from IIT Madras, while the Court separately sought expert assistance from IIT Delhi concerning a disputed Physics question. These measures formed the evidentiary foundation for determining whether the examination had been compromised on a nationwide scale. Findings Concerning Hazaribagh, Patna and Other Examination Centres: The Supreme Court examined the evidence concerning the handling, transportation and distribution of question papers. NTA stated that the question paper was leaked at Oasis School, Hazaribagh, on the morning of 5 May 2024, when an accused person allegedly accessed examination materials, photographed the paper and transmitted copies for solving and distribution. The CBI’s reports indicated that solved papers were subsequently shared with persons in Patna and Hazaribagh. At the stage of the Court’s decision, approximately 155 candidates, including about 30 in Patna and 125 in Hazaribagh, appeared to be beneficiaries of the fraud. The investigation had not established that the paper or solved answers were circulated indiscriminately through social media on a nationwide scale. The Court also examined irregularities at Sawai Madhopur and other centres, where question papers stored with Canara Bank were distributed instead of those stored with the State Bank of India. At eight centres, approximately 3,307 candidates attempted the alternative paper. In Godhra, authorities had reportedly intercepted a plan to manipulate OMR answer sheets before its execution. Although these incidents demonstrated serious lapses, the Court distinguished established localised irregularities from proof of a systemic nationwide compromise. Disputed Physics Question and Revision of Examination Rankings: An important issue concerned a Physics question relating to the electrical neutrality and stability of atoms. NTA had initially identified option (4), stating that the first proposition was correct and the second incorrect, as the correct answer. Following representations based on an older NCERT textbook, NTA also accepted option (2), which treated both propositions as correct, and awarded marks to candidates selecting either option. The petitioners challenged this decision as arbitrary and detrimental to the fairness of the merit list. On 22 July 2024, the Supreme Court requested IIT Delhi to constitute a three-member expert committee to determine the scientifically correct answer. The expert committee concluded that only option (4) was correct. Accepting this opinion, the Court held that the question itself was valid and that the discrepancy arose from an outdated textbook rather than ambiguity in the examination question. Referring to Kanpur University v. Samir Gupta, the Court examined the importance of accurate answer keys in competitive examinations. It directed NTA to award marks only for option (4), revise the marks of all affected candidates and update the rankings accordingly. IIT Madras Data Analysis and Allegations of Conflict of Interest: The Supreme Court considered the statistical analysis undertaken by IIT Madras at the request of the Ministry of Education. The analysis examined examination results, particularly the distribution of high scores across centres and cities, to determine whether unusual patterns indicated widespread malpractice. IIT Madras reported that the overall distribution of marks followed a broadly bell-shaped pattern and that there was no statistically apparent indication of mass malpractice or a localised concentration of beneficiaries producing abnormal scores. It acknowledged an increase in scores, particularly in the 550–720 range, which the report attributed to a reduction in the syllabus. The petitioners questioned the reliability of the analysis and alleged a conflict of interest because the Director of IIT Madras was an ex officio member of NTA’s General Body. The Court examined the respective responsibilities of NTA’s General Body and Managing Committee and found that the Director’s ex officio membership did not establish bias. It also independently scrutinised the examination data. The Court concluded that the available statistical evidence did not demonstrate a nationwide or systemic leak affecting the examination results. Judicial Principles Governing Cancellation of Examinations: The Supreme Court reviewed the established principles governing the cancellation of examinations affected by cheating, leakage or administrative irregularities. It considered Anamica Mishra v. U.P. Public Service Commission, where cancellation of an entire recruitment process was held unjustified because the irregularity was confined to the interview stage. In Bihar School Examination Board v. Subhas Chandra Sinha, the Court had recognised that widespread unfair practices could justify cancellation where the examination itself had become unreliable. The judgment also discussed Madhyamic Shiksha Mandal, M.P. v. Abhilash Shiksha Prasar Samiti, concerning mass copying, and Sachin Kumar v. Delhi Subordinate Services Selection Board, concerning the distinction between systemic malpractice and identifiable individual wrongdoing. Other relevant authorities included Union of India v. Rajesh P.U., Chairman, All India Railway Recruitment Board v. K. Shyam Kumar, Gohil Vishvaraj Hanubhai v. State of Gujarat and State of Tamil Nadu v. A. Kalaimani. The Court reiterated that cancellation of an entire examination must rest on sufficient material showing that its integrity has been compromised at a systemic level. Where affected candidates can reasonably be identified and separated, wholesale cancellation may be disproportionate. Why the Supreme Court Rejected a Nationwide NEET Re-Test: Applying these principles, the Supreme Court held that the evidence available as of 23 July 2024 did not establish a widespread or systemic breach sufficient to invalidate NEET UG 2024. The Court accepted that the question paper had been leaked at Hazaribagh and Patna, but observed that the investigation had not demonstrated unrestricted dissemination affecting the entire country. The CBI’s preliminary findings identified approximately 155 suspected beneficiaries, while the examination involved more than 23 lakh candidates. The Court found that the city-wise and centre-wise data, together with the comparative analysis of results for 2022, 2023 and 2024, did not support a conclusion that the entire examination was vitiated. It also considered the severe consequences of conducting a fresh nationwide examination, including disruption of medical admissions, delays in the academic calendar, adverse effects on medical education and disproportionate hardship to candidates from economically weaker and marginalised backgrounds. The Court therefore declined to order a nationwide re-test. However, it expressly preserved the power of authorities to identify, investigate and take stringent action against candidates subsequently found to have benefited from malpractice, even after counselling or admission. Final Directions, NTA Accountability and Examination Reforms: The Supreme Court disposed of the writ petitions in accordance with its conclusions pronounced on 23 July 2024 and the detailed reasons delivered on 2 August 2024. While declining to cancel NEET UG 2024, it expressed serious concern regarding NTA’s administration of the examination, including deficiencies in question-paper transportation, custody arrangements, distribution protocols, examination-centre coordination, security safeguards and the handling of candidate grievances. The Court expanded the responsibilities of the seven-member expert committee constituted by the Union Government under the chairmanship of Dr K. Radhakrishnan. The committee was directed to examine examination security, technological safeguards, administrative accountability, grievance redressal, institutional transparency, candidate welfare and measures to prevent future malpractices. Its report was required to be submitted to the Ministry of Education by 30 September 2024. The Ministry was directed to consider the recommendations within one month of receiving the report, prepare an implementation plan and report compliance within the prescribed period. The Court also clarified that individual grievances not resolved by the judgment could be pursued before the jurisdictional High Courts in accordance with law. The decision establishes that confirmed instances of examination malpractice do not automatically justify cancellation of an entire nationwide examination unless sufficient evidence demonstrates systemic compromise and the inability to separate tainted candidates from untainted candidates.

Summary: India’s Global Capability Centres (GCCs) are evolving from traditional operational support centres into sophisticated hubs for international taxation, financial reporting, internal audit, risk management and regulatory compliance. This article examines the changing responsibilities of finance professionals within multinational organisations and highlights the importance of recruiting candidates based on applied competence rather than professional qualifications alone. It discusses the specialist knowledge required for transfer pricing, international corporate taxation, tax provisioning, indirect taxation, internal controls and technology-enabled audit processes. The article distinguishes operational support, specialist execution and advisory responsibilities, emphasising the importance of clearly defined process ownership and decision-making authority. A practical recruitment framework identifies five essential assessment dimensions: technical knowledge, practical responsibility, international exposure, systems capability and stakeholder management. Employers are encouraged to adopt role-specific assessments, establish appropriate reporting structures and distinguish between information preparation, technical review and final approval. The article also examines the growing importance of enterprise resource planning systems, financial data analytics, automated control testing and workflow automation in global finance operations. Continuous professional development, knowledge-sharing programmes and career progression based on increasing technical responsibility are identified as important components of employee retention. The article concludes that sustainable GCC operations require a combination of professional qualifications, specialist knowledge, technology capabilities, practical experience and clear accountability. By aligning recruitment and development strategies with actual business requirements, multinational organisations can strengthen their finance, taxation and audit operations while creating meaningful career opportunities for professionals in India. These centres, which historically focused on operational support and cost efficiencies, now undertake increasingly sophisticated functions across finance, technology, analytics, risk and compliance. The Association of Chartered Certified Accountants (ACCA), in its 2025 report Global Capability Centres in India: Trends, Opportunities and the Evolving Role of Talent, highlights the transition of finance roles from routine transaction processing towards process improvement, business partnering and higher-value activities. This shift has important implications for taxation and audit functions. An organisation establishing a finance capability centre may initially transfer activities such as reconciliations, tax data preparation, reporting support and audit documentation. As the centre matures, its responsibilities may expand to transfer pricing analysis, international tax compliance support, internal control testing, regulatory reporting and tax technology implementation. However, these responsibilities do not necessarily require the same professional skills. A qualified accountant experienced in domestic statutory reporting may be highly capable within that specialisation but require additional training before handling international tax matters. Similarly, an internal auditor familiar with financial controls may need further expertise to evaluate technology-enabled controls across multinational systems. The central challenge is therefore not simply finding qualified professionals. It is identifying the right combination of technical knowledge, practical experience and organisational capability. 1. Why GCC Tax and Audit Roles Are Changing The evolution of GCCs has created opportunities for finance professionals to undertake responsibilities that were previously concentrated in corporate headquarters or regional finance teams. The Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) has recognised this development. Its 2025 GCC Summit initiative focused on the expanding role of Chartered Accountants within global finance operations. For tax and audit functions, the change can be understood across three levels. Operational support: Teams prepare reconciliations, financial information, supporting documentation and standard reports. Specialist execution: Professionals undertake technical tax analysis, control testing, compliance preparation and financial reporting activities under established oversight arrangements. Advisory and process ownership: Experienced professionals contribute to risk assessments, policy interpretation, process improvements, stakeholder discussions and the design of compliance controls. These levels should not be confused with job titles or years of experience. A professional may have extensive experience in operational processing without having independently interpreted complex tax matters. Employers should therefore define the level of judgement and accountability required before deciding the qualifications and experience necessary for a particular position. 2. Transfer Pricing Requires Specialist Knowledge Transfer pricing is an important consideration for multinational organisations conducting transactions between associated enterprises. India’s transfer pricing framework includes documentation and reporting requirements for applicable transactions. The Income Tax Department’s guidance explains relevant documentation obligations, while Rule 10D addresses information and records to be maintained in prescribed circumstances. For a GCC supporting transfer pricing activities, professional responsibilities may include: Preparing and maintaining intercompany transaction documentation. Supporting functional, asset and risk analyses. Gathering financial information for benchmarking exercises. Reviewing intercompany arrangements. Supporting transfer pricing reporting and compliance documentation. Coordinating with external advisers and group tax teams. These activities require different levels of expertise. For example, gathering transaction data is fundamentally different from selecting a transfer pricing methodology or evaluating the economic substance of an arrangement. A recruitment process should therefore distinguish between candidates who have assisted with transfer pricing documentation and those who have independently undertaken technical analysis. This distinction can help organisations assign responsibilities appropriately and reduce excessive dependence on senior specialists for routine activities. 3. International Tax Expertise Goes Beyond Domestic Compliance Many finance professionals develop their early careers around domestic accounting standards and Indian tax compliance. International finance operations may require additional familiarity with foreign tax systems, cross-border reporting requirements and the interaction between accounting and taxation. Depending on the organisation’s structure and responsibilities, relevant expertise may include: Corporate tax compliance: Preparing supporting schedules, reconciling tax accounts and coordinating information required for jurisdiction-specific returns. Tax provisioning: Understanding the financial reporting implications of current and deferred tax, including applicable accounting standards. Indirect taxation: Supporting transaction classifications, reconciliations and reporting for relevant jurisdictions. International tax operations: Coordinating information relating to intercompany arrangements, withholding taxes and cross-border transactions. Tax technology: Using enterprise resource planning systems, reporting tools and automation to improve the accuracy and timeliness of tax-related processes. A candidate’s ability to work effectively within a global tax function should be assessed against the actual jurisdictions and responsibilities involved. Knowledge of Indian GST compliance, for example, should not automatically be treated as evidence of experience with overseas indirect tax systems. 4. Internal Audit Teams Need Risk and Technology Capabilities Internal audit functions within GCCs may support enterprise-wide governance, risk management and control assurance. According to ACCA’s research on Indian GCCs, some centres already undertake global internal audit and control-related activities. This creates a need for professionals who can understand both financial processes and technology-enabled operations. Relevant capabilities may include: Internal control evaluation. Risk-based audit planning. Financial process testing. IT general controls. Audit documentation and evidence management. Enterprise risk assessment. Data analytics and continuous monitoring. Coordination with internal and external audit stakeholders. Professional qualifications such as CA, CIA, CISA or ACCA may be relevant, depending on the role. However, qualifications should complement rather than replace an assessment of applied competence. Consider a candidate who has participated in multiple internal audits. The important question is not merely how many audits were completed. It is whether the individual identified risks, assessed controls, documented exceptions, communicated findings and contributed to corrective actions. The distinction between participation and ownership is particularly important when recruiting managers and senior specialists. 5. A Practical Framework for Evaluating Candidates GCC employers can improve recruitment decisions by structuring assessments around five dimensions. Assessment dimension What employers should evaluate Technical knowledge Relevant taxation, accounting, audit or regulatory knowledge Practical responsibility Work personally completed rather than merely supported International exposure Familiarity with relevant jurisdictions and reporting requirements Systems capability ERP, tax reporting, audit and data analysis tools Stakeholder management Ability to communicate with global finance, tax and audit teams A role-specific assessment can provide more useful information than relying solely on a candidate’s designation or professional qualification. For a transfer pricing position, a practical exercise might require the candidate to explain how they would gather information for a functional analysis. For an internal audit position, the candidate might be asked to identify control weaknesses in a hypothetical financial process. For a tax reporting position, the assessment could examine how the candidate would investigate discrepancies between ledger balances and supporting tax calculations. Such exercises should be proportionate to the role, protect confidential information and test skills that the successful candidate will genuinely need. 6. Structuring Teams for Effective Operations A GCC should determine its operating model before undertaking large-scale recruitment. An effective structure might distinguish among: Tax and audit leadership: Professionals responsible for governance, technical oversight, stakeholder coordination and accountability. Subject-matter specialists: Individuals with detailed expertise in transfer pricing, corporate tax, indirect taxation, internal audit or relevant financial reporting areas. Operational professionals: Team members handling defined compliance processes, reconciliations, documentation and reporting activities. Technology and data specialists: Professionals supporting tax automation, controls monitoring, financial systems and reporting infrastructure. The appropriate balance will vary according to the GCC’s scale and responsibilities. For smaller operations, some responsibilities may be combined. Larger centres may require separate teams covering specialist disciplines. In either case, reporting lines and decision-making authority should be clearly documented. The distinction between preparing information, reviewing it and approving a technical conclusion is essential for maintaining effective controls. 7. Technology Skills Are Becoming Part of Finance Competence Finance and tax processes increasingly depend on integrated business systems. ERP platforms, workflow automation, data extraction tools and analytical applications can improve consistency and reduce repetitive manual work. However, technology also introduces risks associated with data quality, access permissions, system configuration and inadequate review procedures. Consequently, tax and audit professionals may benefit from developing capabilities in: Financial data analysis. Spreadsheet modelling and reconciliation. ERP-based financial processes. Process documentation. Data visualisation. Automated control testing. Workflow and reporting automation. The objective is not to turn every accountant into a software developer. It is to ensure that professionals understand the systems supporting their work, can evaluate the reliability of information and recognise circumstances requiring additional technical expertise. Employers should therefore distinguish between essential technology skills and specialist capabilities that can be supplied by dedicated technical teams. 8. Professional Development and Retention Technical recruitment is only one component of building a sustainable GCC capability. Organisations should also consider how employees will develop expertise after joining. A structured development programme may include rotations across finance functions, training in overseas reporting requirements, exposure to global stakeholders and opportunities to participate in process-improvement initiatives. Career progression should recognise increasing technical responsibility and the ability to manage complex assignments. For example, a professional preparing compliance schedules may progress towards independently reviewing those schedules, resolving technical issues or supervising a wider reporting process. This provides a more meaningful development path than progression based on tenure alone. Knowledge-sharing programmes can also reduce operational dependence on individual specialists and support continuity when employees move between teams. 9. What GCC Employers Should Prioritise Before launching a major tax or audit recruitment programme, management should answer several questions. What work will the Indian team actually own? Preparation, technical review, advisory responsibility and final approval involve different levels of accountability. Which jurisdictions and regulatory frameworks are relevant? This determines the appropriate technical experience and training requirements. What professional qualifications are genuinely necessary? Requirements should be proportionate to the responsibilities assigned. Which systems will employees use? Technology requirements should be reflected in the role description and assessment process. How will performance be evaluated? Employers should consider quality, accuracy, risk management, process improvement and stakeholder satisfaction rather than measuring output volume alone. What responsibilities will remain with the parent organisation? Clarity over oversight and decision-making helps avoid gaps between GCC teams and headquarters. These questions provide a starting point for designing a recruitment strategy aligned with business requirements. Conclusion The development of Global Capability Centres in India has created opportunities for tax, audit and finance professionals to contribute to increasingly complex multinational operations. However, the effectiveness of these teams depends on more than the availability of qualified candidates. Organisations need to define responsibilities precisely, distinguish operational experience from technical expertise and evaluate candidates against the actual requirements of each position. For tax functions, this means understanding the difference between preparing information and independently interpreting complex tax matters. For audit functions, it means assessing the ability to identify risks, evaluate controls and communicate findings rather than simply counting completed assignments. Technology capabilities, international exposure and stakeholder management are also becoming important considerations as GCCs assume broader responsibilities. A sustainable recruitment approach should combine appropriate professional qualifications with practical assessment, continuous training and clear accountability. As India-based GCCs continue to develop, the opportunity for finance professionals will lie not only in executing established processes but also in improving their quality, reliability and effectiveness. Building teams with these capabilities can help organisations strengthen their finance operations while creating meaningful professional development opportunities.

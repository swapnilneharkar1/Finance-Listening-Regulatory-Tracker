Vs State of Goa (Civil Appeal No. 3615 of 2023 and connected appeal, decided on 10 May 2023), examined the permissible scope of judicial interference with an arbitral award under Sections 34 and 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. The dispute arose from a Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) dated 10 January 1997 between Reliance Infrastructure Ltd. and the Government of Goa for establishing and operating a power generation station. The original generating capacity was 39.8 MW, which was subsequently revised through supplementary agreements. Disagreements arose concerning electricity tariffs, variable fuel charges, downrating of generating capacity, charges relating to 4 MW of electricity permitted to be sold to third parties, backup power adjustments and interest on unpaid invoices. The controversy ultimately reached the Supreme Court after the Bombay High Court, Goa Bench, partially interfered with an arbitral award that had been upheld by the Commercial Court. The claimant had supplied electricity to the Government of Goa under the contractual arrangements and subsequently proposed using Regassified Liquefied Natural Gas (RLNG) as an alternative fuel. The Government initially communicated its willingness to purchase electricity at ₹8.58 per unit, but the claimant clarified that this rate was based on prevailing fuel prices and the US dollar exchange rate and was therefore subject to fluctuations. The claimant relied on subsequent correspondence, particularly the communication dated 23 May 2013, to establish that the Government had accepted formula-based pricing linked to fuel costs and exchange rates. However, disputes arose when the Government failed to clear invoices from May 2013 onwards. The claimant initiated proceedings before the Joint Electricity Regulatory Commission, which referred the disputes to arbitration. By an award dated 16 February 2018, the sole arbitrator directed payment of ₹278.29 crore, comprising principal and interest calculated up to 31 October 2017, together with further interest as specified in the award. The Government of Goa challenged the award under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, contending that the arbitrator had disregarded material contractual provisions, improperly interpreted correspondence between the parties and failed to consider relevant evidence. The Principal District and Sessions Judge, North Goa, acting as the Commercial Court, rejected the challenge on 12 September 2019 and upheld the award. However, in an appeal under Section 37, the Bombay High Court, Goa Bench, partly allowed the Government’s challenge on 8 March 2021. It set aside portions of the award relating to variable charges of approximately ₹24.66 crore, downrating of approximately ₹18.53 crore, variable charges on 4 MW power of approximately ₹3.94 crore and netting-out adjustments of approximately ₹2.36 crore. The High Court also reduced post-award interest from 15% to 10% per annum. Both parties consequently approached the Supreme Court, challenging different aspects of the High Court’s decision. The Supreme Court reiterated that judicial review of arbitral awards is confined to the limited grounds prescribed under Section 34, and the appellate jurisdiction under Section 37 does not permit a fresh examination of the dispute on merits. Referring to decisions including Associate Builders v. Delhi Development Authority, Ssangyong Engineering and Construction Co. Ltd. v. NHAI, Delhi Airport Metro Express Pvt. Ltd. v. Delhi Metro Rail Corporation Ltd. and Haryana Tourism Ltd. v. Kandhari Beverages Ltd., the Court explained that an arbitral award cannot be interfered with merely because another interpretation of the contract or evidence is possible. The concept of patent illegality requires a defect falling within the recognised statutory grounds and cannot be expanded to include ordinary factual or interpretative disagreements. Where an arbitrator has adopted a plausible view after considering the contractual provisions and evidence, courts cannot substitute their own conclusions merely by describing the award as erroneous, perverse or patently illegal. Regarding variable charges arising from the use of RLNG, the Supreme Court examined the contractual arrangements, correspondence exchanged between the parties and the arbitrator’s findings concerning the agreed tariff formula. The arbitrator had concluded that the Government accepted the principle of determining electricity charges according to prevailing fuel prices and foreign exchange rates. The Supreme Court found that the High Court had effectively reassessed the evidence and substituted its interpretation of the contractual communications for that of the arbitrator. The Court emphasised that the arbitrator’s interpretation was supported by relevant material, including the parties’ correspondence and subsequent conduct. The High Court’s disagreement with that interpretation did not establish patent illegality or justify interference under Sections 34 and 37. Accordingly, the Supreme Court rejected the High Court’s interference with the award concerning variable fuel charges. On the downrating of generating capacity, the Supreme Court considered the supplementary PPAs, the revised contracted capacity, the Original Equipment Manufacturer’s certificate and the minutes of meetings between the parties. The arbitrator had examined these materials and concluded that the issue of downrating had effectively been settled through the contractual arrangements and the parties’ subsequent conduct. The Government had previously accepted and paid invoices calculated on the agreed basis. The Supreme Court held that the High Court was not justified in reassessing these documents and drawing different factual inferences merely because it preferred another interpretation. The Court similarly examined the dispute concerning variable charges on 4 MW of electricity permitted to be sold to third parties. It found that the arbitrator’s interpretation of the relevant correspondence and contractual obligations could not be displaced through an appellate reassessment of the evidence. The High Court’s interference with these components of the award was therefore unsustainable. The Supreme Court also addressed the netting-out mechanism for backup electricity and the award of interest. Regarding backup power, the arbitrator had considered the relevant contractual arrangements and communications, including the rate of ₹3.78 per unit, before determining the parties’ respective obligations. The Supreme Court concluded that the High Court had exceeded its jurisdiction by substituting its interpretation for the arbitrator’s findings. On interest, the Court examined the award under Section 31 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act and rejected the High Court’s reduction of post-award interest from 15% to 10% per annum. It distinguished the circumstances considered in Vedanta Ltd. v. Shenzhen Shandong Nuclear Power Construction Co. Ltd. and noted that the prevailing prime lending rate of the State Bank of India was approximately 13% to 14% per annum. In these circumstances, the arbitrator’s award of 15% post-award interest was within his jurisdiction, and the High Court had no sufficient legal basis to alter it. The Supreme Court ultimately allowed the appeal filed by Reliance Infrastructure Ltd. and dismissed the appeal filed by the State of Goa. It set aside the portions of the Bombay High Court’s judgment dated 8 March 2021 that modified the arbitral award and the Commercial Court’s order, thereby restoring the arbitral award dated 16 February 2018 in its entirety, including the original interest provisions. The Court emphasised that the Commercial Court had appropriately examined the objections raised under Section 34 and that no patent illegality apparent on the face of the award had been established. The judgment reinforces the principle that arbitration is intended to provide finality to commercial disputes and that courts must exercise restraint when reviewing arbitral awards. Mere disagreement with an arbitrator’s interpretation of contractual terms, appreciation of evidence or computation of claims cannot justify judicial interference unless the statutory grounds for setting aside the award are satisfied.

SEO Title: Permanent Establishment Risk in India: Fixed Place, Agent, Service PE and SEP SEO Description: Understand PE risks for foreign companies in India: fixed place, dependent agents, service PE, SEP, EOR arrangements, profit attribution and tax compliance. Summary: Foreign companies conducting business in India without establishing a formal Indian subsidiary or branch may still face Indian income-tax exposure if their activities constitute a permanent establishment (PE) under an applicable tax treaty or a business connection under domestic law. The article examines the major sources of PE risk, beginning with fixed-place establishments and the Supreme Court’s decision in Formula One World Championship Ltd. v. CIT, which emphasised the availability of premises to the foreign enterprise and the conduct of business through those premises. It then discusses dependent-agent permanent establishments, particularly where Indian personnel negotiate or conclude contracts for an overseas company, and considers the distinction between establishing a PE and attributing taxable profits to it in light of DIT v. Morgan Stanley & Co. The article also addresses service PE exposure arising from employees, secondees and consultants working in India, including the importance of tracking treaty-specific presence thresholds. A separate section explains significant economic presence (SEP) under Section 9(1)(i) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, including the prescribed transaction and user thresholds, and its interaction with tax treaties. Employer of Record arrangements are examined as another area of potential exposure where legal employment arrangements differ from the commercial control exercised by an overseas enterprise. A practical risk matrix identifies common triggers and corresponding mitigation measures. Finally, the article discusses Indian profit attribution, corporate tax rates, reporting obligations and related GST considerations. Its central recommendation is that businesses should assess actual conduct, decision-making authority, personnel movements and commercial functions rather than relying exclusively on contractual labels. Fixed place PE Article 5 of most treaties covers a fixed place of business through which the enterprise carries on business. In Formula One World Championship Ltd v CIT (Supreme Court, 2017), the Court held that a motor racing circuit was a fixed place PE on the facts, applying the tests of the physical location, the right of disposal and the business carried on through it. The principle for taxpayers is that a premises need not be owned or leased. What matters is whether the place is at the disposal of the foreign enterprise and the business is carried on through it. 2. Dependent agent PE Where a person habitually concludes contracts, or habitually plays the principal role leading to contracts concluded without material modification, on behalf of the foreign enterprise, a dependent agent PE can arise (treaty Article 5(5) and, domestically, the business connection rules in section 9(1)(i) with its Explanation 2). In DIT v Morgan Stanley (Supreme Court, 2007), the Court recognised that a PE could arise but also held that, where the Indian entity was remunerated at arm’s length, no further profit was attributable to the PE in respect of the same functions, subject to the facts. The outcomes of agent PE disputes therefore turn on both existence and attribution. 3. Service PE Many Indian treaties provide that furnishing services through employees or personnel in India for more than a stated period (often in the region of 90 to 183 days) creates a PE. Calculating the days is the usual weak point, because visits by group employees, secondees and consultants add up. 4. Significant economic presence (SEP) Explanation 2A to section 9(1)(i) of the 1961 Act treats a non-resident as having a business connection in India by reason of SEP, based on transaction value or user thresholds (notified at Rs 2 crore of transactions and 3 lakh users). The domestic provision does not by itself override a treaty, so a treaty resident can ordinarily rely on the treaty definition of PE. The domestic rule is nonetheless the basis for reporting positions by taxpayers in non-treaty jurisdictions, and it matters for the group’s overall risk assessment. 5. Employer of record arrangements An EOR arrangement is sometimes described as eliminating PE risk. It does not. The employees are legally employed by the EOR, but the question remains whether the foreign company, through the people it directs in India, has a fixed place or a dependent agent there. Factors that increase risk: the personnel conclude or negotiate contracts for the foreign company; the foreign company directs the day-to-day work in an office space it has at its disposal; the roles are core to the business, not preparatory or auxiliary; the arrangement continues for years with no plan to move to an entity. An EOR is best presented as a time-limited bridge with a documented conduct protocol. 6. Attribution and reporting If a PE exists, profits attributable to it are taxed at the foreign company rate of 35% plus surcharge and cess, and a return is required. Rule 10 and the transfer pricing provisions apply to attribution. A PE also brings a tax audit obligation and GST registration questions. Practical takeaway: audit the conduct, not just the contracts. The organisation chart in India and the people who negotiate with customers matter more than the label on the engagement.

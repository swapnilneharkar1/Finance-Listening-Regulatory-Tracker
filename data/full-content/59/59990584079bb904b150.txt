Vs Goodyear India Ltd. and Another (Supreme Court of India) Mesne Profits Must Reflect Reliable Rental Evidence and Include Interest, Rules Supreme Court Summary: The Supreme Court held that mesne profits for wrongful occupation must be assessed on reliable evidence of prevailing rental value, taking into account the nature, location and use of the premises. In a dispute concerning a godown in Daryaganj, New Delhi, the Court upheld mesne profits of ₹7 per sq. ft. per month for the ground floor and ₹5 per sq. ft. per month for the mezzanine floor. It also affirmed that interest is an integral component of mesne profits and upheld the award of 7.5% simple interest per annum, subject to the Delhi High Court’s concession for timely payment. Facts of the Case The landlords terminated Goodyear India Ltd.’s tenancy with effect from midnight on 31 December 1989 and instituted a suit in 1990 for possession and damages for continued occupation. They claimed ₹1,47,500 per month from 2 January 1990 until delivery of vacant possession, together with 15% interest per annum. The Trial Court decreed recovery of possession in December 1998 and directed a separate inquiry under Order XX Rule 12 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to quantify mesne profits during the pendency of the suit and thereafter. The possession decree attained finality in 2001. The subsequent litigation concerned the amount payable for continued occupation and the interest thereon. Why the Nearby Bank Lease Was an Unreliable Comparable In the mesne profits inquiry, the Trial Court fixed monthly rates of ₹22 per sq. ft. for the ground floor and ₹11 per sq. ft. for the mezzanine, calculated on carpet area, together with 15% interest. It placed considerable reliance on a nearby lease to Vijaya Bank, under which the ground floor and basement had been let at a consolidated rate of ₹22 per sq. ft. The First Appellate Court found material differences between the properties. The suit premises were in an older building, comprised a ground floor and mezzanine, and were used as a godown. The bank occupied a ground floor and basement in a building constructed in 1972 and used it for banking. The record also lacked valuation material or evidence of comparable construction quality. The First Appellate Court therefore discarded the bank lease as a comparable and assessed mesne profits using the admitted contractual rent. Overlooking a Material Admission Can Raise a Substantial Question of Law The Delhi High Court found that the First Appellate Court had overlooked the testimony of Goodyear’s own officer, who stated in his examination-in-chief that prevailing rents in the locality at the time of termination ranged from ₹5 to ₹8 per sq. ft. per month. The omission directly affected the assessment and justified interference in second appeal under Section 100 CPC. The Supreme Court agreed. The High Court had evaluated the admission alongside the rest of the evidence and arrived at separate rates for the ground floor and mezzanine, both within the range disclosed by the witness. Relying on Martin and Harris Private Limited v. Rajendra Mehta, (2022) 8 SCC 527, the Supreme Court explained that quantification calls for a fair and reasonable estimate based on the evidence and circumstances. An evidence-based determination ordinarily warrants appellate deference unless it is manifestly arbitrary, unsupported by the record or based on an error of law. Interest Runs From the Respective Months in Which Mesne Profits Fell Due The Supreme Court upheld 7.5% simple interest per annum from the respective months in which mesne profits became payable until realisation. It also upheld the High Court’s direction reducing the rate to 6% per annum if the decretal amount was paid within the two months stipulated by the High Court. Section 2(12) CPC expressly includes interest in the definition of mesne profits. Referring to Mahant Naryana Dasjee Varu v. Board of Trustees, The Tirumalai Tirupathi Devasthanam, 1964 SCC OnLine SC 125, the Court reiterated the compensatory character of interest. It dismissed both appeals. Author’s Comments For landlords and tenants litigating mesne profits, this judgment shows why a lease from the same locality can carry limited weight. The usefulness of a comparable depends on its date, permitted use, building condition, floor configuration and area basis. A consolidated rent for a bank’s ground floor and basement may tell the court little about what an older ground-floor godown with a mezzanine could command during the relevant period. The ruling also illustrates how to frame a substantial question of law in a dispute over quantification. The decisive point was that the First Appellate Court overlooked specific testimony that directly addressed prevailing rents. For pleadings and computation, the month-wise due dates deserve equal attention. The judgment affirms interest as part of mesne profits, while the 7.5% rate and conditional reduction to 6% reflect the decree in this case. FULL TEXT OF THE JUDGMENT OF SUPREME COURT 1. The present Appeals arise out of a long-standing landlord-tenant dispute concerning premises bearing No. XI/4239-A/2 (Plot No. 3A), 1, Ansari Road, Daryaganj, New Delhi, owned by the appellants and originally let out to the respondent no. 1, M/s Goodyear India Ltd. Although the litigation was instituted seeking both recovery of possession of the suit premises and damages for its continued use and occupation after termination of the tenancy, the decree for possession has long since attained finality. The controversy which now falls for consideration is confined to the determination of mesne profits payable by the respondent no. 1-tenant for its continued occupation of the premises after the tenancy stood terminated, and, more particularly, the correctness of the rate of mesne profits and interest as determined by the High Court of Delhi1 in the impugned judgment dated 11.08.2011 passed in RSA No. 186 of 2010 and RSA No. 187 of 2010. 2. The original suit2 was instituted in the year 1990 by Late Shri Prem Chandra Jain, Smt. Kund Prabha Jain and Smt. Swayam Prabha Jain, whose legal representatives are presently on record as the appellants, seeking recovery of possession of the suit property after termination of the respondent no. 1’s tenancy with effect from midnight of 31.12.1989. Besides recovery of possession, the plaintiffs claimed damages for use and occupation at the rate of Rs. 1,47,500/- per month (equivalent to Rs. 4,916.67 per day) with effect from 02.01.1990 till delivery of vacant possession, together with interest at the rate of 15% per annum. During the course of the trial, both parties led evidence, including on the issue relating to the determination of damages for use and occupation. 3. By judgment and decree dated 22.12.1998, the learned Trial Court decreed the suit for recovery of possession in favour of the plaintiffs. Insofar as the claim for damages for the single day preceding the institution of the suit was concerned, the Trial Court held that the evidence adduced by the plaintiffs was insufficient to establish that the premises could have been let out for a single day at the rate claimed and, consequently, declined to grant the said relief. However, the Trial Court considered it appropriate to pass a preliminary decree under Order XX Rule 12 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 19083, directing that a separate inquiry be conducted for determination of the pendent lite and future mesne profits payable to the plaintiffs. Thus, while the decree for possession stood adjudicated by the judgment dated 22.12.1998, the quantification of mesne profits was expressly left open for determination in subsequent proceedings. 4. The respondent no. 1-tenant questioned the decree for possession by filing RFA No. 318 of 1999 before the High Court. The appeal came to be dismissed by judgment dated 23.11.2000, affirming the decree passed by the Trial Court. The respondent no. 1 thereafter preferred SLP (C) No. 3879 of 2001 before this Court, which was dismissed in limine on 19.03.2001. Consequently, the decree for possession attained finality and the lis between the parties thereafter entered a second stage, confined exclusively to the inquiry contemplated under Order XX Rule 12 CPC for determination of mesne profits. 5. Pursuant to the preliminary decree, the appellants, along with the other legal representatives of the original plaintiffs, initiated proceedings4 under Order XX Rule 12 CPC before Trial Court seeking determination of mesne profits payable by the respondent no. 1. By judgment and decree dated 30.04.2004, the Trial Court rejected the respondent no.1’s plea of res judicata and held the proceedings under Order XX Rule 12 CPC to be maintainable. On the merits of the inquiry, the Trial Court determined mesne profits at the rate of Rs. 22/- per square foot per month in respect of the carpet area of the ground floor and Rs. 11/- per square foot per month in respect of the carpet annum. Certain clerical errors in the decree were thereafter corrected by orders dated 24.05.2004 and 23.11.2004, resulting in the issuance of amended decrees. 6. Aggrieved by the determination of mesne profits, the respondent no. 1 preferred RCA No. 40 of 2004, subsequently renumbered as RCA No. 9 of 2009, before the learned Additional District Judge5. The appellants, in turn, filed cross-objections contending that the Trial Court ought to have determined mesne profits at the rate of Rs. 30/- per square foot per month for the ground floor and Rs. 15/- per square foot per month for the mezzanine floor on the basis of the covered area of the premises. They further contended that interest formed an integral component of mesne profits and ought to have been awarded from month to month from the respective dates on which each month’s mesne profits became due. By judgment and decree dated 12.07.2010, the learned First Appellate Court allowed the appeal preferred by the respondent no. 1, set aside the judgment and decrees passed by the Trial Court. The First Appellate Court found that the Trial Court’s determination of mesne profits at the rate of Rs. 22/- per square foot per month for the ground floor and Rs. 11/- per square foot per month for the mezzanine floor, on the carpet area, was unsupported by material evidence and was speculative and presumptuous. It also held that the corresponding finding of the Trial Court with respect to interest on mesne profits could not be sustained. The First Appellate Court, however, proceeded on the basis of the admitted rate of rent for determining the mesne profits payable by the respondent. 7. Now aggrieved, the appellants thereupon preferred RSA Nos. 186 and 187 of 2010 before the High Court. RSA No. 186 of 2010 related to the determination of mesne profits for the period from 02.01.1990 to 30.03.2001, whereas RSA No. 187 of 2010 pertained to the period from 02.01.1990 to 30.04.1999. In both appeals, the appellants sought restoration of the Trial Court’s determination while simultaneously pressing their claim for enhancement of mesne profits to Rs. 30/- per square foot per month for the ground floor and Rs. 15/- per square foot per month for the mezzanine floor, together with interest at the rate of 15% per annum calculated from the respective dates on which each month’s mesne profits became payable. 8. By the common judgment dated 11.08.2011, the High Court partly allowed both the second appeals. The High Court held that the First Appellate Court had overlooked a material admission made by the respondent no. 1’s own witness (DW-1 P.V. Ramani, officer of respondent no. 1) regarding the prevailing rate of rent in the locality and that such omission constituted a substantial question of law warranting interference under Section 100 of CPC. Proceeding on that basis, the High Court modified the judgment of the First Appellate Court and determined mesne profits at the rate of Rs. 7/- per square foot per month in respect of the ground floor and Rs. 5/- per square foot per month in respect of the mezzanine floor for the respective periods under consideration. The High Court further directed payment of simple interest at the rate of 7.5% per annum from the respective months in which the mesne profits became payable till realization, while providing that, in the event the to 6% per annum. It is against this common judgment of the High Court which has given rise to present appeals before this Court. 9. We have heard the learned counsel for the parties and have perused the material placed on record. 10. The controversy before us lies in a narrow compass. The decree for recovery of possession has attained finality in 2001 and what survives now for consideration is the correctness of the determination of mesne profits by the High Court. It is well settled that mesne profits are compensatory in nature and are intended to recompense the true owner for the loss occasioned by the continued wrongful occupation of his property. Their assessment is not governed by any rigid or inflexible formula. The Court is required to arrive at a fair and reasonable estimate having regard to the nature and location of the property, the prevailing rental value, the evidence adduced by the parties and the attendant circumstances of each case. Equally, such determination is essentially one founded upon appreciation of evidence and, unless shown to be manifestly arbitrary, unsupported by the record or based upon an erroneous application of law, does not ordinarily warrant appellate interference. [Refer: Martin and Harris Private Limited and Another vs. Rajendra Mehta and Others6] 11. In the present case, the Trial Court had determined mesne profits at the rate of Rs. 22/- per square foot per month for the ground floor and Rs. 11/- per square foot per month for the mezzanine floor. In arriving at the said determination, considerable reliance was placed upon the evidence relating to the premises let out to Vijaya Bank at No. 2/10, Ansari Road, Daryaganj, where the ground floor and basement had been let at a consolidated rate of Rs. 22/- per square foot. The evidence of the owner of those premises as well as the Branch Manager of Vijaya Bank was relied upon in support of the prevailing rental value. 12. The First Appellate Court, however, examined the comparability of the two properties and found that the premises occupied by the respondent could not simply be equated with the premises let out to Vijaya Bank. The former were old buildings, stated to have been in existence since the 1950s, whereas the Vijaya Bank premises had been constructed in 1972. Further, the suit premises comprised the ground floor and mezzanine floor forming part of the same premises and were being used as a godown, whereas the premises occupied by Vijaya Bank comprised the ground floor and basement and were being used for banking purposes. The First Appellate Court also noticed the absence of any valuation report or municipal valuation establishing similarity between the two properties and the absence of evidence regarding the comparative quality of construction. It was, therefore, not persuaded to treat the rent paid by Vijaya Bank as a reliable comparable for determining the mesne profits of the suit premises. 13. We have carefully examined the reasoning adopted by the High Court. The High Court noticed that, while reversing the determination made by the made by the respondent no. 1’s own witness (DW-1 P.V. Ramani, officer of respondent no. 1) in his examination-in-chief regarding the prevailing rental value of comparable properties in the locality at the relevant point of time. The respondent no. 1’s witness had stated that at the time the tenancy stood terminated, the prevailing rate of rent in the locality ranged between Rs. 5/- and Rs. 8/- per square foot per month. Such omission was not insignificant. The omission to consider a material piece of evidence having a direct bearing on the determination of mesne profits was rightly regarded by the High Court as giving rise to a substantial question of law requiring correction. 14. In the impugned judgment, the High Court did not proceed on the premise that the aforesaid admission of respondent no. 1’s witness, by itself, concluded the matter. The admission merely constituted an important circumstance forming part of the evidentiary record. The question was not merely whether the lowest rate which could be said to have been admitted by the respondent should mechanically be adopted. In the impugned judgment, the High Court evaluated the material as a whole and thereafter determined the mesne profits at Rs. 7/- per square foot per month in respect of the ground floor and Rs. 5/- per square foot per month in respect of the mezzanine floor. We find that the determination represents a balanced assessment founded upon the evidence available on record. The High Court did not revive the Trial Court’s reliance upon the Vijaya Bank premises, nor did it equate the suit property with the premises occupied by the bank. The rate so determined falls within the evidentiary range disclosed by the respondent’s own witness and cannot be characterised as arbitrary, speculative or based on no evidence so as to warrant interference by this Court. 15. We are, therefore, unable to persuade ourselves to accept the contention that the impugned judgment suffers from any legal infirmity. On the contrary, the High Court corrected the omission committed by the First Appellate Court in overlooking material evidence and thereafter proceeded to determine the mesne profits on a reasonable appreciation of the record. We find neither perversity in the findings recorded nor any misapplication of the governing legal principles. 16. We also find no reason to interfere with the direction relating to interest passed by the High Court. ‘Interest’ forms an integral component of mesne profits and is intended to compensate the owner for being deprived of the beneficial enjoyment of the property during the period of wrongful occupation.7 Having regard to the facts and circumstances of the present case, the award of simple interest at the rate of 7.5% per annum, with the concession of a reduced rate of 6% per annum upon payment within the time stipulated by the High Court, does not call for interference. 17. There is yet another aspect which deserves notice. The present litigation traces its origin to the termination of the tenancy with effect from 31.12.1989. The decree for possession attained finality more than two decades ago i.e., in 2001. The impugned judgment was rendered by the High Court on 11.08.2011 and the appeals have remained pending before this Court for nearly fifteen years. The controversy now survives only with respect to the quantification of mesne profits on the basis of evidence which has remained unchanged throughout the proceedings. Having independently examined the approach adopted by the High Court and finding no legal error therein, we are of the considered view that the interests of justice would be better served by according finality to a litigation which has engaged the parties for more than three decades. 18. In view of the foregoing discussion, we are satisfied that the impugned judgment represents a fair and reasonable determination of the mesne profits payable by the respondent no. 1 on the basis of the evidence available on record. 19. Consequently, no interference is warranted. The Appeals are dismissed.

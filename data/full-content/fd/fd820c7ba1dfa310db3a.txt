Summary: The Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers – Disclosure Scheme, 2026 (FAST-DS), introduced through Chapter IV, Sections 130–144 of the Finance Act, 2026, provides a time-bound opportunity to regularise specified undisclosed foreign income and assets, with declarations required by 31 December 2026. The scheme primarily benefits employees holding foreign ESOPs or RSUs, returning NRIs, former overseas students and taxpayers who omitted foreign investments from Schedule FA. Section 133 establishes two categories: previously untaxed foreign income and assets aggregating up to ₹1 crore attract 30% tax plus an equal additional amount, effectively 60%, while qualifying foreign assets acquired from taxed income or income earned during non-resident status, valued up to ₹5 crore, attract a flat ₹1 lakh fee. The Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers – Disclosure Scheme Rules, 2026, notified through CBDT Notification No. 114/2026, prescribe valuation as on 31 March 2026, electronic declarations in Form 1, departmental orders in Form 2, payment intimations in Form 3 and validity certificates in Form 4. Significant compliance concerns include the cumulative-deposit valuation of foreign bank accounts, aggregate eligibility thresholds, payment deadlines, false declarations and exclusion of specified cases involving money laundering or completed Black Money Act assessments. Valid declarations provide specified immunity under the Black Money Act, 2015, but not under FEMA or other laws. The article also examines unresolved questions involving foreign tax credit, dividends from RSUs, inherited assets, inter-account transfers, LRS investments and the comparative advantages of updated returns. Accurate source documentation, category selection, asset valuation and separate FEMA review are essential before filing. LRS investments Assessee’s checklist Conclusion Show all 22 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Why FAST-DS matters now Any taxpayer with a foreign asset or foreign income missing from past returns has until 31 December 2026 to settle it under the Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers – Disclosure Scheme, 2026 (FAST-DS). The cost is either a flat ₹1 lakh or about 60% of the value, depending on how the asset was funded. The settlement brings immunity under the Black Money Act. The timing is not accidental. Through CRS and FATCA, the Income Tax Department now receives information on Indian residents’ overseas bank accounts, brokerage holdings and RSU plans. On 25 September 2026 many taxpayers received a system-generated email about the scheme. That email is not a notice, but it shows the department already holds the data. The typical cases are not large evasion cases. They are omissions such as these: A savings account opened while studying abroad RSUs from a foreign employer, taxed through payroll but left out of Schedule FA US shares bought under LRS A property or account held by an NRI who later became resident Who the scheme is meant for. FAST-DS is aimed at small taxpayers, not large offshore evasion. Its core audience is: employees of multinational companies holding ESOPs and RSUs of a foreign parent; students who went abroad to study and left a bank account or small investments behind; returning NRIs whose overseas holdings were never moved into Schedule FA. In each case the foreign assets total ₹5 crore or less, which is the Category 2 ceiling. Where the money was never taxed, the ceiling is ₹1 crore. Without the scheme, such an omission can attract a ₹10 lakh penalty per year under Section 43 of the Black Money Act, 2015. If the source of the asset cannot be explained, tax is 30% plus a penalty of 90% of the value. There is no income test and no age test. Eligible persons (Section 131) A person who was resident in India in the relevant year. No return of income was filed. 2. A return was filed before the scheme began, but the foreign asset or income was left out. 3. The income escaped assessment within the meaning of Section 147. There is no scaled charge for the excess. Three consequences follow: Each category is tested separately, so a declarant may use both in one Form 1. Exceeding a limit bars that category in full. Declaring only some assets to stay under the limit is not permitted, and it risks voiding the declaration under Section 134(3). Category 2 covers only the asset. Unreported income the asset produced (dividends, interest, rent, gains) is foreign income, and it falls in Category 1. Valuation under Rule 3 Every asset is valued as on 31 March 2026, generally at the higher of cost of acquisition and fair market value. In Category 1 that value is the base for the 60% charge. In Category 2 it only decides whether the ₹5 crore limit is met. Money withdrawn and spent over the years is never subtracted. A modest account used for salary and living expenses abroad can therefore carry a value several times its balance, enough to breach the ₹1 crore limit. Practitioners should obtain statements from the date of opening before advising on category. The 20% safe harbour. For assets other than bank accounts, a declared value within 20% of the department’s valuation is not treated as misrepresentation on that ground alone. Procedure and payment: Forms 1 to 4 The 31 December deadline applies only to filing Form 1. Payment follows the department’s order, so the immunity certificate may arrive well into 2027. Form 1: declaration (Rule 5). Filed by the declarant electronically and verified by DSC or EVC. It lists each asset and income item, its value, and the self-computed amount for each category. Portal path: e-File → Income Tax Forms → File Income Tax Forms → Forms as per other Acts → Form 1 of FADS 2026. Form 2: order (Rule 6, Section 135(1)). Issued electronically by the Pr. DG / DG of Income-tax (Systems), fixing the tax, additional amount or fee payable. Form 3: payment intimation (Rule 7). Filed by the declarant with proof of payment. Part payments are allowed. Payment within two months from the end of the month in which Form 2 is received carries no interest. Payment can still be made within a further two months under Section 135, with interest as prescribed in Rule 7. The outer limit is therefore four months from the end of the month in which Form 2 is received. If the amount is not paid within the outer limit, the declaration is void. Under Section 138, nothing already paid is refunded. Form 4: certificate (Rule 8, Section 139). Issued within one month from the end of the month in which Form 3 is filed. It certifies the declaration and the payment, and it works as the immunity certificate. Forms 1 to 3 are annexed to it. Immunity, and where it stops A valid declaration, certified in Form 4, closes the declared items under the Income-tax Act and the Black Money Act. It does nothing for items left out, or under any other law. What it covers, for the declared asset or income (up to the previous year 2025-26) No further tax, penalty or prosecution under the Black Money Act. The declared income, or the amount invested in the declared asset, is not included in total income under the Income-tax Act or the Black Money Act (Section 136). A pending Black Money Act assessment must take the declaration into account (Section 141). What it does not cover Assets or income not declared. Completed assessments: they are not reopened in the declarant’s favour (Section 137). Refunds of any amount paid (Section 138). Declarations with false particulars, which are void at any stage (Section 134(3)). Liability under any other law. The FEMA gap The FEMA gap. The scheme gives no immunity under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. Many LRS holdings involve no FEMA breach at all, but some cases do: an account kept open after returning to India, an overseas investment outside the permitted route, or delayed reporting. These must be regularised separately: delayed reporting through the Late Submission Fee mechanism; substantive contraventions through compounding with the RBI. Form 1 creates a record with the department. The FEMA position should therefore be reviewed before filing, not after. Grey areas practitioners must watch Five questions are not answered by the Act, the Rules or the FAQs. Each can change both the category and the cost, so they need a reasoned view before Form 1 is filed. 1. Foreign tax credit 1. Foreign tax credit. Foreign income taxed abroad but not reported in India is undisclosed foreign income, so it falls in Category 1. Neither Section 133 nor the Rules allow credit for the foreign tax against the 60% charge. Section 138 bars refunds. A DTAA argument under the elimination-of-double-taxation article exists, but the scheme gives no way to claim it. For years still open, an updated return (ITR-U) is often cheaper. The tax is at slab rates plus the additional tax, and foreign tax credit can be claimed through Form 67. But ITR-U does not give the express Black Money Act immunity that Form 4 gives. 2. Dividends on RSUs 2. Dividends on RSUs. The shares were taxed as a perquisite, so they fall in Category 2 at ₹1 lakh. The unreported dividends are foreign income and fall in Category 1, with no credit for the US tax withheld. A split approach is worth weighing: dividends through ITR-U, then the shares through Category 2. 3. Meaning of “withdrawal” in bank-account valuation 3. Meaning of “withdrawal” in bank-account valuation. The word is not defined. Since the rule exists to prevent double counting, it should cover any debit: cash, wire, cheque, card, or a transfer to the declarant’s own account. But only withdrawals traced to a redeposit or a reinvestment reduce the value, and the burden of tracing is on the declarant. Transfers between two of the declarant’s own foreign accounts are not clearly addressed. 4. Inherited foreign assets 4. Inherited foreign assets. An inheritance, or a gift from a relative, is not income. Category 2 needs an asset acquired from foreign income earned while non-resident, or from income already offered to tax, and an inheritance is neither. Category 1 needs an “undisclosed asset”, meaning one whose source cannot be explained, and a documented inheritance is explained. So the asset may sit outside both categories. The only exposure may then be the Section 43 penalty for not reporting it in the return. A gift from a non-relative above ₹50,000 is different: it is income, and it falls in Category 1 if never offered to tax. 5. LRS investments 5. LRS investments. An asset bought with an LRS remittance from taxed savings and left out of Schedule FA is a Category 2 case. TCS proves only that the remittance went out, not that the money had been taxed. Keep ready: Form A2 and the bank’s remittance advice; the debit in the Indian bank statement; Form 27D and the AIS entry; ITRs for the years the savings came from; the purchase documents abroad. These issues, foreign tax credit and inheritance in particular, are strong candidates for a representation to the CBDT before 31 December. Assessee’s checklist If you hold, or have ever held, any asset or earned any income outside India, go through this list before 31 December 2026. List what you hold abroad. Include bank accounts (even dormant ones from student days), ESOPs and RSUs, foreign shares and mutual funds, insurance policies, property, and interests in foreign entities. Check your past returns. Did each year’s ITR show these in Schedule FA, and the income they earned (dividends, interest, rent, gains) in the income schedules? Work out where the money came from. Was it salary taxed in India, savings remitted under LRS, income earned while you were an NRI, an inheritance or gift, or something you cannot document? Collect the papers. Gather bank and broker statements from the date each account was opened, RSU vesting statements and Form 16, Form A2 and remittance advices, TCS certificates, and purchase deeds. Get the value as on 31 March 2026. For a foreign bank account, the value is the total of all deposits made, not today’s balance. It can be much higher than you expect. Check the limits. Assets bought from taxed money or NRI income must total ₹5 crore or less. Untaxed assets and income together must total ₹1 crore or less. Confirm you are not excluded. The scheme is not available if PMLA proceedings have been initiated against you, or if a Black Money Act assessment for the year is already complete. Compare the options with your Chartered Accountant: FAST-DS, an updated return (ITR-U), or no action if there was no default. Review FEMA separately. Tax immunity does not cover FEMA, so check whether any compounding with the RBI is needed. File Form 1 early on the e-filing portal, keep funds ready to pay within two months of the Form 2 order, and preserve the Form 4 certificate permanently. Conclusion FAST-DS is a narrow, well-priced exit for a specific problem: foreign assets that were bought with clean or explainable money but never reported. For that group, ₹1 lakh for Black Money Act immunity is a very good outcome. For income that was never taxed, the 60% charge is still half of what the Black Money Act would levy, but it may cost more than an updated return. The scheme rewards preparation. The right category, the right valuation and a clean FEMA position decide whether the client gets a final settlement or a void declaration.

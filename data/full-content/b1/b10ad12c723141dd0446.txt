Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Wockhardt Limited Vs Commissioner of Central GST & Central Excise (CESTAT Ahmedabad) Summary: The Ahmedabad Bench of the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), comprising Judicial Member Somesh Arora and Technical Member Satendra Vikram Singh, examined whether a 100% Export Oriented Unit (EOU) was entitled to remission of customs duty and central excise duty on imported and indigenously procured raw materials, capital goods and machinery destroyed in an accidental fire. Wockhardt Limited challenged Order-in-Original No. 99/Commr./Surat-II/2012 dated 31.12.2012, passed by the Commissioner of Central Excise and Customs, Surat-II, denying remission of duty and confirming demands of customs and excise duties, interest, penalties and redemption fine on the ground that the fire had occurred due to negligence and violation of prescribed industrial safety requirements. The appellant operated a 100% EOU at Ankleshwar, Gujarat, and procured imported and indigenous raw materials and capital goods without payment of applicable duties for manufacturing goods intended for export. On 03.04.2010, a major fire occurred at its factory premises, destroying raw materials, inputs, work-in-progress, finished goods, capital goods, plant and machinery. The incident was intimated to the Department on 05.04.2010. Following permission from the Disaster Management Authority to enter the affected premises on 20.04.2010, departmental officers prepared a panchnama dated 21.04.2010, recording the duty involved on imported and indigenous goods at Rs.6,16,48,174. A show cause notice dated 04.04.2011 proposed recovery of customs duty and central excise duty on the ground that the goods had not been used for their intended purpose and the corresponding export obligations had not been fulfilled. The Department relied upon a report dated 08.04.2010 issued by the Assistant Director, Industrial Safety and Health, Bharuch, which attributed the possible cause of the fire to temporary discontinuity in the earthing circuit and generation of sparks during the transfer of solvents. The adjudicating authority also observed that the appellant had stored substantial quantities of inflammable chemicals and solvents beyond the permissible limits prescribed under the Gujarat Factory Rules. It concluded that the fire could have been prevented through effective earthing arrangements and adherence to the prescribed storage limits. Consequently, the Commissioner treated the incident as an avoidable accident attributable to negligence, carelessness and wilful omission, resulting in contravention of the bonded warehouse licence conditions and the obligations undertaken under the B-17 bond of Rs.15 crore. Wockhardt contended that Section 23 of the Customs Act, 1962, expressly provides for remission of customs duty where imported goods are lost otherwise than through pilferage or are destroyed before clearance for home consumption. Since the imported raw materials and capital goods remained within the factory-cum-customs bonded warehouse when they were destroyed, the statutory conditions for remission stood fulfilled. The appellant submitted that neither removal for home consumption nor unauthorised diversion or pilferage had occurred. It relied upon decisions including Commissioner of Central Excise Vs Symphony Services Corporation India Pvt. Ltd., Commissioner of Central Excise & Customs Vs Welspun Terri Towels, Winsome Yarn Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise and Jindal Vijaynagar Steel Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs to contend that duty could not be recovered merely because the intended manufacturing and export obligations became impossible to fulfil following accidental destruction of the goods. Regarding the indigenously procured goods, the appellant relied upon Rule 6 of the Central Excise (Removal of Goods at Concessional Rate of Duty for Manufacture of Excisable Goods) Rules, 2001, and Rule 21 of the Central Excise Rules, 2002. It argued that goods lost or destroyed by natural causes or unavoidable accidents during handling or storage were deemed to have been used for their intended purpose under the Explanation to Rule 6. The appellant further relied upon Sami Labs Limited Vs Commissioner of Customs, subsequently upheld by the Karnataka High Court, and Aditya Industries Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, wherein it had been recognised that raw materials already issued for production and capital goods installed and put to use could not be treated as having remained unused merely because they were subsequently destroyed in an accidental fire. The appellant disputed the Commissioner’s findings concerning negligence and violation of safety requirements. It submitted that necessary precautions had been taken and that the earthing connections were properly maintained. Affidavits from independent agencies and the plant-in-charge were furnished to substantiate the precautions adopted to prevent accidents. The appellant had also offered the deponents for cross-examination, but the Commissioner had not undertaken such examination. It argued that the affidavits consequently remained uncontroverted. Further, the appellant submitted that, being an EOU, it was required to maintain its raw material stock within the bonded premises and that the Commissioner had incorrectly applied the permissible quantity relating to the workroom to the entire bonded warehouse. The show cause notice and adjudication order did not allege any mala fide intention, fraudulent diversion or deliberate destruction of the goods. Wockhardt additionally challenged the quantification and legal basis of the duty demands. It contended that Section 11A of the Central Excise Act, 1944, and Sections 28 and 72 of the Customs Act, 1962, could not be invoked when the underlying goods qualified for remission. Alternatively, it submitted that depreciation on imported and indigenous capital goods should be allowed from the date of installation and that the applicable duty rate had been incorrectly determined. The appellant relied upon Khabros Steel India Limited, Shiva Analyticals (I) Ltd. and Maharashtra Hybrid Seeds Co. Ltd. in support of its depreciation claim. It also challenged confiscation under Rule 25 of the Central Excise Rules and Sections 111(j) and 111(o) of the Customs Act, together with the consequential redemption fine, interest and penalties. The adjudicating authority had partly accepted the appellant’s objection concerning arithmetic errors in the computation of duty on indigenous capital goods. It found that the computation for 2008-09 contained duplication of subtotals and erroneous treatment of decimal figures. Accordingly, the duty demand on indigenous capital goods was corrected to Rs.1,89,33,701. The Commissioner also confirmed central excise duty of Rs.1,81,956 on indigenous inputs and raw materials, customs duty of Rs.1,43,782 on imported inputs and raw materials and customs duty of Rs.53,11,185 on imported capital goods. Penalties of Rs.50 lakh under Rule 25 of the Central Excise Rules and Rs.25 lakh under Section 72 read with Section 112 of the Customs Act were imposed, together with applicable interest and redemption fine of Rs.1.25 crore. The Commissioner further directed enforcement of the B-17 bond. In its rejoinder before the Tribunal, Wockhardt placed particular reliance on the settlement of its insurance claim following verification and investigation by the insurance surveyor. Against a total claim of Rs.14,29,42,353, the insurance company had allowed Rs.13,18,37,216. Goods valued at Rs.95,72,768 had been salvaged and subsequently cleared on payment of duty amounting to Rs.18,86,176. The appellant clarified that the insurance claim did not include the duty component because the goods had originally been procured duty-free under the EOU scheme. According to the appellant, the acceptance of its insurance claim after independent investigation supported its contention that the destruction was accidental and was not attributable to any deliberate wrongdoing. Upon examining the rival submissions, the Tribunal observed that the industrial safety report itself acknowledged that earthing arrangements, including earthing wires, strips and an earthing pit, were in place. The report identified temporary discontinuity in the earthing circuit as a possible reason for the incident. The Tribunal considered the statutory provisions applicable to remission of duty and the judicial precedents governing goods destroyed by accidental fire. It noted that the entitlement to remission under Section 23 of the Customs Act could not be denied merely because the importer was an EOU or because the Department alleged that adequate care had not been taken. The Tribunal examined the decisions recognising that goods destroyed before clearance for home consumption qualify for remission, subject to satisfaction of the statutory requirements. The Tribunal referred to judicial decisions including Tej Shoe Factory, Sanskriti Packaging Pvt. Ltd., Commissioner of Central Excise Vs M. Kumar Udhyog (P) Ltd., Union of India Vs Hindustan Zinc Limited and Commissioner of Customs Vs Next Fashion Creators Pvt. Ltd. These decisions emphasised that the expressions ‘natural causes’ and ‘unavoidable accident’ must receive a reasonable and practical interpretation to advance the legislative objective of duty remission. A hypothetical possibility that an accident could have been prevented through additional precautions cannot, by itself, justify denial of remission. The Tribunal also considered the relevance of reports issued by competent authorities and the settlement of insurance claims following independent investigation while assessing whether destruction of goods resulted from an unavoidable accident. Ultimately, the Tribunal held that Wockhardt was entitled to remission of customs and central excise duties in respect of machinery and imported and indigenous raw materials found destroyed in the fire by the insurance company, representing an accepted insurance claim of Rs.13,18,37,216. The corresponding duty demands, penalties and interest were held unsustainable. However, the Tribunal identified an unreconciled difference of Rs.15,32,369 between the amount originally claimed, the insurance claim admitted and the value of goods already cleared as salvage. It directed the appellant to explain this difference to the Department and permitted clearance of any remaining goods available as salvage upon payment of applicable duty. Subject to this limited reconciliation requirement, the appeal was allowed with all consequential reliefs. Cases Discussed Tej Shoe Factory — 2018 (363) ELT 863 (CESTAT Allahabad) — Fire caused by an electrical short circuit may constitute an unavoidable accident for granting remission of excise duty. The adjudicating authority cannot substitute its findings for those of competent fire authorities without adequate evidence. Techtextil Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Indore — 2017 (355) ELT 442 (CESTAT Delhi) — Remission of customs and central excise duties was allowed on goods destroyed in an accidental fire at a 100% EOU. Sumit Chemicals Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Kanpur — 2016 (337) ELT 299 (CESTAT Allahabad) — Cited in support of remission of duty on goods destroyed in an accidental fire. Sanskriti Packaging Pvt. Ltd. — 2015 (318) ELT 451 (CESTAT Ahmedabad) — Settlement of an insurance claim and findings of investigating authorities were considered relevant in determining whether a fire accident was unavoidable. Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai Vs Joy Foam Pvt. Ltd. — 2015 (322) ELT 209 (Madras High Court) — Inputs used in goods subsequently destroyed by natural causes or unavoidable accidents are deemed to have been consumed in manufacture, and reversal of credit cannot be demanded in the absence of an applicable statutory provision. Commissioner of Central Excise Vs M. Kumar Udhyog (P) Ltd. — 2014 (306) ELT 19 (Allahabad High Court) — The expressions natural causes and unavoidable accident under Rule 21 must be interpreted reasonably to advance the legislative objective of remission of duty. Commissioner of Customs, Bangalore Vs Next Fashion Creators Pvt. Ltd. — 2012 (280) ELT 374 (Karnataka High Court) — A 100% EOU is entitled to remission under Section 23 of the Customs Act on imported goods destroyed before clearance for home consumption, subject to satisfaction of the statutory requirements. Sheela Foam Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Noida — 2012 (283) ELT 85 (CESTAT) — Satisfaction of the Commissioner for remission under Rule 21 must be based on an objective analysis of facts and cannot rest on unsupported allegations of negligence. Commissioner of Customs Vs Sami Labs Limited — 2012 (278) ELT 601 (Karnataka High Court) — Upheld the Tribunal’s decision concerning remission of duty on goods destroyed in an accidental fire and rejected the interpretation that work-in-progress was covered by the expression during handling under the Explanation to Rule 6. Commissioner of Central Excise, Surat-II Vs Favourite Industries — 2012 (278) ELT 145 (Supreme Court of India) — Relied upon by the adjudicating authority concerning strict interpretation of fiscal exemption notifications. Commissioner of Central Excise, Bangalore Vs Symphony Services Corporation India Pvt. Ltd. — 2012 (275) ELT 369 (Karnataka High Court) — Explained the distinction between abatement of duty under Section 22 and remission of duty under Section 23 of the Customs Act, 1962. Supreme Industries Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Pondicherry — 2011 (271) ELT 241 (CESTAT) — Remission was allowed where independent reports established that the fire occurred due to unavoidable circumstances and allegations of negligence lacked corroborative evidence. Voltamp Transformers — 2010 (262) ELT 909 (CESTAT) — Cited by the appellant in support of its remission claim. Biopac India Corporation Ltd. — 2010 (258) ELT 56 (Gujarat High Court) — Cited by the appellant among the authorities supporting remission of duty. Commissioner of Central Excise, New Delhi Vs Hari Chand Shri Gopal & Ors. — 2010 (260) ELT 3 (Supreme Court of India) — Explained the principles governing interpretation of fiscal exemptions and compliance with prescribed conditions. Dharmapuri Co-operative Sugar Mills Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai — 2010 (253) ELT 303 (CESTAT Chennai) — Relied upon by the adjudicating authority for the proposition that remission may be denied where loss is entirely attributable to the assessee’s negligence. Aurobindo Pharma Ltd. — 2009 (247) ELT 820 (CESTAT) — Cited by the appellant in support of its claim for duty remission. Aditya Industries Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Ahmedabad — 2009 (249) ELT 567 (CESTAT) — Followed Sami Labs Limited concerning goods destroyed by accidental fire after being put to their intended use. Vamsadhara Paper Mills Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs, Visakhapatnam — 2009 (247) ELT 751 (CESTAT) — Duty demand was held unsustainable where goods intended for use under an exemption notification were destroyed by accidental fire. Jandial Shoe Factory Vs Commissioner of Central Excise — 2009 (239) ELT 203 (CESTAT) — Relied upon by the adjudicating authority to justify denial of remission on grounds of negligence. Union of India Vs Hindustan Zinc Limited — 2009 (233) ELT 61 (Rajasthan High Court) — Held that the expressions natural causes and unavoidable accident must receive a reasonable, liberal and practical interpretation under Rule 21. Commissioner of Customs, Bangalore Vs Maharashtra Hybrid Seeds Co. Ltd. — 2008 (226) ELT 575 (CESTAT) — Recognised entitlement to depreciation on capital goods where export obligations had been partially fulfilled. Biopac India Corporation Ltd. — 2008 (224) ELT 548 (CESTAT) — Cited by the appellant in support of remission of duty. Shiva Analyticals (I) Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs, Bangalore — 2008 (224) ELT 417 (CESTAT) — Relied upon in support of allowing depreciation on capital goods while determining duty liability. Bombay Dyeing & Manufacturing Co. — 2008 (223) ELT 514 (CESTAT) — Cited by the appellant among the authorities supporting its remission claim. Sami Labs Limited Vs Commissioner of Customs, Bangalore — 2007 (216) ELT 59 (CESTAT Bangalore) — Held that raw materials issued for production and installed capital goods destroyed by accidental fire had been put to their intended use and duty foregone could not be recovered merely because of their subsequent destruction. Grasim Industries — 2007 (208) ELT 336 (CESTAT Larger Bench) — Held that remission of duty on goods destroyed by natural causes or unavoidable accidents cannot be equated with exemption from duty and that reversal of input credit was not required under the applicable provisions. Jindal Vijaynagar Steel Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs, Mangalore — 2006 (201) ELT 18 (CESTAT) — Remission under Section 23 was allowed on imported goods destroyed before clearance for home consumption. Insurance coverage was held to be governed by separate legal provisions. Hindustan Cables Ltd. — 2006 (198) ELT 527 (CESTAT) — Cited by the appellant in support of its remission claim. Khabros Steel India Limited Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Jaipur — 2006 (114) ELT 117 (Tribunal), as cited in the source order — Relied upon in support of allowing depreciation on old capital goods when determining customs duty liability upon debonding. Onida Savak Limited — 2004 (176) ELT 143 (CESTAT) — Cited by the appellant among the authorities supporting its remission claim. Mount Shivalik Breweries — 2003 (157) ELT 9 (Delhi High Court) — Cited by the appellant in support of its submissions concerning remission of duty. Commissioner of Central Excise Vs Indchem Electronics — 2003 (157) ELT A206 (Supreme Court of India) — The Supreme Court dismissed the Revenue’s appeal against the Tribunal’s decision. Voltas Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise — 2003 (156) ELT 295 (CESTAT) — Relied upon by the adjudicating authority concerning the interpretation of unavoidable accident and the requirement of reasonable precautions. Commissioner of Central Excise Vs Indchem Electronics — 2003 (151) ELT 393 (CESTAT) — Considered the treatment of input credit where finished goods were destroyed by fire and remission of duty was granted. Commissioner of Central Excise & Customs, Surat Vs Welspun Terri Towels — 2002 (149) ELT 593 (CESTAT) — Held that goods destroyed by accidental fire while lying in a bonded warehouse qualified for remission under Section 23 and that speculative allegations of inadequate precautions could not justify denial. Winsome Yarn Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Chandigarh — 2001 (134) ELT 686 (CESTAT) — Clearance from the port to a bonded warehouse cannot be equated with clearance for home consumption. Imported goods destroyed by fire in the warehouse were eligible for remission. Ginni Filaments — 2001 (128) ELT 273 (CESTAT) — Cited by the appellant in support of its remission claim. Weston Components Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs — 2000 (115) ELT 278 (Supreme Court of India) — Considered the imposition of redemption fine where goods had been released against execution of a bond. S.K. Patnayak — 2000 (115) ELT 9 (Supreme Court of India) — Distinguished in Jindal Vijaynagar Steel Ltd. because the decision concerned the Bihar and Orissa Excise Act, 1915, rather than Section 23 of the Customs Act, 1962. Ganpati Enterprises — 1999 (113) ELT 394 (Calcutta High Court) — Cited by the appellant among the authorities supporting remission of duty. Quest International — 1998 (100) ELT 358 (CESTAT) — Cited by the appellant in support of its submissions concerning remission of duty. Commissioner of Central Excise Vs Foods, Fats and Fertilisers Ltd. — 1989 (41) ELT 277 (Tribunal) — Held that duty could not be demanded on materials intended for specified use within the factory when they were destroyed by accidental fire without diversion for another purpose. Inalsa Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, New Delhi (CESTAT) — The view that remission of duty cannot be equated with exemption and that input credit need not be reversed was approved by the Larger Bench in Grasim Industries. Mafatlal Industries (CESTAT) — The view requiring reversal of input credit where finished goods were destroyed by fire was not approved by the Larger Bench in Grasim Industries. Natural Stones Exports (CESTAT) — Referred to in Maharashtra Hybrid Seeds Co. Ltd. in connection with depreciation on capital goods.

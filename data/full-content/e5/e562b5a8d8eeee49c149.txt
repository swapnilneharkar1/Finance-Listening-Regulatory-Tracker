Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Rekha Khaitan Vs DCIT (ITAT Delhi) Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT), Delhi Bench, adjudicated cross-appeals filed by Rekha Khaitan and the Revenue for Assessment Year 2019-20 concerning an addition under Section 69B of the Income-tax Act, 1961 on account of unexplained jewellery discovered during search proceedings. A search and seizure action under Section 132 was conducted on 19 January 2019 in the case of the Gautam Khaitan group. Jewellery weighing 5,066.69 grams was seized from the assessee’s residence and another 9,092.56 grams from her bank locker. The assessee had declared taxable income of Rs. 1,57,590 in her income tax return and had previously disclosed jewellery weighing 11,198.25 grams in her wealth tax return for AY 2015-16. The assessee relied upon invoices for subsequent purchases of jewellery and explained that additional jewellery could have been received as gifts from relatives and friends on festivals and customary occasions after AY 2015-16. She stated that she had been married for over 60 years and that exchange of gifts was customary in the family. She also relied upon CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11 May 1994 concerning jewellery found during search operations. However, the Assessing Officer made an addition of Rs. 1,14,15,054 under Section 69B, read with Section 115BBE, based on the valuation of jewellery considered unexplained. The Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) granted partial relief by considering the jewellery disclosed in the wealth tax return, subsequent documented purchases, inheritance and the permissible jewellery allowance under CBDT Instruction No. 1916. The addition was restricted to Rs. 38,60,667, representing unexplained jewellery weighing 770.90 grams. In the first round of litigation, the Tribunal allowed the Revenue’s appeal and dismissed the assessee’s appeal on 13 June 2023. The assessee challenged that decision before the Delhi High Court, raising the question whether an addition under Section 69B could be sustained without establishing the necessary jurisdictional facts and solely on the basis of the Departmental Valuation Officer’s report. The High Court restored the matter to the Tribunal for fresh adjudication. During the remand proceedings, the Revenue argued that CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dealt only with seizure of jewellery and did not establish that jewellery falling within its prescribed limits was acquired from explained sources. It further submitted that the jewellery found during the search did not correspond item-wise with the jewellery declared in earlier wealth tax returns, that the assessee had not furnished evidence of subsequent gifts and that her declared income was insufficient to explain additional purchases. The assessee relied upon coordinate Bench decisions recognising that jewellery is frequently remodelled or converted in accordance with changing fashion and customary practices. The Tribunal found the decisions in DCIT Vs Raj Kumar Saraogi and ACIT Vs Sunil Kumar Khemka applicable to the Revenue’s objections. These decisions recognised that insisting upon an exact item-wise reconciliation of jewellery found during a search with jewellery disclosed several years earlier could impose an unreasonable burden, considering the customary practice of remaking ornaments. The latter decision also referred to the Rajasthan High Court ruling in CIT Vs Satya Narain Pami and the Jodhpur Tribunal ruling in DCIT Vs Arjun Dass Kalwani concerning the relevance of CBDT-prescribed jewellery limits in determining whether jewellery could reasonably be treated as explained. The CIT(A) had reconciled the total jewellery weighing 14,159.25 grams found during the search against 11,198.25 grams disclosed in the wealth tax return, 1,950 grams allowed under CBDT Instruction No. 1916, including an additional 500 grams considering the family’s status, 91.55 grams received by inheritance and 148.55 grams supported by purchase records. The total explained jewellery was accordingly determined at 13,388.35 grams, leaving a balance of 770.90 grams. The Tribunal also noted that the Delhi High Court had clarified that the reference to Section 69A in the CIT(A)’s order was a typographical error and that the addition was to be construed as having been made under Section 69B. The Tribunal distinguished the Delhi High Court decision in CIT Vs Dinesh Jain HUF on facts, observing that jewellery exceeding the quantity disclosed in the wealth tax return had actually been found during the search and that the assessee was required to explain its source irrespective of whether she maintained books of account. Her income declared subsequent to AY 2015-16 was insufficient to justify further alleged purchases beyond the credit already granted by the CIT(A). Finding no infirmity in the reconciliation and relief granted by the CIT(A), the Tribunal upheld the addition of Rs. 38,60,667 attributable to 770.90 grams of unexplained jewellery and dismissed both the assessee’s and the Revenue’s appeals. Cases Discussed DCIT Vs Raj Kumar Saraogi (ITAT Kolkata); ITA No. 1776/Kol/2012 and IT(SS)A No. 97/Kol/2013; 01/09/2015: Relied upon for the principle that jewellery is frequently remodelled according to changing fashions and that exact item-wise reconciliation with jewellery disclosed in earlier wealth tax returns may impose an unreasonable burden upon the assessee. ACIT Vs Sunil Kumar Khemka (ITAT Kolkata); IT(SS)A No. 99/Kol/2016; 02/08/2017: Relied upon to recognise customary remaking of jewellery, receipt of jewellery as gifts on auspicious occasions and the relevance of CBDT Instruction No. 1916 in determining explained jewellery holdings. CIT Vs Satya Narain Pami (Rajasthan High Court); (2014) 366 ITR 325 (Raj): Referred to in the decision in Sunil Kumar Khemka for the principle that jewellery within the limits prescribed by CBDT Instruction No. 1916 should ordinarily not be questioned regarding its source, having regard to Indian customs and traditions. DCIT Vs Arjun Dass Kalwani (ITAT Jodhpur); (2006) 101 ITD 337 (Jodhpur); AY: 1991-92: Referred to in the decision in Sunil Kumar Khemka for the proposition that absence of evidence regarding conversion or remaking of previously accepted jewellery does not, by itself, justify an unexplained investment addition. CIT Vs Dinesh Jain HUF (Delhi High Court); 352 ITR 629 (Del): Relied upon by the assessee but distinguished by the Tribunal on facts because excess physical jewellery had actually been discovered during the search and its source remained to be explained. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT DELHI 1. The appeals in ITA No. 2520/Del/2022 filed by the assessee and ITA 2831/Del/2022 filed by the revenue for AY 2019-20, arises out of the order of the ld. Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals)-27, New Delhi [hereinafter referred to as ‘ld. CIT(A)’, in short] dated 21.09.2022 against the order of assessment passed u/s 143(3) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Act’) dated 14.06.2021 by the Assessing Officer, ACIT/ DCIT, Central Circle-19, New Delhi (hereinafter referred to as ‘ld. AO’). As these are cross appeals, they are taken up together and disposed of by this common order for the sake of convenience. 2. The Ground Nos. 1 and 3 raised by the assessee are general in nature and does not require any specific adjudication. 3. The Ground No. 2 raised by assessee is challenging the confirmation of addition of ₹38,60,667 on account of unexplained jewellery under section 69B of the Act. The grounds raised by the revenue are challenging the deletion of addition made on account of unexplained jewellery by the learned CITA. As these are cross appeals on an identical issue of addition made on account of unexplained jewellery, they are taken up together. 4. We have heard the rival submissions and perused the materials available on record. A search and seizure action under section 132 of the Act was conducted on 19/1/2019 in the case of Gautam Khaitan group of companies at various residences and office premises. The assessee is the widow of late O P Khaitan and mother of Gautam Khaitan. The jewellery belonging to the assessee weighing 5066.69 grams was seized from the residence on 19/1/2019 and 9092.56 grams from bank locker on 21/1/2019. On 29/8/2019 the assessee filed the return of income declaring taxable income of ₹1,57,590 for the assessment year 2019-20. Notice under section 153A of the Act was issued to the assessee on 23/9/2020. In the search assessment proceedings, the assessee was asked to explain the source of jewellery for Rs 7,64,46,500 found from the residence and bank locker of the assessee. Prior to the search, the assessee had filed her wealth tax return on 18/9/2015 for assessment year 2015-16 declaring the jewellery weighing 11198.25 grams which was the last available wealth tax return in view of the repeal of Wealth Tax Act, 1957 with effect from 1/4/2016. Subsequent to filing of wealth tax return, the assessee purchased jewellery weighing 130.60 grams on 20/12/2017 and 17.95 grams on 19/1/2018 through invoices. After taking into consideration the wealth tax return and the invoices for purchase of jewellery, the learned AO issued showcause notice dated 31/5/2021 seeking explanation of jewellery weighing 558.74 grams worth ₹2,71,57,132 and as to why the same should not be considered as unexplained jewellery and added to the total income for assessment year 2019-20 under section 69B of the Act. 5. The assessee provided an explanation that the balance jewellery of 558.74 grams may have been received as gifts from family and friends on various occasions after Assessment Year 2015-16 and thus would not form part of the wealth tax return. It was also claimed that assessee having been married for over 60 years, gifts were exchanged on festivals and customary celebrations. Reliance was placed on CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11/5/1994 to claim that the balance 558.74 grams should be considered as explained. The learned AO made calculations of the explained and unexplained items of jewellery and concluded that remaining jewellery amounting to ₹38,01,414 as per Table I weighing a net weight of 1426.97 grams and ₹76,13,640 as per Table 2 weighing net weight of 467.95 grams totaling in weight to 1634.59 grams worth ₹1,14,15,054. Based on the valuation report of the learned Departmental Valuation Officer (DVO) of this remaining unexplained quantity of jewellery, the learned AO made addition under section 69B read with section 115BBE of the Act in the sum of ₹1,14,15,054. On appeal, the learned CITA partly allowed the appeal in terms of CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11/5/1994 restricting the addition to ₹38,60,667 as against ₹1,14,15,054 made by the learned AO on the basis of value of unexplained jewellery weighing 770.90 grams at an average value of ₹5008 per gram. Both assessee as well as the revenue preferred appeals before this Tribunal. The Tribunal in the first round of proceedings vide order dated 13/6/2023 allowed the appeal of the revenue and dismissed the appeal of the assessee. The assessee preferred an appeal before the Hon’ble Jurisdictional Delhi High Court by raising the following question of law :- “Whether the impugned addition under section 69B of the Act is legally sustainable on account of the respondent’s failure to discharge primary statutory onus / jurisdictional facts there under (i.e. amount expended on jewellery exceeded recorded in books) and its sole reliance on the Departmental Valuation Officer’s report”. 6. The Hon’ble High Court restored the question of law to the file of this Tribunal with direction to Tribunal to decide the issues raised by both parties afresh in accordance with law. Hence the present set of proceedings before us. 7. The learned DR before us vehemently argued that CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11/5/1994 talks only about seizure of jewellery and that no part of such jewellery could be construed as emanating from explained sources. Further he argued that the jewellery disclosed in the wealth tax return did not match with the physical jewellery found at the time of search. Further the learned DR also argued that there is absolutely no evidence brought on record by the assessee to prove that assessee was in receipt of gifts from relatives on various festivals and customary occasions after assessment year 2015-16. Further the returned income declared by the assessee in her income tax returns does not commensurate with the purchase of jewellery, if any. Per Contra, the learned AR placed reliance on the coordinate bench decision of Kolkata Tribunal in the case of DCIT vs Raj Kumar Saraogi in ITA No. 1776 / kol / 2012 and IT (SS) A No. 97 / kol / 2013 dated 1/9/2015. The relevant operative portion of the said tribunal order is reproduced here under:- “6. We have considered the submissions of both the parties. Ld. CIT(A) has observed that gold jewellery of gross weight 7277.080 grams (net weight: 6374.056 1005.010 carats of diamonds was duly explained in the hands of assessee and his family members. Further, assessee surrendered gold jewellery of gross weight 3164.160 (net weight 2495.62 gms) and 199.670 carats of diamond in course of search. Consequently, gold jewellery of gross weight 10441.240 gms (net weight 8869.718 gms) and 1204.680 carat of diamond was explained in the hands of assessee and his family members which is not disputed. On the other hand, gold jewellery of gross weight 8905.410 grams (net weight 7655.910 gms) and 968.230 carats of diamond was found at the time of search. These basic details are not disputed/controverted in any manner by the Department. The only dispute was that assessee was not being able to reconcile item-wise jewellery which were found at the time of search vis-à-vis the wealth tax and purchases made during the financial year by assessee. In this regard, we are in agreement with the findings of Ld. CIT(A) that items of jewellery are often subjected to re-making on account of changing fashion and designs. In Indian society, the yellow metal and diamond has assumed lot of significance for ladies which is a status symbol and they buy it or convert the jewellery as per the prevailing fashion. There has no gainsaying that fashion keeps on changing and, accordingly, the jewellery is re-modeled from time to time according to the prevailing fashion. Under the facts and circumstances, comparison with the items of jewellery found at the time of search with wealth tax return, which were filed much earlier was putting an onerous task on assessee to prove something impossible, and assessee cannot be as bed to prove something which is beyond its control. We accordingly, confirm the order of Ld. CIT(A) and this issue of Revenue’s appeals are dismissed.” 8. The learned AR also placed reliance on the coordinate bench decision of Kolkata Tribunal in the case of ACIT vs Sunil Kumar Khemka in IT(SS) A No. 99/Kol/2016 dated 2/8/2017. The relevant operative portion of the said tribunal order is reproduced here under:- “11. We have heard the rival submissions and perused the materials available on record. We find that the Ld. CIT(A) had given a categorical finding that jewellery is duly disclosed by the assessee’s wife and his mother to the tune of 1804.678 gms in VDIS 1997 and therefore, for want of item wise reconciliation of the same. no addition thereon could be made pursuant to the search conducted in the premises of the assessee. We hold that jewellery being a fashion industry, it is very likely that the household people frequently resort to changing of the same to be in tune with the latest fashion trends by either conversion or remaking of the same and hence the same would fall under prevailing domestic, customary and habitual practices. This fact has been duly appreciated by the several judicial forums and had not suggested any addition towards jewellery as long as the weight in grams is satisfied by the assessee. With regard to the remaining gms of 217.822 (2022.50-1804.678 gms), it was explained that 77.09 gms has already been offered by the assessee and remaining jewellery were received as gifts by the two unmarried daughters on various auspicious occasions. We find that it is quite common that jewellery being received by the children on various auspicious occasions and more so by the females depending upon their customs and habits. We also find that the said jewellery is well within the limits prescribed under CBDT instruction No. 1916 dated 11.05.1994, which though issued for the purpose of seizure, should also be extended to the fact of same jewellery being explained by the assessee. Hence, no addition in this regard is warranted as long as the said jewellery is within the limits prescribed by the CBDT instruction supra. In this regard we placed reliance on the following decisions: a) Decision of Hon’ble Rajasthan High Court in the case of CIT vs Satya Narain Pami reported in (2014) 366 ITR 325 (Raj) wherein it was held that:- 12. It is true that the circular of the CBDT, referred to supra dt. 11/05/1994 only refers to the jewellery to the extent of 500 gms per married lady, 250 gms per munarried lady and 100 gms per male member of the family, need not be seized and it does not speak about the questioning of the said jewellery from the person who has been found with possession of the said jewellery. However, the Board. looking to the Indian customs and traditions, has fairly expressed that jewellery to the said extent will not be seized and once the Board is also of the express opinion that the said jewellery cannot be seized, it should normally mean that cary jewellery, found in possesion of a married lady to the extent of 500 gms, 250 gus per unmarried lady and 100 gms per male member of the family will also not he questioned about its source and acquisition. We can take notice of the fact that at the time of wedding, the daughter/daughter-in-law receives gold ornaments jewellery and other goods not only from parental side but in-laws side as well at the time of ‘Vidai (farewell) or/and at the time when the daughter-in-law enters the house of her husband. We can also take notice of the fact that thereafter also. she continues to receive some small items by various other close friends and relatives of both the sides as well as on the auspicious occasion of birth of a child whether male or female and the CBDT. looking to such customs prevailing throughout India, in one way or the another, came out with this Circular and we accordingly are of the firm opinion that it should also mean that to the extent of the aforesaid jewellery, found in possession of the varoius persons, even source cannot be questioned. It is certainly Stridhan’ of the woman and normally no question at least to the said extent can be made. However, if the authorized officers or/and the Assessing Officers, find jewellery beyond the said weight, then certainly they can question the source of acquisition of the jewellery and also in appropriate cases, if no proper explanation has been offered, can treat the jewellery beyond the said limit as unexplained investment of the person with whom the said jewellery has been found. 13. Admittedly, looking to the status of the family and the jewellery found in possesssion of four ladies, was held to be reasonable and therefore, the authorized officers, in the first instance, did not seize the said jewellery as the same being within the tolerable limit or the limits prescribed by the Board and thus, in our view, subsequent addition is also not justificable on the part of the Assessing Officer and rightly deleted by both the two appellate authorities namely’ CIT(A) as well as the Tribunal. 14. It can also be observed here that prior to 1992, when the exemption limit under the Wealth Tax Act was about Rs. 1,00,000/- or Rs. 1,50,000/-, then in most of the cases, returns were filed under the Wealth Tax Act because even in case of possession of 500 gms per lady and the other assets namely; capital, investments in firms/shares, landed property etc. etc. being taxable return of wealth were invariably filed by the assessees. However, by the Finance Act. 1992 w.ef. 01/04/1993 drastic change was introduced under the Wealth Tax Act where only some assets u/s 2(ea) came within the purview of the definition of an “Asset” under the wealth tax and by and large, the other assets namely; liquid, capital investments in firms/shares, one house property, commercial assets were exempt and even the limit of other assets was raised to 15 lacs (for the Assessment Year 1993-94 to 2009-10) and thereafter, by and large, even the assessees, who were furnishing returns prior to 01/04/1992, in view of the drastic amendment made under the Wealth Tax Act, chose not to file wealth tax return as there was no liability for furnishing wealth tax returns. That does not mean that whatever assets were there in their possession, not disclosed under the Wealth Tax Act. remained undisclosed. May be, later on, on account of increase in the gold/silver prices, value of gems/ stones, value of jewellery may have exceeded but that does not mean that if a person has not filed wealth tax return, then jewellery even to the said extent of 500 gms prescribed by the aforesaid circular, became undisclosed. Admittedly, it is not the case of the revenue that the jewellery, so found, which has been prescribed hereinabove, was not admitted by the family members at the time of search. All the ladies in the family admitted that the jewellery found were all their own and some of the jewellery was lying in custody and control of their mother-in-law and in Indian conditions, it happens that the doughter-in-law keeps her jewellery with her mother-in-law orfand head of the family and takes the same whenever required for some occasion in the family. Even otherwise, the jewellery is personal wearing in nature and the revenue has not placed any material on record to show that the items, which were found, weir not personal wearing of the ladies. 15. Considering the above facts and circumstances, in our view, the Tribunal has correctly analyzed the Circular of the Board and we do not find any infirmity or perversity in the order of the ITAT so as to call for any interference of this Court. In our view, no substantial question of law arise out of the order passed by the ΠΑΤ. b) Decision of co-ordinate bench of Jodhpur Tribunal in the case of DCIT vs Arjun Dass Kalwani reported in (2006) 101 ITD 337 (Jodhpur) – The head notes thereon are reproduced as under:- Section 69 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 Unexplained investments Assessment year 1991-92-Consequent upon a search action taken on assessee’s residential premises, certain quantity of gold ornaments was found in his possession When confronted, he explained with evidence that same belonged to his wife, daughter-in-law and two married daughters, and that except his wife, each of them held less than 500 grams of total quantity As regards his wife, he produced list of inventory of gold ornaments prepared on 20-12-1983 during earlier search and tax returns where revenue had assessed his wife at much more jewellery than seized-Assessing Officer, however, treated almost entire quantity of said gold as unexplained on ground that items in list dated 20-12-1983 did not tally with items found in instant search and assessee could not adduce any evidence with regard to remaking or conversion of old gold ornaments Whether simply because assessee could not lead evidence for conversion or remaking of jewellery, possession of which was otherwise accepted, it could be said that holding of gold jewellery to that extent could not be accepted Held, no Whether since assessee’s daughter-in-law and married daughters were in possession of gold jewellery, which was mich below CBDT’s prescribed limit of 500 grams, no addition could be sustained on that account Held, yes Whether, therefore. Commissioner (Appeals) was justified in deleting impugned addition – Held, yes In view of the aforesaid facts and respectful following the aforesaid judicial precedents, we find no infirmity in the order of the Ld. CIT(A) in this regard. Accordingly, ground nos. 3 and 4 raised by the Revenue are dismissed.” 9. We find that the Hon’ble Delhi High Court had already held that there was a typographical error in the order of the learned CITA and the addition made on account of unexplained jewellery need to be construed as having made under section 69B of the Act. Hence there cannot be any dispute on this at this juncture. 11. We find that the income declared by the assessee subsequent to Assessment Year 2015-16 is not commensurate enough to explain the purchase of jewellery, if any, made by the assessee. Hence no credit could be given to the assessee for alleged purchase of jewellery after Assessment Year 2015-16, except to the extent of credit given by the learned CITA. The learned AR before us placed reliance on the decision of the Hon’ble Jurisdictional Delhi High Court in the case of CIT vs Dinesh Jain HUF reported in 352 ITR 629 (Del). We find that this decision is completely and factually distinguishable with the assessee herein. It is a fact that the jewellery in excess of what was disclosed in wealth tax return was found in the premises of the assessee and bank locker of the assessee at the time of search. The assessee is bound to explain the source of such jewellery dehors the maintenance of books of accounts by her. We find that the learned CITA had addressed the entire issue in dispute elaborately after giving credit for CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11/5/1994, Wealth tax return filed by the assessee, purchases made by the assessee subsequent to wealth tax return and jewellery received as inheritance from deceased husband of the assessee. After due consideration of all the relevant items, whatever remained unexplained by the assessee alone to the extent of 770.90 grams of jewellery alone were sought to be confirmed by the learned CITA. We do not find any infirmity in the said order of the learned CITA. Accordingly, the Ground No. 2 raised by the assessee is dismissed and grounds raised by the revenue are dismissed. 12. In the result, the appeal of the revenue is dismissed and appeal of the assessee is dismissed. Order pronounced in the open court on 18-Nov-2026.

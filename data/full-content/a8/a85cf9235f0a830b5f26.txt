Madras High Court sets aside a duplicate notice: A serious reminder on fairness and accountability in tax administration A taxpayer answers a GST notice. The Department accepts the reply and drops the proceedings. Nearly two years later, the same issue appears in another notice. The taxpayer replies again. While that reply is awaiting adjudication, a third notice is issued covering the identical issue, the same tax period and the exact same amount. This is the sequence recorded by the Madras High Court in Popular Mega Motors India Private Limited v. State of Tamil Nadu and Others. The disputed allegation concerned excess input tax credit of ₹56,17,77,372 for April 2022 to March 2023. The Court ultimately set aside the third notice because it duplicated the second notice, which was already pending adjudication. The case raises a straightforward question: how many times should a taxpayer be required to explain the same issue before the Department checks its own records? The concern is not whether the Department should examine an incorrect ITC claim. It certainly should act wherever the law permits and the facts justify action. The concern is whether that examination can take the form of repeated proceedings without proper verification of what has already been decided and what is already pending. 1. The decision at a glance These particulars and the scope of relief are recorded in the judgment. 2. How did the taxpayer receive notices three times? The chronology is important. Without it, the seriousness of the procedural failure may be missed. Date What happened 27 December 2023 A communication in Form GST DRC-01A was issued concerning the issue. 13 February 2024 The first show-cause notice, referred to with Form GST DRC-01, was issued. 27 August 2024 The proceedings were dropped after the taxpayer’s reply was accepted. The final judgment also records that this followed the taxpayer approaching the Court. 8 July 2026 A second show-cause notice was issued. The taxpayer submitted a reply, and the proceedings remained pending. 28 September 2026 A third show-cause notice was issued, duplicating the pending notice dated 8 July 2026. 27 October 2026 The High Court stayed the third notice and called for an explanation. 16 November 2026 The Court set aside the third notice. The above sequence is drawn from the interim order reproduced in the judgment and the Court’s final findings. The three show-cause notices were those dated 13 February 2024, 8 July 2026 and 28 September 2026; the earlier DRC-01A communication was a separate preliminary stage in the chronology. The first proceedings had been dropped. The second proceedings were awaiting a decision. Yet a third notice was issued. This was not merely a taxpayer alleging that two notices looked similar. The Department itself subsequently admitted the repetition before the Court. 3. The same officer had issued the earlier and the impugned notices One particularly troubling feature was noted in the Court’s interim order: the officer issuing the impugned notice and the earlier notice was the same. The Court recorded that the identical ITC issue, involving the exact same amount for the same period, was already under consideration before that officer. Nevertheless, the impugned notice was issued again. The Court therefore observed, at the interim stage: “Therefore, prima facie, it appears that the impugned notice has been issued with a complete non-application of mind.” The words “prima facie” must be retained when reporting this observation. They formed part of the interim assessment. The final order subsequently rested on the established duplication of proceedings. Even with that distinction, the administrative concern is substantial. Before issuing another notice, checking whether the same allegation is already pending before the issuing officer is a basic safeguard that taxpayers should reasonably expect. 4. What explanation did the Department give? Pursuant to the Court’s direction, the third respondent filed an explanation dated 16 November 2026. As recorded in paragraph 3 of the judgment, the respondent admitted that the impugned show-cause notice was a “triplication” of proceedings initiated for the very same cause of action. The respondent also stated that, because of the interim stay granted by the Court, the notice could not be withdrawn. That was the respondent’s explanation. It should not be confused with a judicial finding that an interim stay necessarily prevented withdrawal. More importantly, it did not explain why the third notice had been issued in the first place. A statement about difficulty in withdrawing a notice after a stay does not answer the earlier question: what verification took place before its issuance? The judgment does not identify whether the original cause was a portal problem, a clerical error, inadequate file verification or some other administrative lapse. It would therefore be unfair to invent a motive. What is established is the repetition itself, the respondent’s admission and the Court’s finding of duplication. 5. What did the High Court finally decide? The Court found that: The first notice dated 13 February 2024 had culminated in proceedings being dropped on 27 August 2024. The second notice dated 8 July 2026 had been contested by the taxpayer and remained pending. The third notice dated 28 September 2026 clearly duplicated the pending second notice. Accordingly, the Court set aside the notice dated 28 September 2026 and allowed the writ petition. No costs were awarded. The operative relief was precise. The Court removed the duplicate third proceeding. It did not decide the substantive eligibility of the entire ITC amount, nor did it set aside the second notice dated 8 July 2026. That proceeding was recorded as pending. Therefore, the decision should not be reported as cancellation of an adjudicated tax demand of ₹56.17 crore or as a final declaration that the entire ITC claim was valid. 6. More than nine years into GST, can basic file verification still be overlooked? More than nine years into the GST journey, taxpayers are entitled to expect mature administration—not merely electronic issuance of notices. In this case, the required check was not an elaborate investigation into a complicated transaction. It was a comparison of the Department’s own proceedings: Who is the taxpayer? What is the tax period? What is the allegation? What amount is involved? Has the issue already been decided? Is another notice on the same issue awaiting adjudication? The facts recorded by the Court show why these questions matter. The same period and identical ITC amount appeared repeatedly, and the third notice duplicated a proceeding already pending before the same officer. A digital system is useful only when the officer issuing the notice examines the relevant record. Generating a fresh reference number does not make an existing allegation a fresh dispute. That is the administrative lesson of this case. 7. What fairness should a bona fide taxpayer expect? A bona fide taxpayer does not seek protection from a lawful enquiry. The reasonable expectation is that the enquiry will be conducted carefully, that replies will be examined and that previous orders will not be ignored. Fairness should mean: A notice based on verified facts rather than an unchecked repetition. Consideration of the taxpayer’s earlier replies and documents. Examination of orders already passed on the issue. Identification of any existing proceeding covering the same allegation. A clear explanation of the legal basis for any further action. These are proposed standards of responsible administration. They are not additional directions issued by the Court in this judgment. The larger question is one of balance. If taxpayers are expected to reconcile their returns, maintain documents and respond within the prescribed time, the Department should apply comparable care to its own files before requiring another response. Procedural discipline cannot be a one-sided obligation. 8. Illustration: what repeated notices mean for a business Consider a hypothetical registered dealer, M/s ABC Motor Distributors. For financial year 2022-23, the Department alleges excess ITC of ₹40 lakh. The dealer submits an invoice-wise reconciliation and supporting records. Assume that the officer examines the reply, accepts the explanation and passes an order dropping the proceedings. Two years later, another notice repeats the same ₹40 lakh allegation for the same period. The dealer again submits the earlier notice, reply, reconciliation and closure order. Before that reply is decided, a third notice repeats the same allegation. The dealer must now distinguish between two separate concerns: Concern What requires examination Repetition after the earlier closure The earlier order and the legal basis, if any, for raising the issue again A fresh notice while another identical notice remains pending Whether the later notice duplicates the existing proceeding The immediate principle illustrated by Popular Mega Motors concerns the second situation: the High Court set aside the third notice because it duplicated the pending second notice. The judgment did not separately determine the validity of the second notice merely because an earlier proceeding had been dropped. For the hypothetical business, however, each additional notice means another round of document retrieval, reconciliation, professional review and management attention. These are the practical burdens illustrated by the example; the judgment does not quantify the petitioner’s expenditure or business loss. The taxpayer should not have to repeatedly establish the existence of the Department’s own earlier proceedings. 9. The criticism must be firm, but legally accurate This decision deserves serious criticism of the procedural lapse. However, the criticism should remain within what the record establishes. The Court did not find corruption, deliberate harassment or mala fides. It did not certify the taxpayer’s entire ITC claim as valid. Nor did it impose personal costs or disciplinary punishment on the officer. The interim order required the officer’s personal appearance only if a proper explanation or reply was not filed. The final judgment records that an explanation was filed; it does not record that the officer actually appeared in person. These distinctions do not weaken the criticism. They make it more credible. The established facts are serious enough: repeated proceedings, an identical pending allegation, an admission of triplication and judicial intervention to remove the duplicate notice. There is no need to exaggerate them. 10. What administrative correction is required? In the author’s view, a case of this nature should lead to correction beyond closure of the particular writ petition. A practical internal verification process could require the issuing officer to record: Check before issuing a notice Purpose Search earlier notices for the same GSTIN and period Identify existing proceedings Compare allegations and amounts Detect issue-wise duplication Examine earlier adjudication or closure orders Avoid overlooking a concluded proceeding Record the legal basis for further action Distinguish lawful fresh action from repetition Obtain review where an identical issue appears again Reduce avoidable notices and litigation These are suggested administrative safeguards, not directions contained in the judgment. Accountability should also be proportionate and evidence-based. A clerical error, a system defect and a repeated failure to examine records may require different responses. But an admitted duplicate notice should at least prompt an internal examination of how it happened and how recurrence will be prevented. Otherwise, the immediate notice disappears while the underlying problem remains. 11. Relief from the Court should not substitute for care within the Department The High Court provided effective relief by setting aside the duplicate notice. But the taxpayer had to approach the Court to obtain that correction. The final order granted no costs. This leaves an important policy question: should judicial intervention be necessary to establish that an identical proceeding is already pending before the issuing authority? Tax administration should aim to resolve such an obvious duplication before litigation becomes necessary. Protecting revenue and preventing avoidable proceedings are not competing objectives. Careful verification supports both. Conclusion Popular Mega Motors India Private Limited is an important reminder that issuance of a GST notice is an exercise of statutory responsibility, not a routine administrative formality. The Madras High Court set aside the third notice because it duplicated the second notice already pending adjudication. The respondent’s admission of triplication made the procedural failure particularly clear. For genuine taxpayers, the expectation is modest: examine the records, consider the reply, respect the status of earlier proceedings and do not require the same allegation to be answered repeatedly without a lawful basis. More than nine years into GST, these should be ordinary standards of administration—not safeguards that a taxpayer must obtain through a writ petition. FULL TEXT OF THE ORIGINAL JUDGMENT/ORDER The present Writ Petition has been filed challenging the show cause notice dated 28.09.2026 issued by the third respondent. 2. When the matter came up for hearing on 27.10.2026, this Court passed the following order:- “Challenge has been made against the show cause notice dated 28.09.2026 passed by the third respondent. 2. The issue in the present case, as raised in the impugned Show Cause Notice, pertains to a sum of Rs.56,17,77,372/- regarding an alleged excess claim of Input Tax Credit (ITC). For the very same issue, a Show Cause Notice in Form GST DRC-01A was previously issued on 27.12.2023, followed by a Show Cause Notice in Form GST DRC-01 dated 13.02.2024. After the petitioner filed their reply, the same was accepted by the respondents, and the proceedings were dropped vide an order dated 27.08.2024. Subsequent to the dropping of the said proceedings, the respondent once again issued a Show Cause Notice in Form GST DRC-01 on 08.07.2026 and 28.09.2026, raising the identical issue along with two other distinct issues. The petitioner has filed a detailed reply to the same, which is currently pending adjudication before the respondents. Notably, the officer who issued the present notice and the earlier notice is one and the same. Even though the identical issue covering the alleged excess claim of ITC to the extent of Rs.56,17,77,372/- for the same period from April 2022 to March 2023 is already under consideration before the said officer, viz., Parthiban Thangavelu, the present Show Cause Notice has been issued for the exact same figure. Therefore, prima facie, it appears that the impugned notice has been issued with a complete non-application of mind. 3. In view of the above, there shall be an order of interim stay of operation of impugned show cause notice dated 28.09.2026. 4. List the case on 16.11.2026. In the meantime, the respondents are directed to file a reply. In the event that a proper explanation or reply is not filed by the respondents, the concerned officer, viz., Parthiban Thangavelu, who issued the Show Cause Notice, shall appear in person before this Court on the date of hearing, i.e., 16.11.2026. 3. Pursuant to the earlier order of this Court, the third respondent filed an explanation/reply dated 16.11.2026 today, admitting that the impugned show cause notice is a triplication of the proceedings initiated for the very same cause of action. It is further stated therein that, in view of the interim stay granted by this Court on 27.10.2026, the third respondent was unable to withdraw the impugned show cause notice dated 28.09.2026 for the tax period from April, 2022, to March, 2023. 4. Heard the learned counsel for the petitioner and the learned Government Advocate (Taxes) appearing for the respondent, and perused the materials on record. 5. On a perusal of the records, it is seen that the first show cause notice was issued on 13.02.2024, which was subsequently dropped on 27.08.2024 after the petitioner approached this Court. Thereafter, a second show cause notice was issued on 08.07.2026, which the petitioner has contested by filing a reply, and the said proceedings are currently pending. However, the third respondent issued the impugned third show cause notice dated 28.09.2026, which is a clear duplication of the pending show cause notice dated 08.07.2026. 6. In view of the above, this Court is inclined to set aside the impugned show cause notice dated 28.09.2026, as it is a duplication of the earlier show cause notice dated 08.07.2026, and accordingly, the same is hereby set aside. Accordingly, this Writ Petition stands allowed. There shall be no order as to costs. W.M.P. No. 48846 of 2026, filed to dispense with the production of the original impugned show cause notice dated 28.09.2026, is allowed. Consequently, the connected Miscellaneous Petition is closed.

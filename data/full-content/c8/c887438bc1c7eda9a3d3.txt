Summary: A personal or corporate guarantor for a business loan may face recovery proceedings when the principal borrower defaults, even if the lender holds security over the borrower’s assets. Under Sections 126 and 128 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a guarantor’s liability is ordinarily co-extensive with that of the borrower, unless the guarantee agreement provides otherwise. The amount recoverable depends on the terms of the deed, any monetary limit, interest and costs covered, and amounts already recovered by the lender. The Supreme Court’s decision in Bank of Bihar Ltd. v. Damodar Prasad establishes that a creditor generally need not exhaust remedies against the principal debtor before proceeding against the guarantor. However, invocation requirements, limitation, variation of loan terms, release of security, misrepresentation and other statutory defences can affect enforceability. Lenders may pursue recovery through the appropriate civil, DRT, SARFAESI or insolvency processes, subject to the relevant statutory conditions. The article also explains a guarantor’s rights of subrogation, reimbursement and contribution from co-guarantors, and examines the effect of resignation, revocation, death and corporate insolvency on existing guarantees. Significant developments under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026 include the removal, from 26 May 2026, of the automatic Section 96 interim moratorium for personal guarantors to corporate debtors. Separately, applicable RBI directions permit classification of guarantors as wilful defaulters where specified conditions are established through the prescribed procedure. Practical checklists and FAQs help guarantors evaluate demand notices, determine their actual exposure, preserve contractual defences and review settlement options. This can come as a surprise to a director who signed a personal guarantee years ago, a promoter who has since sold their shares, or a family member who offered property as security. The extent of their exposure depends on what they signed, which debts the documents cover, and how the lender enforces its rights. Consider Meera, the promoter of a manufacturing company that borrowed ₹50 lakh through a working capital facility. She gave a personal guarantee capped at ₹30 lakh. Two years later, the company lost a major customer and missed several repayments. The bank still held security over the company’s machinery, but it sent Meera a demand notice. Could it ask her to pay before selling the machinery? Generally, yes. Whether it can claim the full ₹30 lakh depends on the guarantee deed, the outstanding balance and the terms of the demand. What is a guarantor in a business loan? Under Section 126 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a guarantee is a promise to perform an obligation or discharge a liability if another person defaults. In a business loan, the lender is the creditor, the business is the principal debtor, and the individual or entity giving the guarantee is the surety, commonly called the guarantor. A personal guarantee creates a payment obligation for the individual who gives it. A corporate guarantee creates an obligation for the company that gives it, often in support of a subsidiary or another group company. A third-party mortgage gives the lender security over a specific asset. An individual can sign both a personal guarantee and a mortgage, so the documents need to be read together to understand the lender’s remedies. A director who signs a loan agreement on behalf of the company may have a different obligation from a director who also signs a personal guarantee. How much can a lender recover from a guarantor? Under Section 128 of the Indian Contract Act, a guarantor’s liability is generally co-extensive with that of the borrower, unless the contract provides otherwise. In practice, this means the lender can claim the unpaid debt covered by the guarantee, which may include principal, contractual interest and specified recovery costs. A guarantee can limit liability to a fixed amount, a particular facility, or specified transactions. Where there is a monetary cap, the wording determines whether interest and costs fall within that cap or can be added separately. Suppose Meera’s ₹30 lakh cap expressly includes interest and costs. If the covered debt is ₹38 lakh, her liability under that deed is capped at ₹30 lakh. If the bank subsequently recovers ₹15 lakh from the sale of machinery and credits it to the same debt, the balance falls to ₹23 lakh, assuming no further interest or costs accrue. The ₹30 lakh cap sets the maximum exposure. The lender’s actual claim depends on the covered amount still outstanding. Can the bank recover from the guarantor before pursuing the borrower? Generally, yes. In Bank of Bihar Ltd. v. Damodar Prasad, the Supreme Court held that a creditor need not exhaust its remedies against the principal borrower before proceeding against the guarantor. A lender can therefore demand payment from a guarantor even when it holds the borrower’s assets as security. It must still comply with any conditions in the guarantee deed, such as serving a written demand or allowing a specified period for payment. A lender may pursue available remedies against both the borrower and the guarantor, but recoveries against the same debt must be credited to the outstanding balance. If a borrower, co-guarantor or insolvency resolution applicant has already paid part of the dues, the guarantor should compare the demand with the loan account statement and ask how the lender applied those payments. The Supreme Court addressed the adjustment of recoveries in BRS Ventures Investments Ltd. v. SREI Infrastructure Finance Ltd. How does a lender invoke a business loan guarantee? Invocation is the lender’s demand that the guarantor pay under the guarantee. A loan recall notice to the borrower, an invocation notice to the guarantor, and a statutory notice to enforce security can serve different purposes. If the deed specifies how notice must be served, what it must contain or how long the guarantor has to pay, those terms can affect when the lender may enforce the guarantee. The date of invocation can also affect limitation. In Syndicate Bank v. Channaveerappa Beleri, the Supreme Court explained that where a guarantee is payable on demand, limitation can begin when a valid demand is made and the guarantor fails to comply. The relevant date depends on the guarantee’s wording, the facts surrounding the demand and the recovery procedure the lender uses. The borrower’s first missed instalment, the recall date and the guarantor’s default date may differ. Depending on the lender and the documents, enforcement may proceed before the Debts Recovery Tribunal (DRT), through a civil suit, or through arbitration where an applicable arbitration agreement binds the guarantor. A creditor may also initiate insolvency proceedings under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) against a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor, subject to the applicable requirements. Can the lender attach or sell the guarantor’s personal property? Where a guarantor mortgaged property to secure the business loan, an eligible secured creditor may enforce that security under the SARFAESI Act, 2002, where the Act applies. The usual process under Section 13(2) includes a 60-day demand notice before the lender takes enforcement measures under Section 13(4). The recipient can raise objections under Section 13(3A). Once the lender takes a measure under Section 13(4), an aggrieved person can generally apply to the DRT under Section 17 within 45 days of the measure. If the guarantor gave only a personal guarantee, the lender has a claim for payment under that guarantee. To attach or sell a property that was never mortgaged or otherwise charged to it, the lender would ordinarily need to follow the applicable judicial or statutory enforcement process. The distinction matters when a notice identifies a particular home, commercial property or other asset. The security documents establish whether the lender already has a charge over that asset. When can a guarantor challenge or reduce liability? The Indian Contract Act gives guarantors several grounds to challenge a demand. Whether a defence applies depends on the deed, the lender’s conduct and the evidence available. Ground How it can affect liability Change in loan terms without consent — Section 133 An unauthorised variation in the borrower’s contract can discharge the guarantor for transactions after the change. The deed may already contain consent to specified renewals, extensions or enhancements. Release of the borrower — Section 134 A contractual release of the borrower by the creditor, or certain acts or omissions that legally discharge the borrower, can also discharge the guarantor. Statutory insolvency resolution is treated differently. Compromise or extension of time — Section 135 A binding agreement with the borrower to compromise the debt, give time or refrain from suing can discharge the guarantor unless the guarantor assents. Delay in taking action — Section 137 Mere delay or forbearance by the lender generally leaves the guarantee enforceable, subject to applicable limitation rules. Impairment of remedies or loss of security — Sections 139 and 141 A lender’s conduct that impairs the guarantor’s eventual remedy can support a defence. If the lender loses or gives up qualifying security without consent, liability can be reduced to the extent of the value of that security. Misrepresentation or concealment — Sections 142 and 143 A guarantee may be invalid if obtained through a material misrepresentation by the lender or with its knowledge and assent, or through the lender’s concealment of material circumstances. Failure of a required co-guarantor to join — Section 144 A guarantee can be invalid if the lender agreed it would take effect only when another person joined as co-guarantor and that person never joined. For example, a director may guarantee a ₹20 lakh facility that the bank later increases to ₹40 lakh. Whether the guarantee covers advances after the increase depends on the deed, the director’s consent and any liability cap. The original sanction letter, the revised sanction letter and any confirmation signed by the guarantor can help establish what the parties agreed. Does resignation, revocation or death end a guarantee? A director’s resignation or a promoter’s sale of shares generally leaves an existing personal guarantee in force. An agreement between the outgoing director and the new owners to arrange a replacement may create rights between them. The lender’s written release establishes whether the bank has agreed to give up its claim against the outgoing guarantor and from what date. A continuing guarantee covers a series of transactions, such as repeated drawdowns under a working capital facility. Under Section 130, a guarantor may revoke a continuing guarantee for future transactions by giving notice to the creditor. The effect of revocation depends on the deed and the facility arrangements, and exposure for transactions already covered can remain. Under Section 131, a guarantor’s death generally revokes a continuing guarantee for future transactions unless the contract provides otherwise. Liability arising from earlier covered transactions may still be enforceable against the deceased guarantor’s estate. What rights does a guarantor have after paying the lender? Right to step into the lender’s position Under Section 140, a guarantor who pays or performs everything for which they are liable generally acquires the lender’s rights against the borrower. This is called subrogation. Section 141 also gives the guarantor the benefit of qualifying security the lender held against the borrower when the guarantee was given. Where there has been a partial recovery or a settlement, the amount paid and the lender’s remaining rights can affect what the guarantor can enforce. Right to recover the amount from the borrower Under Section 145, the borrower has an implied obligation to reimburse the guarantor for amounts rightfully paid under the guarantee. A guarantor who pays should retain the invocation notice, proof of payment, the statement showing how the lender applied the payment, and any discharge letter. These documents help establish the reimbursement claim. Actual recovery will depend on the borrower’s financial position and any applicable insolvency restrictions. Right to seek contribution from co-guarantors Under Sections 146 and 147, co-guarantors for the same debt generally contribute equally among themselves, subject to any agreement between them and the individual limits of their guarantees. If two co-guarantors have equal liability and one pays the entire ₹12 lakh due, that guarantor may ordinarily seek ₹6 lakh from the other. The lender’s right to claim from either guarantor depends on the guarantee deeds. What happens to a guarantee when the borrower enters insolvency? The corporate borrower’s moratorium under Section 14 of the IBC generally allows the lender to continue pursuing the guarantor, subject to any protection applicable in the guarantor’s own proceedings. The Supreme Court confirmed this principle in State Bank of India v. V. Ramakrishnan. In Lalit Kumar Jain v. Union of India, the Court also held that approval of a corporate borrower’s resolution plan does not automatically discharge a personal guarantor. The approved plan, the guarantee and the lender’s actual recoveries determine what remains enforceable. The IBC Amendment Act, 2026 also clarified that the borrower’s Section 14 moratorium applies when a guarantor seeks to start or continue proceedings against the corporate debtor under the guarantee. After a resolution plan is approved, the amended Section 31 generally extinguishes pre-approval claims against the corporate debtor and its assets unless the plan provides otherwise. A guarantor seeking reimbursement should therefore check how the insolvency process and the plan treat that claim. What changed for personal guarantors in 2026? From 26 May 2026, Section 96(4) of the IBC excludes personal guarantors to corporate debtors from the automatic interim moratorium that previously arose when an insolvency resolution application was filed. Filing an application under Section 94 or 95 therefore does not by itself pause recovery proceedings under Section 96. The IBBI’s September 2026 circular addressed the cessation of the filing-stage moratorium for applications that were pending on 26 May 2026. If the tribunal admits the guarantor’s application under Section 100, the moratorium under Section 101 can apply from the date of admission. Can a guarantor be classified as a wilful defaulter? Under the RBI’s Treatment of Wilful Defaulters and Large Defaulters Directions, 2024, a covered lender may classify a guarantor as a wilful defaulter in specified circumstances. One ground is failure to honour an invoked guarantee despite having sufficient means to pay. Other grounds include unauthorised disposal of assets securing the facility or failure to honour an equity infusion commitment despite having the ability to do so, where the prescribed conditions are met. The directions specify an outstanding threshold of ₹25 lakh or above, subject to any revised threshold notified by the RBI. Classification requires a prescribed process. The lender must issue a show-cause notice, disclose the material it relies on and give the guarantor an opportunity to respond. A guarantor receiving such a notice should address the specific grounds raised and support the response with relevant records, such as evidence of financial capacity, asset ownership or the lender’s consent to a transaction. What should a guarantor check after receiving a demand notice? What to check Why it matters Guarantee deed and amendments Confirm which facilities are covered, the liability cap and any consent given to changes in loan terms. Loan statement and calculation of dues Check principal, interest, costs and credits from repayments, settlements or asset sales. Default, recall and invocation dates Identify the response deadline and assess when the lender’s claim became enforceable. Mortgage and release documents Establish which assets were charged and whether the lender later released or substituted any security. Restructuring or insolvency records Check how the borrower’s settlement or resolution plan treats the debt and the guarantee. Proposed settlement terms Confirm whether payment gives the guarantor a full discharge, releases any security and closes pending proceedings. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) Q1. Is a guarantor liable for the entire business loan? Ans. A guarantor can be liable for the entire unpaid debt covered by the guarantee. Any monetary cap, facility restriction or other contractual limit applies according to the wording of the deed. Q2. Can a bank demand payment from a guarantor before selling the borrower’s assets? Ans. Yes. A lender can generally proceed directly against the guarantor, subject to the terms of the guarantee and applicable law. Q3. Is a company director personally liable for a loan signed on behalf of the company? Ans. Signing in a representative capacity generally binds the company. The director can incur personal liability by giving a personal guarantee or making another enforceable commitment in their individual capacity. Q4. Does a personal guarantee cover interest and recovery costs? Ans. It can. Check whether the deed covers those amounts and whether the liability cap includes them or permits specified amounts to be added separately. Q5. Can the bank sell a guarantor’s house if the house was never mortgaged? Ans. An unsecured personal guarantee alone does not give the bank a security interest over the house. Attachment or sale would ordinarily require the applicable judicial or statutory enforcement process, subject to any legal protections. Q6. Can a guarantor withdraw a continuing guarantee? Ans. A continuing guarantee can generally be revoked for future transactions by giving notice to the lender under Section 130. The terms of the deed and the timing of the relevant transactions determine what liability remains. Q7. Does a one-time settlement with the borrower release the guarantor? Ans. It depends on the settlement terms, the guarantor’s consent and the applicable law. The guarantor should check whether the lender has expressly released the guarantee or reserved its rights against them. Q8. Can a guarantor recover money from the borrower after paying the bank? Ans. Yes. Section 145 allows a guarantor to seek reimbursement for amounts rightfully paid. Recovery can be affected by the borrower’s financial position and any insolvency moratorium or approved resolution plan. Q9. Does filing a personal guarantor insolvency application stop recovery proceedings? Ans. From 26 May 2026, filing alone does not trigger the Section 96 interim moratorium for a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor. A moratorium under Section 101 can apply once the tribunal admits the application. Q10. Can a guarantor be declared a wilful defaulter simply because the borrower defaulted? Ans. The borrower’s default alone is insufficient. A covered lender must establish the applicable grounds for classification against the guarantor and follow the RBI’s prescribed notice and review process.

From India to Spain: What is driving protests around the world?

From disputes over education funding and rising living costs to electoral processes and government spending, protests across the world have brought students, workers, activists and opposition supporters onto the streets.

In France, Belgium, Albania, Argentina, Tunisia and India, demonstrators have raised concerns ranging from public services and political accountability to the fairness of government decisions. Some rallies have escalated into clashes with police, while protests elsewhere have focused on housing affordability.

Though the triggers vary, the demonstrations highlight a common point of tension: how governments make decisions, who bears their costs and whether citizens believe their concerns are being heard.

India’s Voter-List Dispute Draws Protests

In India, opposition parties and young protesters are demanding accountability from the Election Commission over a revision of voter lists that critics allege could benefit Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) and disproportionately affect minorities. The Election Commission and the BJP deny wrongdoing, saying the exercise is intended to remove duplicate and ineligible entries.

According to reports, more than 130 million names have been removed from voter lists since the revision began last year. The Election Commission’s figures include people recorded as deceased, those who have relocated and individuals whose whereabouts could not be established.

Opposition leaders and supporters of the youth-led “Cockroach” movement have demanded the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. Opposition demonstrators marched to the Supreme Court in New Delhi on October 12, seeking an audit of voter-list deletions, safeguards against removals without notice and an interim suspension of the revision, according to Reuters. The petition included opposition leader Rahul Gandhi among its signatories.

Reuters reported that authorities detained thousands of protesters as police blocked a planned rally. The United Nations human rights office also criticised the detentions, while the government said restrictions were necessary to maintain public order.

The “Cockroach” movement emerged from an online satirical campaign and became a rallying point for youth-led protests. The Election Commission has said eligible voters whose names were wrongly removed can re-enrol, while the BJP has accused the opposition of undermining confidence in electoral institutions.

France Protests Expand Beyond Education

In France, protests that began last month among high-school students in the country’s northern suburbs have expanded into demonstrations over inequality, public services and the cost of living, according to Reuters.

Students initially raised concerns about teacher shortages, overcrowded classrooms and deteriorating school facilities. Their grievances subsequently broadened to include public spending and high fuel prices.

The French Interior Ministry counted more than 161,000 demonstrators on October 13, although participation was lower than a week earlier. Tens of thousands took to the streets again on October 17, with clashes reported in several cities.

By mid-afternoon, 272 people had been arrested, the Interior Ministry said. Police used tear gas in parts of Paris, while shops and other property were damaged. In Lyon, demonstrators set fire to the tourist office, while Marseille’s mayor said the town hall had been vandalised, Reuters reported.

The Interior Ministry blamed what it described as the “ultra-left” for the unrest. Protesters, meanwhile, said most participants were peaceful and blamed troublemakers for the destruction.

The protests have also brought scrutiny of police conduct. A police watchdog is investigating at least two dozen allegations of police violence, Reuters reported. These include an incident in which a teenager lost a hand after picking up a stun grenade. French authorities have faced criticism over the use of such devices during demonstrations.

Belgium Students And Workers Oppose Spending Cuts

In Belgium, trade unions and students have protested against government spending cuts and proposed education reforms.

Unions argue that the measures would place a disproportionate burden on workers, pensioners and low-income households. Prime Minister Bart De Wever has defended spending restraint as necessary to contain public debt and interest costs.

Student groups have separately raised concerns that rising education costs and proposed reforms could make higher education less accessible.

A demonstration on October 9 brought together workers and students opposed to the cuts. Several hundred mostly young people also gathered in Brussels on October 17, where some clashed with police. Officers used tear gas and water cannon, while some demonstrators overturned a car, according to a Reuters witness.

Interior Minister Bernard Quintin said authorities would use all necessary means to restore order. Protests also took place in Liège, where demonstrators raised concerns about the French-language education system, costs and study materials, according to Reuters.

The demonstrations reflect concerns over how the government’s spending restraint could affect workers, pensioners and students, particularly those who depend on public services.

Albania’s Student Protests Turn Into Wider Anti-Government Demonstrations

In Albania, students initially took to the streets over university conditions, education costs, financial support and employment prospects after graduation. Their demands have widened to include allegations of corruption, inequality and calls for Prime Minister Edi Rama to resign.

On October 13, protesters in the capital, Tirana, damaged public buildings and banks and clashed with police, Reuters reported. Police responded with tear gas and water cannon, while businesses were damaged.

On October 17, thousands of young people gathered outside the Education Ministry in Tirana for a fifth day of demonstrations, Reuters reported. The agency said some masked protesters threw rocks at riot police, who responded with stun grenades, tear gas and water cannon.

Police figures cited by Reuters put the number of protesters detained at 500, with 330 subsequently released. Police recorded 120 officers and 45 protesters as injured, although Reuters reported that injuries among protesters may have been undercounted.

Students have cited inadequate university facilities, the cost of higher education and limited job prospects as central grievances. Some demonstrators have also expressed anger over inequality and alleged government corruption.

The unrest follows months of protests over a proposed luxury resort development on Albania’s southern coast. Reuters reported that the project had been backed by investors including Jared Kushner, the son-in-law of US President Donald Trump. Villagers have alleged that their land was wrongfully sold for the development. Reuters said it found no evidence of wrongdoing by Kushner, who was not directly involved in the villagers’ dispute.

Rama has said his government respects students and will protect their right to protest. He has rejected calls to resign, according to Reuters.

The protests have broadened from university grievances into a wider challenge over economic opportunity, inequality and government accountability.

Argentina’s University Funding Dispute Pits Students Against Austerity

In Argentina, students, professors, university employees and trade unions have protested against President Javier Milei’s austerity measures, arguing that cuts to public funding threaten universities and the opportunities they provide.

The dispute centres on legislation passed by Congress to increase university funding, adjust salaries for inflation from December 2023, restore scholarships and provide money for research, hospitals and infrastructure.

Milei vetoed the law, but Congress overturned his veto. His government has nevertheless refused to implement the measures, arguing that they lack a clear financing plan, according to AFP. AFP also reported that a federal judge had begun fining the government for failing to implement parts of the legislation.

Protests in Buenos Aires on Thursday drew students, university staff and opposition supporters. Police used tear gas and rubber bullets during clashes. At least 25 people were detained, and several people, including security personnel, were injured, according to Al Jazeera.

Students and professors say funding pressures are undermining Argentina’s public university system and that salaries have failed to keep pace with inflation. Professor Maria Teresa de Jesus told Al Jazeera that her monthly salary of about $700 made it difficult to cover living costs.

Milei’s government has defended spending cuts as necessary to stabilise the economy, bring inflation down and support recovery. The dispute highlights the tension between fiscal restraint and funding public institutions that many people regard as essential to social mobility.

Tunisia Protests Link Economic Hardship With Democratic Concerns

Thousands of Tunisians demonstrated against President Kais Saied on October 17 in what Reuters described as the country’s largest protest against him in years.

Led by the UGTT labour union and civil society groups, the rally brought together opposition parties, lawyers, doctors, journalists and unemployed people. Protesters accused Saied of undermining democratic institutions and mishandling the economy, Reuters reported. They also called for the release of government critics and an end to what they described as attacks on trade union rights.

Tunisia is facing an economic crisis, deteriorating public services, recurring water and electricity cuts, and shortages of medicines and other essential goods, according to Reuters.

Saied expanded his powers in 2021, dissolved parliament and began ruling by decree. His opponents described his actions as a coup, while rights groups have raised concerns about pressure on civil society, journalists and opposition figures. Saied rejects accusations that he is a dictator and has accused his opponents of contributing to social unrest.

The demonstration took place on Habib Bourguiba Avenue, a focal point of the 2011 protests that helped trigger the Arab Spring. Protesters revived slogans from that uprising and called for greater political freedoms.

The rally brought economic grievances and concerns about political rights together in a broader challenge to Saied’s leadership.

Spain’s Housing Crisis Puts Sánchez Under Pressure

Spain’s housing crisis has sparked protests over soaring rents, evictions and a shortage of affordable homes, putting Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez under pressure ahead of a snap election scheduled for November 29, 2026.

The attempted eviction of 87-year-old María del Carmen Abascal from her Madrid flat, where she had lived for 70 years, became a rallying point for housing activists. Abascal died on October 7, Reuters reported.

Protesters in Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia and Malaga have demanded stronger tenant protections and more affordable housing. Spain faces a housing deficit of around 750,000 homes, expected to exceed one million by 2028, according to Bank of Spain estimates cited by Reuters.

The affordability gap is especially stark for young people. Average monthly rent stood at €1,176, equivalent to 98.7% of the average monthly salary of a young person, Reuters reported, citing Spain’s Youth Council.

Sánchez’s government has faced setbacks in its efforts to address the crisis. Parliament rejected a proposed housing package on October 2, while two further housing decrees approved on October 6 still required parliamentary approval, Reuters reported. Trade unions UGT and CCOO have called a national general strike for November 11.

The housing crisis continues to put pressure on the government as disagreements persist over rent controls, tenant protections and expanding supply.

The protests span disputes over elections, public spending, education, political freedoms and housing. While the immediate triggers differ, they highlight the challenges governments face when citizens question the fairness of public policy, the quality of essential services and the distribution of economic costs.
